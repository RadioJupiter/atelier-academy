Methods, and why

The guiding rule: for each subject, use the method with the best record of producing real competence, wherever it comes from. Where a famous method is better suited to a classroom than to one student, it is adapted, and the adaptation is stated plainly.

The language of instruction is English throughout. Spanish and Latin are taught as subjects, not used as the language of the rest of the course.

Across all subjects

  • Explicit instruction with worked examples, then practice. For new material, fully guided instruction outperforms leaving students to discover methods unaided (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Every lesson states the idea, shows it worked, then asks for practice.
  • Retrieval practice. Each day begins by recalling earlier material from memory; testing yourself improves long-term retention more than rereading (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
  • Answer keys on separate pages, so that every exercise is attempted before the solution is seen.
  • Mastery checks. Fridays end with cumulative checks; under 80% means re-teaching before moving on.

Mathematics

  • Singapore model method (bar models) for problem modelling. Developed in 1983 by the team led by Dr Kho Tek Hong at Singapore's Curriculum Development Institute, it represents the relationships in a word problem as bars (part-whole, comparison and change models) before any equation is written (Kaur, 2019).
  • Kiselev's geometry, the Russian school text used for generations, translated by Alexander Givental. It builds geometry as a chain of proofs from axioms; geometry is where most students first meet real proof.
  • Gelfand-style algebra (Gelfand & Shen, Algebra; Gelfand, Glagoleva & Shnol, Functions and Graphs; Gelfand & Saul, Trigonometry): algebra as understanding, with identities proved, graphs built by transformation, and problems that require thought rather than routine.
  • The Russian problem tradition for practice: many short, carefully graded problems, as in Demidovich's Problems in Mathematical Analysis (for calculus) and the olympiad circles.

Writing & Rhetoric

  • The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler, 2017): explicit instruction at the sentence level first (kernels and expansion, because/but/so, appositives, subordinating conjunctions, sentence combining), then single-paragraph outlines, always practised on the content of other subjects.
  • Renaissance imitatio: copying model passages exactly, analysing them, and varying them, as in Erasmus's De Copia (1512), which gives 150 variations of a single sentence.
  • Franklin's method, from his Autobiography: reduce a model essay to hints, rebuild it days later, and compare with the original; and turn prose into verse and back.
  • The Trivium as the overall order: grammar (how sentences work), logic (how arguments work, with Toulmin's model), rhetoric (Aristotle's three appeals; the classical progymnasmata).

Atelier

The 19th-century academic sequence still taught in classical ateliers: line and proportion through copying the Bargue–Gérôme Drawing Course (197 plates, published by Goupil in Paris, 1866–1871), sight-size comparison, value scales and simple forms under one light, cast drawing, then monochrome painting and finally colour. One difficulty is added at a time. It is the largest block of the day because drawing skill depends on many hours of deliberate, corrected practice.

Physics

Peer Instruction (Eric Mazur, Harvard): pre-reading, then conceptual multiple-choice ConcepTests, answered individually, discussed, and answered again. Ten years of data at Harvard showed large gains in conceptual understanding and problem solving (Crouch & Mazur, 2001), consistent with a broad survey of interactive-engagement teaching (Hake, 1998). Adaptation for one student: the "peer" step becomes writing the strongest argument for a different answer, or explaining aloud to a tutor, before re-answering. This keeps the core mechanism (committing to an answer, then defending or revising it) but is not identical to classroom discussion.

Science

  • Phenomenon-based learning from Finland: Finland's 2014 national core curriculum requires every school to run at least one multidisciplinary learning module a year, built around a theme or phenomenon (Finnish National Agency for Education). Here, each science module begins with a real phenomenon and crosses subject lines.
  • The Russian olympiad tradition for rigour: estimation, units in every line, and non-routine problems, the tradition of the Russian physics and mathematics olympiads and the magazine Kvant (founded 1970 by Kikoin and Kolmogorov).

Modern language (Spanish)

The written lessons use explicit instruction of sounds, high-frequency words and present-tense grammar, with daily oral practice. Explanations and exercise instructions are in English; the Spanish to be learned is in Spanish. Italian or French may replace this block as a subject (see Electives), but they are not written yet.

Latin

The natural method of Hans Ørberg's Lingua Latina per se Illustrata: the book is written entirely in Latin and is understood through context, pictures and margin notes, so students read Latin as Latin from the first page. Daily short sessions suit language learning. All exercises here are original; the book's text is not reproduced.

History and literature

History is taught chronologically, from primary sources with explicit sourcing, corroboration and contextualisation. Literature is the great books, read whole, discussed in writing and used as material for the writing course.

The seminar subjects

Logic, music theory, art history and economics and personal finance each get 30 minutes once a week, so they are steady rather than intensive. Art history runs in step with History, and its looking exercises feed the Atelier.
