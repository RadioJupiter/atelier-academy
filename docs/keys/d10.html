Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium III, rhetoric: the three appeals

Check. 1. Ethos, pathos, and logos. 2. Feeling can set the audience's state, but it cannot replace a reason; without logos the appeal can slip into manipulation.

1–2. Use the Day 9 rubric adapted for a paragraph. A logos sentence might cite the Latin roots of art vocabulary (pigment from pigmentum, sculpture from sculptura, medium) and the Latin inscriptions and titles on Renaissance works; ethos might cite the training of past artists; pathos the pleasure of reading an inscription unaided. Every factual claim must be true. 3. (a) ethos (b) pathos (c) logos. 3. Model: ethos. Own words should match this idea: The character the speaker or writer seems to have: credible, careful, fair. One of the three appeals in classical rhetoric. Near-miss: Ethos is not ‘being famous’, and it is not a substitute for evidence. pathos. Own words should match this idea: The appeal to feeling: the reader’s pity, anger, or loyalty, used on purpose and still tied to a claim. Near-miss: Pathos is not ‘being emotional instead of arguing’, and it is not automatically dishonest. It becomes dishonest when it replaces evidence. logos. Own words should match this idea: The appeal to reason: the structure of claim, evidence, and warrant, and the consistency of the argument. Near-miss: Logos is not ‘using Greek terms’, and it is not a list of facts without a claim. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Physics · Newton's third law and the two-week check

Check. 1. Because they act on two different bodies. 2. Of the box's push on the table (same kind of force, two bodies). The weight is a different pair with the Earth's pull. 3. Model: Newton's third law. Own words should match this idea: If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size, opposite in direction, and of the same type. The two forces act on different bodies. Near-miss: The two forces do not cancel, because they do not act on the same body. The third law is not ‘every action has an equal reaction’ if that is taken to mean the forces are on one object and so nothing can accelerate. action–reaction pair. Own words should match this idea: The two forces named by the third law: they are equal, opposite, simultaneous, of the same type, and they act on two different bodies. Near-miss: The weight of the book and the normal force on the book are not an action–reaction pair: both act on the book. (They may cancel, but that is the first and second laws on one body.) The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

History · Synthesis: law, citizenship and judgment

Check. 1. It must make a claim organized by idea (not by document), support it with sourced evidence from more than a tour of one text, and acknowledge limits of the evidence. 2. Model: synthesis. Own words should match this idea: A reasoned putting-together of several sources around an idea, not a tour of one document after another. Near-miss: Synthesis is not a summary of each source in turn, and it is not a list of quotations without a thesis. judgment. Own words should match this idea: The act of deciding a case, or the standard by which people are judged. In this course the word also means your own reasoned decision about a source. Near-miss: Judgment is not ‘having an opinion’. In a document-based essay it must be argued from sourced evidence. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Rubric (1–4 each): Thesis answers the question and can be argued · Uses all four documents accurately · Sourcing sentence for each · Organized by idea, with comparison inside paragraphs · Acknowledges limits of the evidence (e.g., Thucydides' reconstruction of speeches; Plato's loyalty to Socrates; the stele as royal self-presentation). A strong thesis might be: These sources move from law as a king's decree applied by rank, to law as the shared possession of citizens, to the claim that even the citizens' judgment must answer to reason.

Elective · Poetry: meter and the iambic pentameter

Check. 1. A spondee is two stressed syllables (DUM-DUM). 'Rough winds' breaks the regular iambic beat and makes the winds feel forceful. 2. Model: meter (verse). Own words should match this idea: The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of verse. (US spelling: meter.) Near-miss: Meter in this lesson is not the SI unit of length, which is a different English word of the same spelling, also from μέτρον. iambic pentameter. Own words should match this idea: A line of five iambs (five da-DUM feet), the most common meter of English verse from Chaucer onward, and the line of Shakespeare’s sonnets and blank verse. Near-miss: It is not ‘any ten-syllable line’, and a spondee (DUM-DUM) such as ‘Rough winds’ is a variation, not a mistake. foot (verse). Own words should match this idea: The repeating unit of meter: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), spondee (DUM-DUM). Near-miss: A foot is not a syllable, and it is not a line. A pentameter line has five feet. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Line 1: shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer's DAY (the first foot is often read with the stress on I or on Shall). Line 2: thou ART | more LOVE | ly AND | more TEM | per-ATE (the last foot uses a light final stress on -ate). Line 3: ROUGH WINDS (a spondee) | do SHAKE | the DAR | ling BUDS | of MAY. Line 4: and SUM | mer's LEASE | hath ALL | too SHORT | a DATE.
  2. The opening question and the heavy "Rough winds" break the regular beat; the spondee makes the winds feel forceful. Natural speech stresses are allowed to pull against the meter.
  3. Check that each line has ten syllables in a mostly da-DUM pattern, and the ABAB rhyme.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. A primary source was produced at the time by someone involved. A 19th-century painting of Rome is secondary for Rome but primary for 19th-century taste: the class follows the question. 2. Displacement is final position minus initial position, with a direction. It is not equal to distance traveled when the path doubles back; returning to the start gives displacement 0 and a positive distance. 3. An exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles. The adjacent interior angle is its supplement (they add to 180°), not its equal, and not the same as that sum. 4. Fnet = m a. A net force of zero means the forces present cancel, not that there are none; the first law is the special case a = 0. 5. Compound interest pays on the principal and on interest already earned, A = P(1 + r)n if compounded yearly. Simple interest pays only on the original principal. 6. Aidōs is shame before others, a sense of honor tied to the community's gaze: Hector will not shirk battle even though he foresees Troy's fall. Achilles, so far, fights for personal honor (timē) and has withdrawn from the common cause.
