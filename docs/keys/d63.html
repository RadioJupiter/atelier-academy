Latin · Capitulum XI, part 3: the accusative and infinitive

Check. 1. avem is the accusative subject of the infinitive; volāre is the infinitive, the second action (flying). 2. Magister puerum dormīre putat. (Or: Putat puerum dormīre.) 3. It uses two finite verbs. Today’s construction keeps the second verb as an infinitive: Puella avem volāre videt. 4. After possum / potest, there is no second person in the accusative; the same subject is able to do the infinitive. In AcI, the accusative is the subject of the infinitive, after a verb of seeing, saying, or thinking. 5. Model: accusative and infinitive (AcI). Own words should match this idea: A construction after verbs such as vidēre, dīcere, and putāre: an accusative names the person or thing who does the action of an infinitive. English often turns it with “that”: Puella avem volāre videt, “The girl sees that the bird is flying,” or “The girl sees the bird fly.” Near-miss: Not posse + infinitive (already taught), where possum / potest itself is the finite verb and there is no second person in the accusative. Not two finite verbs in a row (*Puella videt avem volat). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Dīcō puellam cantāre. (Or: Puellam cantāre dīcō.)
  2. Puer avem volāre videt.
  3. Putō puerum dormīre. The subject of dormīre must be accusative, and dormīre is an infinitive, not dormit.

Mathematics · Geometry XXVII: a missing side, from a scale factor

Check. 1. 6/9 = 10/SU, so SU = 15. 2. 4 and 6 are two sides of the smaller triangle, not a corresponding pair. 10 is not the partner of 4 in that writing. The right proportion is 4/10 = 6/x (or 4/10 = 6/x written with the other pair). 3. EF = 3 × 5 = 15. 4. AB is the partner of DE, and the factor from ABC to DEF is 2/3, so 8 = (2/3) × AB, and AB = 12. (Or: from DEF to ABC the factor is 3/2, and 8 × 3/2 = 12.) 5. Model: scale factor (similarity). Own words should match this idea: The constant ratio of corresponding sides of two similar triangles. If △ABC ∼ △DEF and the scale factor from ABC to DEF is k, then each side of DEF is k times the corresponding side of ABC, so a missing side can be found from one known pair. Near-miss: Not a ratio of sides that do not correspond, and not a claim that any two sides from the two triangles may be placed in a fraction. The vertices in the similarity statement tell you which sides match. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 9/6 = 12/YZ, so YZ = 8.
  2. 5/15 = 8/x, so x = 24.
  3. The proportion uses corresponding sides. If you pair the wrong sides, the scale factor is wrong and the missing length is wrong.

Writing & Rhetoric · A warrant is not a restated claim

Check. 1. (A) is a warrant: it is a general rule joining the grounds (shines and is slippery) to the claim (the path is icy). (B) is a restated claim. 2. A warrant is a rule that might or might not hold, so a reader can accept or reject it. A restated claim just repeats the claim; disagreeing with it is only disagreeing with the claim again, not with a joining rule. 3. A model: a civil war of this kind tests whether a nation so conceived and so dedicated can long endure. 4. A general rule that joins “we are engaged in a great civil war” to the claim about a test. Copying the sentence gives the claim (and the grounds) again, not a warrant.

  1. A model: claim, a fire is lit; grounds, there is a glow on the hearth and the room is warmer; warrant, a lit fireplace in this room glows and warms the air; restated claim, labeled, “so a fire is lit.” Other accurate invented examples are accepted.
  2. A repair must be a rule, not praise: for example, “a civil war of this kind tests whether such a nation can endure.” “Lincoln is right” is not a warrant for the set sentence.
  3. A warrant is a general rule that makes the grounds count as support; a restated claim only says the claim again, often after “therefore” or “so.”

Physics · The lever, as a case of rotational equilibrium

Check. 1. The support about which the lever turns; the point about which torques are taken in this lesson. A force through the fulcrum gives zero torque about it, because d⊥ = 0. 2. The left arm is 0.50 m; the right arm is 2.50 m. 30 N × 0.50 m = F × 2.50 m, so F = 6 N downward. 3. 30 N + 6 N = 36 N downward, so the fulcrum supplies 36 N upward. 4. It is Στ = 0 written for two downward forces about one fulcrum. The condition is the one already taught; the lever is a special case. 5. Model: fulcrum. Own words should match this idea: The support about which a lever turns. In this lesson the fulcrum is the point about which torques are taken. A force through the fulcrum gives no torque about it. Near-miss: Not the load and not the effort. The fulcrum is the turning point, not a second downward force. Moving the fulcrum changes the two distances, and so changes the forces needed for Στ = 0. the lever rule. Own words should match this idea: For a light lever in rotational equilibrium about its fulcrum, the effort times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum equals the load times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum (opposite senses). It is Στ = 0 written for two downward forces and one support. Near-miss: Not a new unit, and not an excuse to ignore ΣF = 0 when the lever is at rest. Not a measurement from a named official machine; the numbers in this lesson are invented and labeled as such. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 24 N × 0.50 m = E × 1.50 m, so E = 8 N downward. Support: 24 N + 8 N = 32 N upward.
  2. No. If both torques have the same sense, they add rather than cancel. Στ = 0 needs opposite senses (or some other opposing torque not given in that picture).
  3. The support force acts at the fulcrum, so its torque about the fulcrum is zero. It is used for ΣF = 0, not as a third arm in the lever rule.

History · 476 CE: a conventional marker, and the East continues

Check. 1. Romulus Augustulus, the western emperor in Italy, was deposed by Odoacer. Later writers often use 476 as a conventional marker for the end of the western imperial line in Italy. 2. The eastern empire continued, with its capital at Constantinople. 476 marks the western line in Italy, conventionally, not the disappearance of the whole empire, of Roman law, or of the city of Rome. 3. 410 came with a set Augustinian extract and an edition footnote. 476 is taught as a conventional later marker, without a set primary-source quotation in this course. 4. It continued. A careful sentence names the western line in Italy and then says that the East remained. 5. Model: 476 as a conventional year. Own words should match this idea: The year later historians often use as a marker for the end of the western imperial line in Italy, when Romulus Augustulus was deposed by Odoacer. It is a conventional marker, not a day on which “the empire vanished,” and the eastern empire, with its capital at Constantinople, continued. Near-miss: Not a primary-source extract this course has set, and not a claim that 410 and 476 are the same event. Not a death toll, and not a statement that Roman life, law, or the city of Rome itself disappeared that year. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: 410 is the sack of the city by the Goths under Alaric, sourced from Augustine; 476 is a conventional later marker for the deposition of Romulus Augustulus by Odoacer in Italy, after which the East continued. They are not the same event.
  2. This lesson has not sourced a death toll for 476. The number would be invented.
  3. Late antiquity is a period name for a long transition, so 410 and 476 can both sit inside it without either date having to be “the day Rome ceased to exist.”

Atelier · Planes of the head, and one large terminator, resumed

Check. 1. A relatively flat surface on the head that catches or turns from the light — forehead, front plane, side plane, jaw, underside of the chin — so the light can be read before small forms are carved in. 2. The boundary where the large form turns out of the light into shadow. One large event first keeps the head as a single solid, rather than a collection of little cut-out features. 3. Those sittings practised a large terminator on simple solids. The head uses the same idea, now on a more complicated form. 4. Correct the drawing to the plaster. The plate or plaster wins.

  1. A model: forehead, front, side, jaw, underside of the chin — or the actual order used, if it is still large-to-small. Honest lists are accepted.
  2. The large turn of the form, and so the sense that the features sit on one head in one light.
  3. If the light moves, the planes and the terminator move, and Monday’s set-up is no longer the same sitting.

Art history · The Arch of Constantine, from Platner

Check. 1. Constantine’s victory over Maxentius in 312 CE; completion in 315–316 CE, from the decennalia inscriptions of the side arches. 2. It is not mentioned by any of the literary sources he uses; the sculptures belong to several different periods. 3. A model: Dacian statues or a Trajanic frieze from the Forum of Trajan (or the Marcus Aurelius reliefs, or the Hadrianic medallions) as earlier work; the low frieze of Constantine’s exploits as Constantinian. Other groupings actually named in the entry are accepted. 4. The Arch of Titus is a Flavian monument already taught from Platner. This arch is later, Constantinian in purpose, with mixed-period sculpture. Both lessons use a named Platner entry, later fabrics rather than photographs, and only what the entry actually states. 5. Model: Arch of Constantine. Own words should match this idea: A three-bay triumphal arch in Rome, which Platner records as erected by the senate in honour of Constantine to commemorate his victory over Maxentius in 312 CE, with completion dated to 315–316 CE by the decennalia inscriptions of the side arches. Platner notes that it is not mentioned by any of the literary sources he uses, and that its sculptures belong to several different periods. Near-miss: Not the already-taught Arch of Titus, and not a claim that every sculpture on the arch was carved for Constantine in 315–316. Platner is explicit that some sculpture was taken from earlier monuments, and that some work is Constantinian. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Because Platner says the sculptures belong to several periods, and then groups earlier work (Trajanic, Hadrianic, and Antonine, as he discusses them) apart from Constantinian work. A single date would erase that distinction.
  2. As Platner’s measurements, cited from the entry, not as figures you measured yourself or as a modern survey unless a later sourced measurement is set.
  3. Platner’s 1929 entry “Arcus Constantini,” as posted at LacusCurtius.
