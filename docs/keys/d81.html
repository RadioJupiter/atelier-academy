Latin · Capitulum XV: the present in the first and second persons

Check. 1. Second person plural: the ending -tis names “you” as more than one person. 2. Recitāmus. Taceō. 3. Recitās already names “you” in the ending. In the contrast sentence, ego and tū are there to set the two people against each other, not because the verbs lacked a subject. 4. Model: present first person. Own words should match this idea: The first-person present endings are -ō (I) in the singular and -mus (we) in the plural. The verb’s ending is enough to name the person; a pronoun is not required. Near-miss: Not a first look at the third-person endings -t and -nt, which this course has used from the start. Not a past tense. present second person. Own words should match this idea: The second-person present endings are -s (you, singular) and -tis (you, plural). Again the ending names the person. Near-miss: Not a first look at tū or at mē and tē, which were already taught. The new work is the verb ending itself. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Recitō, recitās, recitāmus, recitātis.
  2. We hear. You (singular) hear.
  3. Ego audiō, tū recitās. (Pronouns are appropriate here because of the contrast.)
  4. Already used: -t and -nt (third person). First looks today: -ō, -s, -mus, and -tis.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXVIII: the circumference of a circle

Check. 1. C = 2π × 7 = 14π. 2. C = 2πr = 16π, so r = 8. 3. 22/7 is an approximation (and historically an upper bound in Archimedes’ work). π is not exactly 22/7, and it is not exactly 3.14. 4. Model: circumference of a circle. Own words should match this idea: The circumference of a circle is the length of the circle itself, the distance once around. For a circle of diameter d, or radius r, that length is C = πd = 2πr, where π is the same constant already used in this course. Near-miss: Not the area inside the circle, which is reserved for tomorrow. Not a chord, and not an arc taken as if it were the whole circumference. π, as C divided by d. Own words should match this idea: π is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter: C / d = π, the same for every circle. In this course, leave exact answers in terms of π unless a problem asks for a labeled school approximation such as 3.14. Near-miss: Not a claim that π is exactly 22/7 or exactly 3.14. Those are approximations (22/7 is a famous upper bound from Archimedes). Not a first look at π as a symbol in the already-taught centripetal formula. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. C = 12π.
  2. C = 6π. A decimal estimate is 6 × 3.14 = 18.84, using π ≈ 3.14, labeled as an approximation.
  3. The second circumference is twice the first, because C is proportional to d, and 8 is twice 4.
  4. C = 2π × 20 = 40π. Invented path, not a measured track.

Writing & Rhetoric · A second body paragraph, from the outline

Check. 1. It carries the next heading of the existing outline, with its own evidence and a tie back to the thesis. It does not copy the first body paragraph, and it does not write the essay’s opening or close. 2. Quotation is exact wording. This course quotes only set, already-printed texts, so that the wording can be checked and is not guessed from memory. 3. The other-side heading is a later job, taught tomorrow. Today is only the next body stage of the case itself. 4. Model: second body paragraph. Own words should match this idea: A second body paragraph carries the next main reason or next stage of the already-made outline, in the same order, with its own evidence and a sentence that ties that evidence back to the thesis. It is not a new essay, and it is not a second copy of the first paragraph. Near-miss: Not a first look at the analytical essay (Day 9) or at the multi-paragraph outline (Day 78), and not a first look at writing one paragraph from an outline (Day 79). Not a new opening or a close; those come later this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete paragraph that follows the next heading, with evidence and a tie to the thesis. (The wording will vary with the student’s outline.)
  2. The margin names the heading and the tying sentence. If either is missing, that is the fault to repair.
  3. A later job honestly named: the other side, the opening, or the close, not a leftover reason that belonged in this paragraph.
  4. An exact phrase and a named course source (for example Longfellow 1867, as printed in a set literature lesson). Invented wording must not be presented as a quotation.

Physics · Hooke’s law, and the force constant

Check. 1. k = 25 / 0.10 = 250 N/m. Invented figures. 2. |F| = kx = 200 × 0.03 = 6 N, toward equilibrium. 3. The force is opposite to the displacement. When you compute k = |F| / |x|, you use sizes, so the minus sign does not make k negative. 4. Model: Hooke’s law. Own words should match this idea: Hooke’s law says that, for a system that obeys it, the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and is opposite in direction: F = −kx. The minus sign marks that opposition. Near-miss: Not every restoring force. Hooke’s law is the special case of a direct proportion. A bent ruler may follow it only for a small displacement, as OpenStax notes in a related picture. Not a first look at the word oscillation, already taught last week. force constant. Own words should match this idea: The force constant k (also called the spring constant) measures how stiff the system is. A larger k means a larger restoring force for the same displacement. Its units are newtons per meter (N/m). From F = −kx, the size of k is |F| / |x|. Near-miss: Not a force, and not a period. k is a property of the spring or system, not of a particular stretch, as long as Hooke’s law still holds. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. k = 5 / 0.02 = 250 N/m. Invented figures. Compression still has a restoring force toward equilibrium.
  2. A larger k means a larger restoring force for the same displacement, so the system is harder to deform.
  3. m = 80.0 kg, settlement 1.20 cm, k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m, from OpenStax 16.1, Example 16.1.
  4. Hooke’s law, in the form taught today, is not holding over that range: the restoring force is not proportional to the displacement.

History · The west entrances, the four piers, and the four arches (Dewing I.1.36–41)

Check. 1. A wall containing the entrances, and on either side a semicircle of columns and the structure above them, “very similar” to what has just been described. 2. In the center, two on the north and two on the south, opposite and equal. One might suppose they were “sheer mountain-peaks.” 3. A circular structure, cylindrical in shape; “it is through this that the light of day always first smiles.” 4. Model: four piers (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing I.1.37–38, four man-made eminences (lophoi), “which they call piers (pessoi),” stand in the center of the church, two on the north and two on the south, “opposite and equal to each other, each pair having between them just four columns.” They are “composed of huge stones joined together,” and “one might suppose that they were sheer mountain-peaks.” Near-miss: Not a first look at the east half-cylinder or the seeming-float of the crescent, already read in I.1.31–35. Not a claim that Dewing’s “just four columns” has been surveyed in modern units. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A quotation that keeps Dewing’s wording (for example “four man-made eminences… which they call piers”). A précis in the student’s words and in American spelling, without a new measurement.
  2. The east and west arches rise over empty air; the other two (north and south, by the earlier pairing) have structural elements and rather small columns under them.
  3. Dewing does not give that measurement here, and this course does not invent one.
  4. The four triangles and the huge spherical dome of I.1.43–46 (and the later comments on the eye). They are Wednesday’s reading.

Atelier · Grisaille: a thin lay-in of the families

Check. 1. A thin monochrome first pass that states the light and shadow families. It is not a finish of edges, features, or halftones, and it is not full color. 2. It remains a turning of the form, not an outline drawn around the head. 3. Make the same large-family pass in graphite or charcoal, and call it a rehearsal of a grisaille lay-in. 4. Model: grisaille lay-in. Own words should match this idea: A grisaille lay-in is a thin first pass in monochrome that states the large light family and the large shadow family on the head, without finishing edges or features. The terminator is kept as a turning, not as an outline drawn around the form. Near-miss: Not a first look at grisaille, at the light and shadow families, at the terminator, or at the value map, all taught last week. Not a finished head, and not a first look at full color, which remains reserved for Term 3. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A thin, large-family lay-in exists on the page or board, or an honest note of what was possible today.
  2. A specific note about the terminator, not a vague “good” or “bad.”
  3. A feature honestly left, because today was the families, not the finish.
  4. The word rehearsal is present if paint was not used.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of poder

Check. 1. Pude, pudiste, pudo, pudimos, pudisteis, pudieron. 2. Yesterday I managed to finish the work. 3. Tuve is the preterite of tener and uses tuv-. Pude is the preterite of poder and uses pud-. Both are irregular, but they are not the same stem. 4. Model: pude. Own words should match this idea: Pude is the first-person singular preterite of poder. The stem is pud-: pude, pudiste, pudo, pudimos, pudisteis, pudieron. In the preterite, the usual school sense is a completed ability: I managed, or I succeeded in doing something, on that occasion. Near-miss: Not a first look at the present puedo, or at tuve, estuve, anduve, fui, or hice, already taught. Not the imperfect podía, which this course has not made today’s job and must not be guessed as a synonym of pude. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete invented sentence, such as Él pudo abrir la puerta. — He managed to open the door.
  2. Pudimos.
  3. Puedo is present: I can, in general or now. Pude is preterite: I managed, on a completed occasion.
  4. Pudiste; pudieron.

Elective · Logic: Camestres, a second-figure AEE syllogism

Check. 1. All P are M. No S are M. Therefore No S are P. 2. Yes. P = metals, M = elements, S = plants. All P are M; No S are M; No S are P. 3. Cesare is EAE: No P are M; All S are M; No S are P. Camestres is AEE: All P are M; No S are M; No S are P. Both are second figure. 4. Model: Camestres. Own words should match this idea: Camestres is a second-figure syllogism of the form AEE: a universal affirmative premise, a universal negative premise, and a universal negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: All P are M; No S are M; therefore No S are P. Near-miss: Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2), taught last week. Cesare begins with a universal negative about P; Camestres begins with a universal affirmative about P. Not a first-figure mood, and not Celarent. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A valid AEE-2 example with P, S, and M labeled, and a universal negative conclusion about S.
  2. The premises are in the wrong mood and order for Camestres, and the conclusion is not No S are P in the taught pattern. (It is also not last week’s Cesare in the taught order.)
  3. The conclusion has swapped S and P. Camestres concludes No S are P, not No P are S (even if those two E statements are related, the taught conclusion has S as subject).
  4. The middle term is the predicate of both premises, which is the second figure already taught.
