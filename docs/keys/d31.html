Latin · Capitulum IX, part 1: a third pattern of endings

Check. 1. Rēx in hortō est. 2. Vōx rēgis nōn parva est (or nōn magna, if you honestly change the meaning). 3. Model: third declension. Own words should match this idea: A large family of Latin nouns whose nominative singular does not follow the first-declension -a or second-declension -us / -um pattern, and whose genitive singular typically ends in -is (rēx, rēgis; vōx, vōcis). Near-miss: Not a new tense, and not a claim that every new noun is third declension. puella remains first declension; hortus remains second. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. rēgis, vōcis; -is marks the genitive singular of this family.
  2. Rēx is masculine; vōx rēgis, not *vōx rēx.
  3. Clear reading.
  4. The grammar is the third-declension pattern; the practice sentences are original.
  5. A parsed original sentence.

Mathematics · Algebra VIII: functions and function notation

Check. 1. f(3) = 13; f(0) = −2. 2. h(10) = 0; h(1) = 9. 3. Model: function. Own words should match this idea: A rule that assigns to each allowed input exactly one output. We write y = f(x), or simply f(x), for the output when the input is x. Near-miss: Not a one-to-many rule (a single x must not be given two different outputs). And not the everyday word “function” meaning a social event, or the logic “inverse” already taught as a transformation of a conditional. f(x). Own words should match this idea: Function notation: the output of f when the input is x. To evaluate f(a), replace x by a in the rule. Near-miss: Not g times x, and not a request to solve g(x) = 0 unless that is asked. f(x) is the output, not automatically a root. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 2x + 3 = 11, so 2x = 8, x = 4. That matches f(4) = 11 from Watch me.
  2. A fair rule is f(x) = 3x + 1 (check: 3, 7, 10). Then f(4) = 13.
  3. The parentheses mark an input, not a hidden multiplication sign.
  4. Yes: g(3) and g(−3) are both 8. One input with two outputs would fail; two inputs with one output may succeed.
  5. A clear rule, two evaluations, and the one-output condition named.

Writing & Rhetoric · The topic sentence in a three-paragraph essay

Check. 1. A fair trio: a function assigns one output to each allowed input; a one-to-many rule is the near-miss; therefore f(x) is evaluated by substituting one x and getting one value. 2. Two sentences that stay on that promise, not a new subject. 3. Model: topic sentence. Own words should match this idea: The sentence that states the job of a paragraph — what the paragraph will make good, not a new subject sprung in the last line. Near-miss: Not a title, and not a plot summary that never arrives at a claim. A topic sentence is a promise the rest of the paragraph must keep. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A marked topic sentence.
  2. Split the jobs, or choose one (functions, or circles), and close on that job.
  3. A title names a field; a topic sentence makes a promise the paragraph keeps.
  4. A sentence that names the objection to be granted.
  5. An original three-sentence plan.

Physics · Power: work over time

Check. 1. 30 W. 2. W = P × t = 40 W × 3 s = 120 J. 3. Model: power (mechanics). Own words should match this idea: The rate at which work is done: power is work divided by the time in which that work is done. In symbols, P = W / t, when W is work and t is the time taken. Near-miss: Not the everyday sense of “a powerful person,” and not the same word as the exponent in algebra (x²). In this lesson, power is a mechanical rate. watt. Own words should match this idea: The SI unit of power: one watt is one joule per second. The unit is named after James Watt. Near-miss: Not a unit of energy (that is the joule), and not a unit of force (that is the newton). A watt is a rate. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A watt is joules per second, a rate; the energy (the work) is in joules.
  2. Work = 10 N × 2 m = 20 J; P = 20 J / 4 s = 5 W.
  3. It is halved.
  4. A fair set: power is a rate, because it is work over time; but it is not a force, so a small force can still have a large power if the work is done quickly.
  5. 1,000 J, if the 100 W rate is steady for those 10 s.

History · From republic to principate: 31 and 27 BCE

Check. 1. From Latin prīncipātus, from prīnceps, “first (citizen).” 2. A formal public command; not a private letter, and not a later historian’s book. 3. Model: principate. Own words should match this idea: The form of rule associated with Augustus and his successors, in which one man holds lasting pre-eminence while republican offices still have names and some business. The word comes from Latin prīncipātus, from prīnceps, “first (citizen).” Near-miss: Not a modern constitution with a single founding day, and not a simple “the Republic fell on this date.” Offices continued; the reality of who decided changed. edict. Own words should match this idea: A formal public command or proclamation by a person in authority. From Latin edictum (a thing proclaimed). Near-miss: Not a private letter, and not a later historian’s narrative (Tacitus or Suetonius will be named on Wednesday as later writers). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Actium 31 BCE, then the 27 BCE settlement (name and powers).
  2. A victory changes who can impose a settlement; it is not itself the later legal and political arrangement.
  3. A fair set: 27 BCE is useful, because historians need a conventional start; but it is not a single fall day, so we should not teach “the Republic ended that morning.”
  4. Octavian, thereafter Augustus.
  5. A short paragraph that keeps that promise.

Near-miss: Not an ear copied from a diagram and pasted on, and not a left ear used unchanged on the right side without a check. If the plate disagrees with a memorized proportion, the plate wins. Modern language · Spanish: ir a + infinitive

Check. 1. Vas a leer. Va a leer. 2. For example: ¿Qué vas a pedir? Voy a pedir agua. 3. Model: ir a + infinitive. Own words should match this idea: A present-tense pattern that talks about a near future: ir (conjugated) + a + infinitive, ‘to be going to’ do something. Near-miss: Not a past tense, and not the English will-future as a Spanish tense taught today. And not *Voy estudiar (the a is required in this pattern). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Va a pedir agua. And either Vais a practicar (vosotros) or Van a practicar (ustedes), labeled.
  2. Today’s pattern is ir + a + infinitive, not ir + a + a present participle.
  3. Two heard patterns, each in a correct new sentence.
  4. A short, correct three-sentence string.
  5. ir a + infinitive talks about what is going to happen; tener que + infinitive talks about what someone has to do.

Logic · The syllogism: a first look at Barbara

Check. 1. A three-line Barbara with M, S, and P labeled, and content that is actually true or clearly assumed. 2. All S are P. 3. Model: syllogism. Own words should match this idea: A form of argument with two premises and a conclusion, in which a middle term links the other two terms. The word comes from Latin syllogismus, from Greek συλλογισμός (a putting-together of thoughts). Near-miss: Not a single if–then sentence (that was a conditional, already taught), and not any two sentences followed by a ‘therefore.’ If the middle term does not link the other two, the form has failed. Barbara (first look). Own words should match this idea: The traditional name for one valid syllogism in the first figure: All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P. All three sentences are A-form (the vowels in the name Barbara are a reminder of that). Near-miss: Not a license to rearrange the premises at will and keep the same validity without checking, and not a claim that every everyday argument is a Barbara. Other moods and figures are not taught as valid today merely by being named. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It is no longer this form: the middle term should not appear in the conclusion of Barbara.
  2. A name is not a proof; only the form taught today is granted today.
  3. For example: All rectangles have four sides; all squares are rectangles; therefore all squares have four sides.
  4. A conditional is one if–then sentence; a syllogism, here, is two premises and a conclusion linked by a middle term.
  5. The form copied, and a new example that keeps it.
