Own words should match this idea: The tendency of a body to keep its velocity — to stay at rest, or to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed — unless a net force acts on it. This is Newton’s first law. Near-miss: Inertia is not a force, and it is not ‘a force that keeps things moving’. No net force is needed to keep a constant velocity. net force. Own words should match this idea: The vector sum of all forces on a body. Newton’s first law says that if the net force is zero, the velocity does not change. Near-miss: A net force of zero does not mean ‘no forces act’. Forces can be present and cancel. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Modern language · Spanish: questions

Check. 1. Where. 2. Por qué is why; porque is because. 3. Model: question (Spanish). Own words should match this idea: A sentence that asks, marked in writing by ¿ at the start and ? at the end, and often begun with a question word such as qué, dónde, or cómo. Near-miss: Leaving off the opening ¿ is an English habit, and using ser for a place question is the Day 4 contrast forgotten. question word. Own words should match this idea: A word such as qué (what), dónde (where), or cómo (how) that opens many questions and is written with an accent in these forms. Near-miss: The accent is not optional in these question words, and dónde is not the same word as donde without the accent. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Logic · Validity, soundness and two classic fallacies

Check. 1. Denying the antecedent: invalid (the match could be cancelled for another reason). 2. Model: validity. Own words should match this idea: A property of an argument’s form: the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are true. Validity does not require the premises to be actually true. Near-miss: Valid does not mean ‘true’, and it does not mean ‘persuasive’. A valid argument can have a false conclusion if a premise is false. soundness. Own words should match this idea: The property of an argument that is valid and whose premises are actually true. Only then must the conclusion be true in the world. Near-miss: Sound is not a synonym of valid. ‘All birds can fly; penguins are birds; so penguins can fly’ is valid but unsound. fallacy. Own words should match this idea: A tempting but invalid form of argument. Two classics: affirming the consequent, and denying the antecedent. Near-miss: A fallacy is not ‘any false conclusion’, and a true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacy. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (a) Affirming the consequent: invalid (the proportions might match by chance in a drawing that is wrong elsewhere). (b) Modus tollens: valid. (c) Denying the antecedent: invalid (it could be cancelled for another reason).
  2. E.g., "All fish can fly; sharks are fish; so sharks can fly." Valid, so at least one premise must be false.
  3. Valid but unsound: the first premise is false.
