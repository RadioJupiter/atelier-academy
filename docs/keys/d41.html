Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: first and second family nouns

Check. 1. Puer nōn in hortō est. 2. Puella in hortō est. 3. Model: first-and-second-family nouns. Own words should match this idea: Nouns whose endings follow the first pattern (often ending in -a in the nominative singular, like puella) or the second (often -us or -um, like hortus). The ending still has to match the job in the sentence. Near-miss: This is not a license to invent a new chapter’s endings, and it is not Capitulum XI. Today only reviews families already used in Capitula I–X. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Puer in hortō est.
  2. Clear reading.
  3. A labeled drawing.
  4. The Latin is a review of noun families; the decimals and the year stay in the English lessons.

Mathematics · Algebra XIII: composition of functions

Check. 1. (h ∘ k)(x) = 3(x + 2) = 3x + 6; (h ∘ k)(1) = 9. 2. (k ∘ h)(x) = 3x + 2. 3. Model: composition of functions. Own words should match this idea: The function (f ∘ g) that sends x to f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f to that result. Near-miss: Composition is not multiplication of f and g, and f ∘ g is not generally the same as g ∘ f. The little circle is not a decimal point. order of composition. Own words should match this idea: Which function is applied first. In the writing (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), g is applied first. Near-miss: Not “the letters are just a product, so the order cannot matter.” For functions, the order is a different job. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (f ∘ g)(3) = 5 − 6 = −1; (g ∘ f)(3) = 2(5 − 3) = 4. Different.
  2. A decimal point marks the ones place in a number. The circle is a composition sign between two functions.
  3. (p ∘ q)(x) = (x − 1)², invented pair.
  4. (q ∘ p)(x) = x² − 1, which is not (x − 1)² in general (for example at x = 0: 1 against −1).
  5. The inverse is a function you compose with, and that job is clearer once composition itself has been taught.

Physics · A first look at circular motion, and a center-seeking force

Check. 1. Velocity along the tangent; centripetal force toward the center. 2. Acceleration can be a change of direction, even when the speed is steady. 3. Model: circular motion. Own words should match this idea: Motion along a circular path. Even if the speed (the distance along the path in each unit of time) is constant, the direction is still changing, so the velocity is changing. Near-miss: Not “constant speed means no change of any kind.” Speed can be constant while direction changes. And not an invented orbital speed for a planet or a spacecraft. centripetal. Own words should match this idea: Center-seeking: a force (or an acceleration) directed toward the center of the circle, needed if the motion is to stay on that circle. Near-miss: Not a second outward force that “balances” it in this first look, and not a license to compute a satellite’s speed. We do not invent a number of newtons for the string, and we do not yet treat a formula as today’s law. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The word is center-seeking. An outward “fling” is not the force that keeps the motion on the circle in this first look.
  2. Today taught no orbital-speed formula and no sourced orbital speed. A computed Mars speed would be invented.
  3. Without a net inward force, the motion would not keep turning toward the center; it would not remain on the circle.
  4. Invented demo; no invented speed, length, or number of newtons.
  5. Both are the touching line at a point of the circle: in geometry, the line; in this motion, the direction of the velocity.

History · After 68 CE: 69, a later year-name, and Tacitus’s opening

Check. 1. 69 CE; 68 CE. 2. “Year of the Four Emperors.” 3. Model: Year of the Four Emperors (later name). Own words should match this idea: A later name for the civil-war year 69 CE after Nero’s death in 68 CE, when four men — Galba, Otho, Vitellius, and Vespasian — are usually listed. It is not Tacitus’s own title for the year. Near-miss: Not a single “fall of Rome,” and not a claim that the year came already boxed under this modern name. 68 CE ends the Julio-Claudian list already taught; 69 CE is civil war and a new start under Vespasian, not a fall day. Tacitus as a later source. Own words should match this idea: Tacitus wrote the Histories later than the events of 69 CE. In the opening he says he had no acquaintance with Galba, Otho, or Vitellius through kindness or injury, and he cannot deny that his political career owed its beginning to Vespasian, that Titus advanced it, and that Domitian carried it further. Near-miss: Not a reason to throw the work out, and not a license to invent a different four names. The caution is about how we use the source. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The course has already refused a single fall day; 69 is a civil-war year after which Vespasian holds power, not the end of Rome.
  2. He cannot deny that his career began under Vespasian (and was advanced by Titus and Domitian). It matters because it is a limit on reading him as a neutral eyewitness.
  3. The Flavian sons, named as advancing and carrying further Tacitus’s career.
  4. They stand in the same opening we can point to; the caution is how we use the source, not a ban on the names.
  5. A note that the work is not a score.

Logic · Affirming the consequent, and denying the antecedent

Check. 1. Affirming the consequent. 2. Denying the antecedent. 3. Model: affirming the consequent. Own words should match this idea: An invalid move: from “If P, then Q” and “Q,” to conclude “P.” The consequent Q is affirmed, but that is not enough to give the antecedent P. Near-miss: Not modus ponens (from “If P, then Q” and “P,” to “Q”), which is valid. And not the undistributed middle (Day 36), which is a different syllogism error. This is a conditional error. denying the antecedent. Own words should match this idea: An invalid move: from “If P, then Q” and “not P,” to conclude “not Q.” The antecedent is denied, but Q may still hold for another reason. Near-miss: Not modus tollens (from “If P, then Q” and “not Q,” to “not P”), which is valid. The two are easy to mix because both use a “not.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It is a rectangle (modus ponens: P, therefore Q).
  2. It is not a square (modus tollens: not Q, therefore not P).
  3. The undistributed middle is a syllogism error about a middle term; today’s errors are conditional moves.
  4. A second invented example with a clear counterexample.
  5. A note that the work is not a score.
