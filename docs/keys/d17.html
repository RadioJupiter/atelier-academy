Latin · Capitulum VI, part 3: the locative

Check. 1. Rōmam, Rōmā, Rōmae. 2. Līberī in vīllā habitant. 3. Model: locative. Own words should match this idea: A special case used with the names of towns, cities, and small islands (and a few other words) to answer ubi? (where?). It often looks like a genitive in the first and second declensions: Rōmae (at Rome), Tusculī (at Tusculum). Near-miss: The locative is not the genitive of possession (even when it looks the same), and it is not in Rōmā (which would be a wrong blend of in + a town name). For a common noun, place where is still in + ablative: in vīllā. habitāre. Own words should match this idea: The Latin verb meaning ‘to live, to dwell’. It often takes a locative with a town name, or in + ablative with a common noun. Near-miss: habitāre is not ‘to have’ (habēre). The two verbs look related in English memory, but they are different Latin words with different objects and complements. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Rōmae; Tusculī.
  2. Mārcus Rōmae habitat; Iūlia Tusculī est (or habitat).
  3. For example, Iūlia Rōmae habitat.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. In the first declension the locative singular looks like the genitive singular. Tell them apart by the verb and the question: Rōmae habitat is place; vīlla Rōmae would be possession (‘Rome’s villa’), a different construction.

Mathematics · Algebra VI: radicals

Check. 1. √80 = √(16 × 5) = 4√5. Check: (4√5)² = 16 × 5 = 80. 2. √18 + √8 = 3√2 + 2√2 = 5√2. 3. Model: square root. Own words should match this idea: For a ≥ 0, the square root √a is the unique number ≥ 0 whose square is a. It undoes squaring on the non-negative numbers. Near-miss: √a is not allowed to be negative: √25 is 5, not −5, even though (−5)² = 25 too. The equation x² = 25 has two real solutions, ±5; the symbol √25 names only the non-negative one. radical. Own words should match this idea: An expression that uses a root symbol, such as √a or ³√a. A square-root radical with a whole-number radicand is often rewritten by taking out a square factor: √(k² · n) = k√n when k ≥ 0. Near-miss: You may not add radicands: √12 + √27 is not √39. You may multiply them: √a · √b = √(ab) for a, b ≥ 0. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. √8 · √18 = √144 = 12. (Or: 2√2 · 3√2 = 6 · 2 = 12.)
  2. √45 − √20 = 3√5 − 2√5 = √5.
  3. √50 = √(25 × 2) = 5√2, so they are equal.
  4. No. The left side is 4 + 3 = 7; the right side is 5.
  5. Let s = √a and t = √b, so s ≥ 0, t ≥ 0, s² = a, t² = b. Then (st)² = s² t² = ab and st ≥ 0, so st = √(ab). And √(12/27) = √(4/9) = 2/3.

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method: reconstruction from the hints

Check. 1. A complete reconstruction in the student’s words, plus two specific misses (verb, delay, noun, or connection), not a vague judgment. 2. The rewrite should show the chosen move and should not quote Johnson. 3. Model: reconstruction. Own words should match this idea: The step in Franklin’s method in which you rebuild a model passage from your hints alone, in complete sentences, and only then compare what you wrote with the original to see which choices you missed. Near-miss: Reconstruction is not reciting the original from memory, and it is not revising the original to improve it. The comparison is for noticing, not for scoring. comparison step. Own words should match this idea: The last step of a reconstruction: laying your version beside the original and naming, in writing, two or three specific choices the author made that you did not — a verb, a delay, an apposition, a transition — not a vague judgment that the original is ‘better’. Near-miss: The comparison step is not a hunt for errors in the original, and it is not a grade. It is a list of choices to try in the next imitation. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete paragraph from the hints.
  2. Two or three specific choices, named.
  3. Any copied-clause hint is replaced by a cue.
  4. The reading comes after the comparison.
  5. An honest, specific contrast (length of the period, delay of the verb, diction) is enough.

Science · Pigment and binder

Check. 1. The pigment gives color (and often opacity) as solid particles; the binder forms the film and holds the particles to each other and to the support. 2. The solvent is meant to leave (evaporate); the drying-oil binder is meant to stay and harden, holding the pigment. 3. Model: pigment. Own words should match this idea: A finely divided solid that gives a paint its color (and often its opacity) and that remains as particles in the film. It is not the liquid that hardens around it. Near-miss: A pigment is not a dye dissolved as a true solution (though some colorants are dyes), and it is not the binder. Crushing a rock does not, by itself, make a paint until a binder is added. binder. Own words should match this idea: The substance that holds the pigment particles in a film and attaches the film to the support. In oil paint, the binder is a drying oil; in watercolor, it is usually gum, and the water is mainly a vehicle that evaporates. Near-miss: The binder is not the pigment, and it is not a solvent added only to thin the paint (that solvent, if used, is meant to leave). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. An honest dated record.
  2. Lead compounds are toxic; the lesson is not a studio license to use them.
  3. Yellow or red ochre, colored by iron oxides, is the expected example. A cautious ‘I need a source’ is acceptable if the student has not yet checked.
  4. A charcoal drawing is mostly pigment (carbon) laid on the support with little or no binder; it can smear unless a fixative (a later binder) is added. The exact answer may vary; the distinction should be clear.
  5. A complete sentence: the binders, and therefore the way the film forms, are different.

Literature · Iliad, Books 17–22: the shield, the return, the death of Hector

Check. 1. It returns the gifts and the public honor, and it lets Achilles back into the field; it does not restore him to an ordinary life, and it does not make him eat or put away the grief and rage over Patroclus. 2. Priam and Hecuba watch from the wall; Andromache is still inside, at her work, and has to hear later. The delay is part of the pathos the poem has been preparing since Book 6. 3. Model: ekphrasis. Own words should match this idea: A set-piece description of a work of art inside a poem. The great example here is the shield of Achilles in Book 18, made by Hephaestus, which holds not only war but also a city at peace, a lawsuit, harvest, and dance. Near-miss: An ekphrasis is not a stage direction, and it is not ‘Homer getting distracted’. The shield is a made object in the story and a picture of a wider world than this war. the death of Hector. Own words should match this idea: The climax of Book 22: Achilles, now back in the war, chases Hector around the walls of Troy and kills him, then mistreats the body. The poem gives you Hector’s flight, his decision to stand, Andromache still unaware, and the parents on the wall. Near-miss: This is not ‘the end of the Iliad’. Two books remain, and they will not be a victory lap. It is also not a simple moral (‘Hector cowardly, Achilles just’). The poem is more difficult than that. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A page with real scenes from Book 18 and a claim about the ekphrasis, not a plot summary of 17–22.
  2. A close reading of a short stretch, accurate to the text read.
  3. The body is mistreated; Priam and the Trojans still have to recover it; the poem has not yet given a funeral or a return of the body. That is a problem for Books 23–24, not a conclusion.
  4. The paragraph should use Book 6 (Andromache, the child, the helmet) as a memory inside Book 22, not as a separate book report.
  5. A real although-sentence: although a god deceives or helps, the poem still gives Hector a choice to stand, and still gives Achilles a choice in how he treats the body.

Atelier · A plaster feature: from plane to a more particular form

Check. 1. The plaster is a form in the round under your light; the plate is a printed, already-translated set of values. The method is the same: large shapes, measurement, then families. 2. Sharp: a cartilage ridge, a fold of an ear’s helix, a hard plane break under the nose. Soft: the turn of a wing into a cheek, or a bowl of the ear as it disappears. The examples should be possible on the feature named. 3. Model: transition of planes. Own words should match this idea: The way one plane of a form gives way to the next: as a sharp fold, as a soft turn, or as a series of small planes that together read as a curve. A plaster nose has both: a sharper change at a cartilage ridge, a softer turn into the cheek (if the cast includes it), and small planes around a nostril. Near-miss: A transition is not a smear that hides the large planes, and it is not a cut as sharp as a cube if the plaster is soft there. You look, then you decide. plaster feature. Own words should match this idea: A small white or off-white cast of a nose, ear, mouth, or eye, used so that color does not distract and the light can be read as value. It is a particular form, still built from large planes first. Near-miss: This is not a portrait of a person, and it is not a full head (unless you already have a whole plaster head and choose a feature-sized passage). Stay with one feature, large on the page. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A large, constructed study of a real plaster feature, planes first, with a first modeling. Not a symbol and not a tiny sketch.
  2. Planes and two labeled transitions, honestly observed.
  3. Four sentences that include a real light-to-shadow relation and a remaining error.
  4. A plane-only study that already reads as the feature’s large form.
  5. A fair comparison, or a note that only one of the two was available, without inventing an agreement.

Modern language · Spanish: the personal a

Check. 1. Visitamos a nuestra profesora. Visitamos el museo. 2. Sí, lo busco. (Pedro → lo.) 3. Model: personal a. Own words should match this idea: The preposition a placed before a direct object that is a specific person (or a pet, treated as a person). It does not translate as a separate English word. Near-miss: The personal a is not the a of ir a + infinitive, and it is not needed before a thing. It is also not used with tener for ordinary possession: Tengo un hermano, not Tengo a un hermano in that sense. a specific person as object. Own words should match this idea: The personal a appears when the object is a particular person you have in mind, including names, family words used of real people, and pronouns for people. It is not used when the object is an indefinite, unspecific ‘anyone’ in a few set patterns, and it is not used for things. Near-miss: Do not put a before la casa, el libro, or la lección. Do not drop a before María. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Veo la casa (no a). / ¿Conoces a Ana? (name takes a).
  2. A real pair, e.g. Busco a mi amigo / Busco el cuaderno.
  3. Tengo una hermana, y ayudo a mi hermana. (tener without a; ayudar with a.)
  4. a (near future); a (motion, voy a la profesora); a (personal a: Veo a la profesora). The middle sentence is motion, not ‘I see’.
  5. Four grammatical sentences that actually make the four distinctions.

Music theory · The triad

Check. 1. C–E–G major; E–G–B minor; F–A–C major. 2. C–D–G has a second, not a third, in the middle. For C major the middle note is E. 3. Model: triad. Own words should match this idea: A three-note chord built from a root, a third above the root, and a fifth above the root. In this lesson the fifth is perfect, and the third is either major or minor. Near-miss: A triad is not ‘any three notes’, and it is not a scale. Three neighboring scale steps (C–D–E) are not a triad in this sense. major and minor triad. Own words should match this idea: A major triad has a major third and a perfect fifth above the root (the upper interval, from the third to the fifth, is then a minor third). A minor triad has a minor third and a perfect fifth (the upper interval is then a major third). Near-miss: ‘Major’ and ‘minor’ here name the triad, not the key of a whole piece, and not a mood. A minor triad can appear in a major key, and a major triad in a minor key. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. G–B–D is major. B–D–F has a fifth, B–F, that is one half step smaller than perfect (a diminished fifth). The white-key triad on B is not a minor triad with a perfect fifth; today’s passing answer names that fact rather than forcing it into major or minor.
  2. F–A–C major; E–G–B minor; D–F♯–A major.
  3. It is still a C-major triad (first inversion, if the student has the word; ‘C major with E at the bottom’ is enough).
  4. Major: C–E–G, F–A–C, G–B–D. Minor: D–F–A, E–G–B, A–C–E.
  5. A major third above the root starts a major triad; a minor third starts a minor triad, once the fifth is filled in as perfect.
