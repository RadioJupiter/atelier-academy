Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: quis, quid, and ubi

Check. 1. Hortus magnus est. (or Hortus est magnus.) 2. The question asks where the boy is, so Puer in hortō est, not the toward phrase ad hortum. 3. Model: question words (quis, quid, ubi). Own words should match this idea: Three question words already used in Capitula I–X: quis (who), quid (what), and ubi (where). They ask; they do not invent a new verb tense. Near-miss: Not a later chapter’s question system, and not a hidden subjunctive. Today only reviews these three words with present-tense original sentences. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Quid in hortō est?
  2. Quis in hortō est?

Mathematics · Algebra XVI: a line and a parabola, as a system

Check. 1. x² = −x + 2 → x² + x − 2 = 0 → (x + 2)(x − 1) = 0, so x = −2 or x = 1. Points (−2, 4) and (1, 1). Checks: (−2)² = 4 and −(−2) + 2 = 4; 1² = 1 and −1 + 2 = 1. 2. x² = x − 3 → x² − x + 3 = 0. The discriminant is 1 − 12 = −11, less than 0, so no real x, and no meeting. 3. Model: a line and a parabola, as a system. Own words should match this idea: Two equations together, one the already-taught parabola y = ax² + bx + c (often y = x² in a first look) and one a line, solved by substitution. The resulting quadratic may give two meetings, one meeting, or none. Near-miss: Not a line-and-circle system (that was Day 44), and not a claim that every line meets a parabola twice. The algebra decides. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Both use substitution, then a quadratic. Day 44 used a circle; today uses a parabola.
  2. Two meetings: (2, 4) and (−2, 4).

History · The Arch of Titus: a later commemoration, not a photograph of 70 CE

Check. 1. Josephus is a later written narrative by a Flavian client. The arch is a later stone commemoration, finished after Titus's death, with a nineteenth-century rebuild. Neither is a camera of 70 CE. 2. Platner and Ashby describe a figure of Titus in a quadriga; they do not give us a sourced likeness to invent in a drawing, and week 9 already refused an invented Flavian portrait. 3. Model: the Arch of Titus, as a later monument. Own words should match this idea: A single-bay arch on the Sacra Via in Rome, erected in honour of Titus and in commemoration of the siege of Jerusalem, but, in Platner and Ashby's account, not finished and dedicated until after Titus's death (CIL VI.945). It is a later commemoration, not a photograph of 70 CE. Near-miss: Not a building completed in 70 CE, and not the original unaltered fabric: the same entry says the arch was taken down in 1822 and rebuilt by Valadier. spoils, in a triumphal relief. Own words should match this idea: Goods carried in a triumph, as later carved. On the Arch of Titus, in Platner and Ashby's description, the south inner relief shows spoils from the temple at Jerusalem: the table of shewbread, the seven-branched candlestick, and the silver trumpets. Near-miss: Not a claim that we can date the carving to the month of the event, and not a classroom invention of other objects “surely also” on the relief. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete Together answer should match this idea: Because it was not finished and dedicated until after Titus's death, in Platner and Ashby's account (CIL VI.945). Table of shewbread, seven-branched candlestick, silver trumpets. A modern view includes Valadier's 1822 rebuild, so it is not simply the Flavian fabric.
  2. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card; the near-miss should be the one the lesson named, or a close equivalent.

Modern language · Spanish: desde and hasta

Check. 1. Camino hasta allí. 2. The start is desde aquí and the end is hasta allí: Camino desde aquí hasta allí. The corrected sentence had the two ends swapped. 3. Model: desde and hasta. Own words should match this idea: Two prepositions already useful in the present: desde marks a starting point (“from,” “since”); hasta marks an end point (“until,” “as far as”). They often work as a pair, but each can be used alone. Near-miss: Not a past tense, and not a swap (hasta aquí as if it meant “from here”). Desde is the start; hasta is the end. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete Together answer should match this idea: Camino desde aquí hasta allí. Estudio desde las dos hasta las cuatro.
  2. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card; the near-miss should be the one the lesson named, or a close equivalent.
