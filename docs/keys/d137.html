Latin · The future of ēgredior, a first look

Check. 1. Ēgrediar, ēgrediēris, ēgrediētur, ēgrediēmur, ēgrediēminī, ēgredientur. 2. He will go out (Puer ēgrediētur). We shall not go out. 3. Ēgressus eram is the pluperfect, I had gone out, already completed before another past moment, not a future. 4. Model: future of ēgredior. Own words should match this idea: The future of ēgredior is a deponent future of the third conjugation in -iō: ēgrediar, ēgrediēris, ēgrediētur, ēgrediēmur, ēgrediēminī, ēgredientur, I shall go out, you will go out, and so on. It is not the present ēgredior, and it is not ēgressus eram. Near-miss: Not ēgredior, I go out. Not ēgressus eram, I had gone out. Not sequar, I shall follow. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. You (s.) will go out; they will go out. 2. Ēgrediar; ēgredientur. 3. Ēgrediētur, he will go out; sequētur, he will follow. The verbs are different, even though both are deponent futures.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXX: the law of cosines with an obtuse included angle

Check. 1. 120° = 180° − 60°, and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ, so cos 120° = −cos 60° = −1/2. 2. 4√3, because c² = 48. 3. It is the same 4√3. The two laws agree on this invented triangle; neither is being replaced.

Exercises. 1. c² = 4 + 4 − 2·2·2·(−1/2) = 8 + 4 = 12, so c = 2√3. 2. Because cos 120° is negative, −2ab cos C is positive, and c² is larger than a² + b². 3. They are classroom numbers chosen for a clean cosine, not a measured official triangle.

Writing · A close comment on the set maxim

Check. 1. “Eat to live, and not live to eat.” A fair restatement: eat in order to live, and do not live in order to eat. 2. A fair comment stays general and names no invented occasion. 3. The source is a printed precept, not a clinic, and not a scene the 1733 page does not give.

Exercises. 1. Drop the invented dinner and the invented speech. A maxim is not a chreia. 2. A second comment might note that the line contrasts two purposes, living and eating, without adding a tale. 3. Last week retold a person, an occasion, and a saying. Today restates a general precept and comments on it.

Science · Cinnabar, a red mercury sulfide, a first look

Check. 1. A sulfide, HgS, from Manhattan, Nevada, described as blood red and red translucent. 2. Invent no official price and no official mercury percentage of a classroom stone. Cinnabar is not added to the four-paint limited palette, and it is not a studio material in this course. 3. The sample is impure and contains 0.6% iron by weight, a figure for HS133, not a general official iron percentage of all cinnabar. 4. Model: cinnabar. Own words should match this idea: Cinnabar, in USGS Spectral Library sample HS133, Clark et al. 2003, Open File Report 03-395, is a sulfide mineral whose formula is given as HgS. The collection locality is Manhattan, Nevada. The microscopic description calls the color “blood red” and “red translucent.” It is not azurite, not malachite, and not a fifth atelier tube. Near-miss: Not azurite or malachite, which are copper carbonates. Not lapis lazuli or ultramarine. Not a classroom recipe, and not a tube added to the limited palette. Not an official mercury percentage of a stone not in the record. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Malachite is a green copper carbonate, Cu2CO3(OH)2 in USGS HS254. Lapis lazuli is a rock whose blue is lazurite, already taught, not HgS. 2. The lesson is a mineral record, not a studio method, and cinnabar is mercury sulfide, not handled as a studio material in this course. 3. The description notes iron enough to be present as the oxide, while XRD found no sign of iron oxide. The lesson reports both statements and does not invent a second official composition.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 4: Jane and Elizabeth alone, a first look

Check. 1. Jane: “sensible, good humoured, lively,” with “perfect good breeding.” Elizabeth: “He is also handsome,” and “His character is thereby complete.” 2. “Compliments always take you by surprise, and me never.” “You never see a fault in any body.” 3. “Very fine ladies,” “proud and conceited,” with “a fortune of twenty thousand pounds,” the novel’s figure, not an official salary. 4. Model: the contrast of Bingley and Darcy (Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 4). Own words should match this idea: In the 1813 Pride and Prejudice, Volume I, Chapter 4, Jane and Elizabeth talk alone after the assembly, and the chapter then contrasts Bingley and Darcy. The fortunes named in the chapter are the novel’s figures, not official salaries. British and older forms stay inside quotations. Near-miss: Not an official identification of a character with a living person. Not an official salary. Not Chapter 5, which remains reserved. Not a correction of 1813 wording such as “neighbour” or “offence.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Jane: “we shall not find a very charming neighbour in her.” Elizabeth “was not convinced.” “Neighbour” stays inside the quotation. 2. They are the novel’s household words, and they stay inside quotation or clearly in the book’s household. 3. The contrast of Bingley and Darcy, and “nearly an hundred thousand pounds,” wait until Thursday. Chapter 5 remains reserved.

Modern language · Spanish: the present subjunctive of regular -er and -ir verbs

Check. 1. Viva, vivas, viva, vivamos, viváis, vivan. 2. He or she wants you (pl.) to eat. Comen is the present indicative, not the subjunctive coman. 3. The -ar subjunctive takes -e; the -er and -ir subjunctive takes -a. Swapping the vowels is a miss. 4. Model: present subjunctive of regular -er and -ir verbs. Own words should match this idea: The present subjunctive of a regular -er or -ir verb uses the endings -a, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an: coma, comas, coma, comamos, comáis, coman; viva, vivas, viva, vivamos, viváis, vivan. It is not the present indicative, and it is not yesterday’s hable pattern with -e. Near-miss: Not come or vive, the present indicative. Not the -ar subjunctive hable, which uses -e. Not an irregular subjunctive such as sea or vaya, which remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. That you (s.) eat; that we live. Fair invented sentences use “quiere que tú comas” and “quiere que vivamos.” 2. He or she eats; will eat; would eat; that he or she eat (present subjunctive). 3. Irregular subjunctives remain reserved.

Seminar · Music: theme and variations, a first look

Check. 1. A short theme is stated and then repeated with changes, each repetition a variation. A rondo is A–B–A–C–A, with contrasting episodes around a refrain, not variations of one theme. 2. Theme: 1-3-5-3 | 5-4-3-1. Variation 1: the same degrees, last note lengthened or repeated. Variation 2: 1-2-3-5 | 5-4-2-1. 3. The lesson uses an invented classroom figure. A copyrighted score is not being copied or transcribed. 4. Model: theme and variations. Own words should match this idea: Theme and variations is a form in which a short theme is stated and then repeated with changes, each repetition a variation, while the theme remains recognizable. It is not rondo form, and it is not a copyrighted score. Near-miss: Not rondo form, A–B–A–C–A, whose episodes contrast with the refrain rather than varying one theme. Not binary or ternary form as a first look already taught. Not a copyrighted score, and not a transcription of a recorded work. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. A fair further variation keeps the 1 … 5 | 5 … 1 shape, for example by repeating each degree, and remains labeled invented. 2. Binary form is a two-part A–B shape, already taught, not a theme followed by variations. 3. A longer variation set, and any later form not yet taught, remain reserved.
