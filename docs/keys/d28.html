Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 3: capiō, accipiō, faciō in the present

Check. 1. Mārcus hunc librum capit. 2. Puerī dōnum accipiunt. Mārcus labōrem facit. 3. Model: third-conjugation -iō verbs. Own words should match this idea: A small group of third-conjugation verbs whose first principal part ends in -iō (today: capiō, accipiō, faciō). In the present they show an i in several forms (capiō, capis, capit, capimus, capitis, capiunt) and -iunt in the third-person plural, not *capunt. Near-miss: Not first-conjugation -at (dat, amat), and not a regular third without the extra i (legit, legunt — no *legiunt). Learn this set as a listed set. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. capit, capiunt; accipit, accipiunt; facit, faciunt.
  2. Puerī rosās capiunt.
  3. dat is first conjugation (-at). capit is a third-conjugation -iō form (-it, and -iunt in the plural).
  4. Clear reading.
  5. This type keeps an i in the third-person plural: capiunt, not the bare -unt of a verb like legunt, and not a first-conjugation -ant.

Mathematics · Geometry XIV: mixed circle problems

Check. 1. BC = 8 (6-8-10). The inscribed angle on the same arc as the 80° central angle is 40°. 2. 4×9=6×ED, ED=6. ∠D=80° (opposite angles in a cyclic quad). 3. Model: mixed circle problems. Own words should match this idea: Problems that use more than one circle fact from this fortnight — inscribed and central angles, the angle in a semicircle, cyclic quadrilaterals, or intersecting chords — in a single figure, with the reason named at each step. Near-miss: Not a license to use every theorem at once without checking its hypothesis (a diameter, the same arc, a cyclic quad, a meeting point inside the circle). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Isosceles right triangle: ∠BAC = ∠ABC = 45°.
  2. The theorem you were taught (III.35) is for a meeting point inside the circle. An exterior meeting is a different statement (two secants), not used today.
  3. Each angle of a rectangle is 90°, so each is the angle in a semicircle on the opposite diagonal; that diagonal is a diameter.
  4. The central angle is twice, not half, the inscribed angle on the same arc: 40°, not 10°.
  5. A small original two-step problem with a correct solution, using both facts, with hypotheses checked.

Writing & Rhetoric · Evidence and attribution, in three paragraphs

Check. 1. A complete sentence with Euclid or Livy named (and dated or located), plus a short explanation of what the fact supports. 2. A raw version and a repaired version with an introduction and an explanation. 3. Model: attribution (review). Own words should match this idea: Naming a source when you quote or précis it, so a reader can tell whose words or whose claim you are using. In this course that means a short introduction and a precise pointer (author, work, book or year as you have them) — not a decorative name-drop, and not a long academic bibliography unless one is asked for. Near-miss: Not a quotation left raw, and not “science says” with no source. A theorem can be attributed too: Euclid, Elements III.20. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It names no particular account or lesson, and it invites a vague appeal. Better: “In the leftover-light account taught in this course, chlorophyll absorbs…” — still modest, still specific.
  2. A short attributed précis of one fortnight claim, in the student’s words.
  3. Usually in the support paragraph (and, more lightly, in a concession). It should not replace the opening thesis, or be the entire close.
  4. For example: “Caesar, writing as a commander about his own campaigns…” or “a later marble copy of a fifth-century bronze type…”.
  5. A yes, or an added source/theorem job on the outline for Friday.

Physics · Mechanical energy, conserved when friction does no work

Check. 1. 2gh = 2 × 9.8 × 2 = 39.2, v = √39.2 m/s (about 6.3 m/s, approximate), from rest. 2. PE = 2 × 9.8 × 1 = 19.6 J; total still 49 J; KE = 29.4 J; v² = 29.4 (because ½ × 2 × v² = 29.4). 3. Model: mechanical energy (no friction). Own words should match this idea: In this course’s first conservation model, mechanical energy is the sum KE + PE, with PE = m g h near the Earth. When no friction (and no other force that takes energy out of this account) does work, the sum is constant: ½ m v² + m g h stays the same along the path. Near-miss: Not a claim that energy is “used up” when the object stops in your hand, and not a license to ignore friction when friction is actually present. That case is Friday’s caution and is already previewed: friction’s work decreases the mechanical-energy sum as this course is keeping it. conservation (mechanical, first model). Own words should match this idea: The statement that KE + PE is constant when only gravity (in the PE = m g h model) and forces that do no work (such as a normal force perpendicular to the motion) act. It is a first model, not a complete account of every form of energy. Near-miss: Not “energy is always the same number on every line of every problem,” and not a refusal of the work–energy theorem: conservation of mechanical energy is that theorem, plus PE, when gravity’s work is already stored as ΔPE. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. KE = ½ × 3 × 16 = 24 J; PE = 3 × 9.8 × 2 = 58.8 J; total 82.8 J. At the floor PE = 0, so KE = 82.8 J, ½ × 3 × v² = 82.8, v² = 55.2.
  2. PE numbers depend on the zero; mixing two zeros in one sum would invent or lose joules on paper.
  3. The normal force is perpendicular to the displacement along the slope, so it does no work and does not change the mechanical-energy sum.
  4. Total at the top is 15 + 49 = 64 J, so KE at the floor is 64 J in this model.
  5. Friction does (usually negative) work, so KE + PE at the end is less than at the start by the amount of that work (energy leaving this account, not a denial of the work–energy theorem).

History · Late Republic sources: Caesar as a participant, and Cicero’s letters

Check. 1. That the author was a participant in the campaigns he describes, and that the text is also advocacy — a shaped public account, not a neutral log. 2. Because even contemporary voices are placed, interested, and incomplete; richness is not the same as transparency. 3. Model: Caesar as a participant source. Own words should match this idea: Julius Caesar’s Commentaries (including the Gallic War) are a primary source in the strict sense that the author was a commander writing about campaigns he led — and they are also advocacy: the writer has a stake in how he is seen in Rome. Both facts must be in the sentence, not only the first. Near-miss: Not a late antiquarian only (Caesar is closer to his own wars than Livy is to the kings), and not “therefore false.” A participant source can be close and still shaped. late Republic (sources). Own words should match this idea: The first-century BCE crisis of the Republic, for which we have unusually rich literary sources — speeches, letters, commentaries — written by participants and contemporaries, alongside later narrative. The wealth of sources does not remove the need to date and to place each voice. Near-miss: Not “the Republic was always this well documented” (early Rome was not), and not a claim that this lesson has taught the whole civil-war narrative. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Writing that argues for the author’s side, conduct, or reputation, even when it also reports events.
  2. Likeness: literary sources with aims, used for wars. Difference: Caesar writes as a commander of his own campaigns; Polybius writes a history of Rome’s rise, closer to some events than others, but not usually as the general in the anecdote.
  3. A late, second-hand (or later-tradition) tag: he is not a participant in Caesar’s campaigns, even if he used earlier sources.
  4. A caption such as: “Cicero, in a letter, writing from his own position in a named year if you have it — evidence of his view and of what he chose to write, not a census of Rome.”
  5. How we know the Republic: early law and tradition (late or fragmentary), Polybius as a second-century analysis, and late-Republican participants whose closeness still includes advocacy.

Atelier · Lost-and-found edges on the head

Check. 1. Two lost (or nearly lost) edges and one found edge, justified by this subject’s values, not by a general recipe. 2. Because the edges come from this light and this form, not from a side-of-the-page habit. 3. Model: lost-and-found on the head. Own words should match this idea: The week-4 skill of losing an edge where values meet softly or nearly match, and finding an edge where a plane cuts clearly, now applied to the head: hair against a temple, jaw into neck, a lid into a socket, the far cheek into shadow. Near-miss: Not a smudge of the whole head, and not an outline equally hard all the way around the egg. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A list from looking, which may differ from a standard “always lose the far side” slogan.
  2. The socket-into-temple edge, if it is not actually sharp: the larger false found edge, before the eyelash accent.
  3. Lost when values are close; found where a lock or a plane of hair cuts clearly against a lighter temple, if that is what you see.
  4. A specific critique and a matching, limited change.
  5. One likely accent (an inner lid, a nostril’s small dark, a lip’s found corner) noted, not a constellation of highlights.

Modern language · Spanish: telling the time

Check. 1. ¿Qué hora es? La clase es a las diez. 2. Son las cuatro. Son las cuatro y cuarto. Son las cuatro y media. Son las cuatro menos cuarto (3:45). 3. Model: telling time (present). Own words should match this idea: The present-tense pattern for the hour: ¿Qué hora es? Es la una (1:00), Son las dos (2:00 and the other hours). Minutes: y cuarto (15), y media (30), menos cuarto (15 before the next hour). Official 24-hour announcements can wait; today is the everyday pattern taught here. Near-miss: Not *Es las dos, and not *Son la una. The verb agrees with la una (singular) or las dos, las tres… (plural). This is still the present, not a past narrative tense. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The everyday pattern often names the next hour and subtracts a quarter, rather than adding 45 to six. (Son las seis y cuarenta y cinco is a different, more numerical style, not required today.)
  2. For example: Tengo que leer a las siete. Tenemos que pedir el libro a las diez.
  3. Es mediodía. Es medianoche. (Short forms with es, as taught here.)
  4. A full-sentence answer to an invented time, after hearing the question.
  5. Four invented lines, with a la una once and a las… for the others, agreed correctly.

Art history · Hellenistic sculpture: the Nike of Samothrace, and the Laocoön with a date caution

Check. 1. As a Hellenistic work usually dated to the second century BCE (often around the early second century), not as an exact year this course has fixed. 2. Because feeling, style, or pathos is not a calendar, and because the Laocoön’s date is actually debated. An invented year X would be a fake precision. 3. Model: Nike of Samothrace. Own words should match this idea: The marble “Winged Victory” now in the Louvre, a Hellenistic statue of the goddess Nike, usually dated to the second century BCE (often around the early second century; exact year is not settled, and this course will not invent one). It is a type example of Hellenistic movement and drapery, not a High Classical still stance like the Doryphoros type. Near-miss: Not a complete statue in its first condition: the sculpture is fragmentary (the head is missing, among other losses), and it has a modern museum setting that is not the original terrace on Samothrace. Laocoön (date caution). Own words should match this idea: The Vatican Laocoön group, a marble of Laocoön and his sons attacked by serpents, famous since its Renaissance rediscovery. Its date and whether it is a Hellenistic original or a later (even Roman) copy or version are argued; this course will not settle the argument, and it will not assign an exact year. Near-miss: Not a secure fifth-century work, and not a photograph of a named sculptor’s workshop in a known month. Vivid feeling in the marble is not itself a date. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. For example, the missing head, or the Louvre display instead of the original terrace: a modern photograph is not the ancient approach or the complete object.
  2. That Pliny’s sentence is certainly this very marble, in this very version, at a settled date.
  3. You can describe faces, strain, and a spiral composition. You should not jump from that description to an exact workshop date, or to a single sculptor’s name as if it were proved.
  4. Two captions: Nike — Louvre, fragmentary, second century BCE conventional dating. Laocoön — Vatican, date and original-or-copy status debated, describe the group.
  5. Date carefully; name copies, fragments, and later settings; look before you explain; do not invent measurements or years.
