Latin · Capitulum VII, part 2: dative plural, and verbs that take two objects

Check. 1. Mārcus puellīs rosās dat. 2. With cum, servīs is ablative (‘with the slaves’). In the giving sentence, there is no preposition and servīs is dative (‘to the slaves’). 3. Model: dative plural. Own words should match this idea: The ‘to/for’ form for more than one recipient, in the first and second declensions ending in -īs (puellīs, puerīs, servīs). Near-miss: The ending -īs is also ablative plural, which you have already met (with cum, in, ex). A preposition is the usual clue for the ablative; the bare ‘to/for’ job is dative. double object. Own words should match this idea: A verb such as dare can take both an accusative (the thing) and a dative (the person). Both are needed when you want to say who receives what. Near-miss: This is not two accusatives for ‘to’ and ‘what’. *Māter fīlium cibum dat is not the pattern taught here. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. puellīs; puerīs.
  2. Māter fīliō cibum dat (or fīliae, if the recipient is a daughter).
  3. For example, Pater fīliae fābulam nārrat.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. Two accusatives would make both the girls and the roses things given. The girls are recipients, so they take the dative: puellīs.

Mathematics · Geometry XI: the angle in a semicircle, and angles in the same segment

Check. 1. 55°. The triangle is right-angled at C, so the other two angles sum to 90°. 90° − 35° = 55°. 2. 42°. Angles in the same segment (on the same arc) are equal. 3. Model: angle in a semicircle. Own words should match this idea: An angle inscribed in a semicircle — the angle at the point C on the circle when AB is a diameter — is a right angle. This is Euclid III.31. It is often called Thales’ theorem, after Thales of Miletus; the name is traditional. This course uses the Euclidean statement and does not claim a reconstruction of Thales’ own proof. Near-miss: C must be on the circle, and AB must be a diameter (the central angle AOB is a straight angle, 180°). An inscribed angle on a shorter arc is not automatically 90°. angles in the same segment. Own words should match this idea: Inscribed angles that stand on the same arc (and so lie in the same segment of the circle) are equal. Euclid III.21. Near-miss: If one vertex is on the other arc (the other segment), the angle stands on the major or minor arc the other way, and the measures need not match. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

    1. (A scaled 5-12-13: sides doubled, 10-24-26.)
  2. True, in the sense taught here: the angle at the third vertex is 90°, so it is the angle in a semicircle on the hypotenuse; the hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumcircle.
  3. Not must. They might stand on equal arcs (or on the same arc). Equal inscribed angles stand on equal arcs, but those arcs need not be the same single arc.
  4. The inscribed angle standing on arc BC is ∠BAC = 20°, so the central angle on that arc is 40°.
  5. No. If the angle at C is 90°, AB is a diameter (III.31 in the other direction). A non-diameter chord would give a different inscribed measure.

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: a new public-domain model

Check. 1. A short imitation with a When-clause, two matching infinitives, and a main clause that requires reasons to be declared. Content not copied from the Declaration. 2. The two infinitives marked; any mismatch repaired (both to-forms, or another single shared shape). 3. Model: imitatio. Own words should match this idea: A writing exercise in which you keep the structure of a model sentence or passage and change the content. The aim is to feel the joints of the model, not to parody the topic and not to copy the wording of the subject matter. Near-miss: Not quotation, and not a summary of the Declaration. Do not keep the political claims and swap a few nouns. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A second, different imitation that still keeps the joints.
  2. Imitatio keeps structure and changes content. A long lift of the model’s wording is quotation, or pastiche of the original subject, not an exercise in the joints.
  3. For example: hold a long condition before the main clause; or put two necessary acts in parallel infinitives; or end by requiring that reasons be stated.
  4. Add a second infinitive in the same slot, joined by and, that is a genuine second act, not a restatement of the first.
  5. A revision if the match could not be heard; otherwise a brief note that the two acts match.

Science · Living color: why many leaves look green

Check. 1. It treats the color we see as the color most used. In the account taught today, green is the region absorbed less, so more of it remains to be seen. 2. Yes. Leftover green light can leave the leaf by transmission as well as by reflection; both paths can look green. 3. Model: chlorophyll. Own words should match this idea: The green pigment in leaves (and in other photosynthetic parts) that absorbs some of the light the plant uses. In ordinary leaves it is the pigment we notice first, because it absorbs more strongly in the blue and the red than in the green. Near-miss: Not the only pigment in a leaf (carotenoids, and in some leaves anthocyanins, are also present or can be made). And “plants are green because they like green light” is the wrong story: the green is, in the main, the leftover, not the most absorbed color. pigment. Own words should match this idea: A substance that absorbs some wavelengths of light more than others, and so gives a material a color when white light falls on it. Near-miss: Not the same as a binder in oil paint (week 4), though a paint pigment is the same kind of thing in a different place: a material that absorbs selectively. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Chlorophyll; blue and red.
  2. No particular absorption peaks or percentages, because they depend on the sample and the method, and this course will not invent them.
  3. Likeness: both use a pigment that absorbs some wavelengths more than others. Difference: a drying oil film is a binder plus pigment (and hardens by autoxidation); a leaf is living tissue, and the pigment’s job is tied to photosynthesis, which is next.
  4. Yellow or orange — the color of those pigments in leftover light — at this lesson’s level. Real autumn leaves can also involve other pigments; that caution comes later.
  5. A sentence that names chlorophyll, stronger absorption in red and blue than in green, and leftover green light reaching the eye.

Literature · Odyssey, Books 1–4: the Telemachy

Check. 1. Men who occupy Odysseus’s house, pressing Penelope to remarry and consuming the stores, while Telemachus cannot yet drive them out. 2. It is not the only translation, and a verse translation may still be better for reading the poem as verse. It is a public-domain option for short, attributed quotation. 3. Model: Telemachy. Own words should match this idea: The opening movement of the Odyssey (Books 1–4), whose foreground is Telemachus in Ithaca and on his journey to Pylos and Sparta, rather than Odysseus’s travels at sea. The poem delays the title character and lets the son’s search, the suitors’ pressure, and the household’s disorder be seen first. Near-miss: Not the whole Odyssey, and not a separate poem you must treat as detachable in every ancient edition. The name is a later convenience for this opening. suitors. Own words should match this idea: The men who occupy Odysseus’s house in Ithaca, pressing Penelope to remarry and consuming the household’s stores, while Telemachus is not yet able to drive them out. Near-miss: Not guests in the honorific sense of hospitality well kept. The poem will have much to say later about guests who abuse a house. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. In Sparta he hears that Odysseus still lives, held on Calypso’s island (a delay, not a death).
  2. The war is already over and the return has already been long delayed; we enter a story already in motion, with the son, not at the fall of Troy.
  3. A short accurate outline: Athena and Ithaca; the voyage to Nestor; Sparta and news of the living father; danger from the suitors at home. No Cyclops, no raft, no bow yet.
  4. The poem’s title character is still Odysseus, and most of the poem is still ahead. Telemachus is the opening focus, not the whole design.
  5. Two sentences on one chosen scene, grounded in Books 1–4 only, connecting the scene to the problem of the house.

Atelier · The head: working proportions, checked against the subject

Check. 1. If the plate disagrees with a remembered fraction, the plate wins. 2. Often between the brow line and the base of the nose, on a typical academic view; if this plate disagrees, follow the plate. 3. Model: working proportion. Own words should match this idea: A measured or constructed relationship (halfway, a third, an angle) used to start a drawing, then corrected against the subject. It is a tool, not a claim about how all beautiful heads are made. Near-miss: Not a canon you enforce against the plate, and not a set of invented “ideal beauty” fractions this course will ask you to memorize as facts about people. feature triangle. Own words should match this idea: The group of the two eyes (or the brows) and the base of the nose and mouth, placed as one shape on the front plane of the face, rather than as three ornaments. Near-miss: Not a geometry theorem, and not a requirement that the features form an equilateral triangle. It is a grouping habit. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Two real comparisons from the drawing (for example, “brow-to-chin equals brow-to-crown on this plate”), not a quoted ideal.
  2. Because the halfway construct, when it is used, is halfway down the whole head, not halfway down the face below the hairline only. (And, again, the plate wins.)
  3. The large shadow on the egg of the head — the features will not sit on a solid otherwise.
  4. It is foreshortened with the front plane of the face; the far eye shortens, and the center line is not the midline of the page.
  5. Any serious check: tilt, center line, height of the features as a group, or the insertion of the neck.

Modern language · Spanish: the present progressive (estar + -ando / -iendo)

Check. 1. hablando, comiendo, pidiendo. 2. Ella está sirviendo la comida. (or Ana está sirviendo…) 3. Model: present progressive. Own words should match this idea: A present-tense form that presents an action as in progress: estar + the present participle (-ando for -ar verbs, -iendo for most -er and -ir verbs). Instruction in English; the Spanish name is el presente progresivo (or estar + gerundio). This course will say “present participle” or “the -ando / -iendo form,” to avoid mixing it with the Latin gerund, which is a different form and is not being taught this week. Near-miss: Not a new tense meaning the past, and not estar + infinitive (*estoy comer). English “I am eating” is the usual match, but Spanish uses this form less automatically than English does: a simple present (Como ahora) can still be right. Today you learn to build the form correctly. present participle (-ando / -iendo). Own words should match this idea: The -ando or -iendo form used with estar to build the present progressive: hablando, comiendo, viviendo. Spelling changes to learn today: leer → leyendo; also pedir → pidiendo, servir → sirviendo (the e → i change appears in the participle for these verbs). Near-miss: Not the past participle (hablado, comido), which this lesson does not use to build the progressive, and not the infinitive. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. estoy leyendo, estás leyendo, estamos leyendo.
  2. The second piece must be a present participle (comiendo), not the infinitive.
  3. For example: Estoy pidiendo un libro. Estamos hablando.
  4. Spanish often uses the simple present for actions English describes as “right now” or as a current habit. The progressive is used when you want to present the action as in progress, not automatically whenever English uses “-ing.”
  5. Two heard forms, each in a correct estar + participle sentence.

Music theory · The natural minor scale, and relative minor

Check. 1. E F♯ G A B C D E (F♯ from G major; half steps G–A wait: 2–3 is F♯–G, 5–6 is B–C). 2. No. That is the parallel minor (same tonic). The relative minor of C major is A minor. 3. Model: natural minor scale. Own words should match this idea: A seven-note scale whose stepwise pattern of whole and half steps is whole–half–whole–whole–half–whole–whole (W H W W H W W). In A natural minor, the notes are A B C D E F G A — the same pitch set as C major, starting on A. Near-miss: Not the major scale (W W H W W W H), and not a claim about harmonic or melodic minor, which raise one or two notes and are not required today. “Minor” here is the scale quality, not “less important.” relative minor. Own words should match this idea: The minor scale that shares its key signature (its pitch set) with a given major scale, and starts a minor third below the major tonic, or on the sixth degree of the major scale. C major and A minor are relatives; G major and E minor are relatives. Near-miss: Not the parallel minor, which starts on the same tonic (C major and C minor). Parallel minor is a useful later contrast; it is not the same as relative. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Major: 3–4 and 7–8. Natural minor: 2–3 and 5–6.
  2. F major (up a minor third from D, or degree 3 of D minor is F; F major has Bb, which D natural minor also has).
  3. A short listening note: the first half step comes sooner (after two notes in A minor: B–C).
  4. Relative keys share a pitch set and a signature, but each has its own tonic (home note). C and A are different homes.
  5. That looks like A major (or a raised-note minor the student has jumped to). A natural minor is A B C D E F G A, with C and F natural, not sharped.
