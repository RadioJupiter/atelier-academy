Latin · Friday mix: the two times of the absolute, deponents, and fiō

Check. 1. The present-participle kind is contemporaneous. The perfect-participle kind is already complete when the main verb happens. 2. With the city having been captured, the farmers flee. 3. Loquor is deponent, so locūtō is active in meaning: with the boy having spoken. 4. Fit, fiunt, fierī. Fiō means “I become” or “it happens,” and is not a deponent, though some forms look passive.

  1. A sound pair will keep both words ablative and will mark the two times. Sample: Magistrō recitante, puerī silent; Opere cōnfectō, puerī lūdunt.
  2. The boys walk out of the school. It is an ordinary prepositional ablative, not a noun-plus-participle phrase.
  3. With these things having been said, the schoolmaster is silent.
  4. Those forms have not been taught.

Mathematics · Friday mix: pyramid and cone surface, and composites

Check. 1. Pyramid: B + 2bl. Cone: πrl + πr². 2. 96 square units. 3. 24π square units. 4. Volume 264 cubic units, surface 240 square units. Volume adds the two spaces in full. Surface counts only what is still outside.

  1. l = 5. Surface 144 square units.
  2. 96π square units.
  3. 90π + 12π = 102π cubic units.
  4. The joined face is not outside, so it is not part of the surface. It is also not extra space, so the volumes are simply added (or a cut-out is subtracted as a whole piece, not as a face).

Writing & Rhetoric · Friday mix: hints, reconstruction, and a précis

Check. 1. Hints, then reconstruction, of Inferno IV.25–42 in Longfellow. 2. “Without hope we live on in desire.” 3. Forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver. Do not invent a modern official conversion. 4. When it rests upon itself, it will no longer need the props beneath it. The whole arch then hung secure.

  1. A sound list will have: sighs, not lamentations; sorrow without torment; they sinned not; the two lacks; the guide among them; without hope they live on in desire.
  2. A sound short précis will have the refused full account, the Inner Sanctuary, and forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver.
  3. It is Dewing’s British spelling and stays inside the quotation.
  4. Inferno: importing a later commentary name. Dewing: inventing a kilogram equivalent, or a width for the crack.

Atelier · Friday mix: the jaw, the mouth, a check, and a pause

Check. 1. It starts in the shadow family. A seen bounce may enter it, but must remain darker than the light-family halftones. It is a plane, not a wire. 2. A slot, or a soft change, in the already-blocked form, not a dark line, a wire, or an invented smile. 3. Nose to mouth; mouth to the underside of the chin; width of the mouth against the already-placed eyes and nose; the underplane still in the shadow family if the light is from above. 4. A second lamp, a smile not seen, a color pass, or a bone name not used in the drawing.

  1. It must remain darker than the light-family halftones.
  2. Because a mouth that sits too high or too wide changes how the chin is read, and the other way around.
  3. The pause records the drawing. The Gray names are a separate elective, unless they were actually used in the work, which this week’s drawing lesson did not require.
  4. Full color remains reserved for Term 3.

Modern language · Spanish: Friday mix of pedir and dormir in the preterite

Check. 1. Pedí, pediste, pidió, pedimos, pedisteis, pidieron (pidió, pidieron change). Dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron (durmió, durmieron change). 2. In the present, the change happens in all forms except nosotros and vosotros. In the preterite, it happens only in the two third-person forms. 3. Sample: Ayer pedí un libro. Ella pidió ayuda. Anoche dormí bien. El niño durmió. 4. Pidimos and durmimos wrongly change the nosotros preterite. Duermió uses the present ue. Dirmió uses pedir’s i on dormir.

  1. Yesterday we asked for. Last night we slept.
  2. Pide is present; pidió is preterite. Duerme is present; durmió is preterite.
  3. Pidió belongs to pedir (asked for); durmió belongs to dormir (slept).
  4. Vi is last week’s preterite of ver, already taught, a different irregularity.

Elective · Anatomy: the mandible, from Gray 1918

Check. 1. The largest and strongest bone of the face. It serves for the reception of the lower teeth. 2. A curved, horizontal portion, the body, and two perpendicular portions, the rami, which unite with the ends of the body nearly at right angles. 3. With the two temporal bones. Last week already named the mandible as the movable exception among the twenty-two bones of the skull. 4. The mental foramen is for the passage of the mental vessels and nerve. The coronoid process is in front, the condyloid behind, separated by the mandibular notch. 5. Model: the mandible (Gray). Own words should match this idea: In Henry Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 20th ed., 1918, “The Mandible (Lower Jaw),” the mandible is “the largest and strongest bone of the face,” and “serves for the reception of the lower teeth.” “It consists of a curved, horizontal portion, the body, and two perpendicular portions, the rami, which unite with the ends of the body nearly at right angles.” It “articulates with the two temporal bones.” Last week already named it as the movable exception among the twenty-two bones of the skull. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s cranium of eight and face of fourteen, or at the mandible as the movable exception, already taught. Not a required recitation of Gray’s age-changes, or of Meckel’s cartilage, which remain reserved. The hyoid, already noted last week as not one of the twenty-two, remains outside this count. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. This is a first closer day. Those lists remain reserved, rather than being required as if they had been taught in full.
  2. No. Last week already noted that the hyoid is not one of the twenty-two.
  3. The earlier Bartleby lit-hub posting used in weeks 17–18 is no longer serving the page. The wording was checked on Theodora’s public-domain HTML of Gray 1918.
  4. The ossification, including Meckel’s cartilage, and the changes Gray describes at birth, in childhood, in the adult, and in old age.
