Latin · Capitulum X, part 3: the present infinitive, passive

Check. 1. habēre; habērī. 2. Potes vidērī. Homō vocārī potest. 3. Model: present infinitive (passive). Own words should match this idea: The ‘to be …-ed’ form that pairs with yesterday’s active infinitive. In this first look: vocārī (to be called), habērī (to be held / to be had), vidērī (to be seen). The ending is -rī on these verbs (the first and second conjugations as used today). Near-miss: Not the active vidēre (‘to see’), and not a finite passive (which this lesson does not teach as a full conjugation). vidērī is ‘to be seen,’ not ‘he is seen.’ The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. vidēre: the king can see. vidērī: the king can be seen.
  2. Two heard forms, each in a correct new sentence.
  3. A short, correctly labeled string.
  4. The job today is the infinitive pair, not a new finite conjugation.
  5. A complete copied set of pairs.

Mathematics · Algebra XI: Vieta’s formulas for a quadratic

Check. 1. x² − 3x − 4 (because sum = 3 = −b, so b = −3; product = −4 = c). 2. Sum = −6, product = 9. The roots are repeated: both −3 (a repeated root; the vertex is on the x-axis). 3. Model: Vieta’s formulas (quadratic). Own words should match this idea: For a quadratic equation, the sum and the product of the roots can be read from the coefficients. For x² + bx + c = 0, the sum of the roots is −b and the product is c. For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0, the sum is −b/a and the product is c/a. The name honors François Viète (Vieta). Near-miss: Not a substitute for solving when you are asked to find the roots, and not a claim that every quadratic has two real roots. If there are no real roots, today’s reading of a real sum and product from a real factored form does not apply in that way; this lesson works with quadratics that have two real roots (possibly repeated). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The sum is −b, not b. For x² + 5x + 6, the sum is −5.
  2. b = 0. The roots are symmetric about 0, and the vertex lies on the y-axis (the axis of symmetry is x = 0).
  3. x² − (½ + 6)x + (½)×6 = x² − (13/2)x + 3. Product 3, as required.
  4. The vertex’s x-coordinate is half the sum of the roots (the average).
  5. A fair set: the formulas come from expanding (x − r)(x − s); but they describe roots that the quadratic actually has, so a quadratic with no real roots is not given two real intercepts by the formulas in this lesson.

Writing & Rhetoric · The concession paragraph, revisited

Own words should match this idea: The middle paragraph of a three-paragraph essay, when its job is to grant a real point on the other side, in that side’s terms, and then return to the essay’s own job without a sneer or a sudden new subject. Near-miss: Not a fake concession (“some may say, wrongly…”), and not a second introduction. Last week’s parenthetical interruption is a sentence-level tool; today the grant is a whole paragraph’s job. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Physics · Mass and weight

Check. 1. W = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N. 2. m = W / g = 49 / 9.8 = 5 kg. 3. Model: mass (mechanics). Own words should match this idea: A measure of the amount of matter, and of how strongly the body resists a change of motion (inertia), in this first look. The SI unit is the kilogram (kg). Mass is not the same as weight. Near-miss: Not a synonym of weight, and not a number of newtons. A mass is measured in kilograms here, not in newtons. weight. Own words should match this idea: The gravitational force on a body. Near the Earth, in the first model already used, weight has size W = mg, with g = 9.8 N/kg, and is measured in newtons. The English word is related to Old English wiht / ġewiht, and to weigh. Near-miss: Not a mass in kilograms, and not a new unit. A scale that is labeled in kilograms is often reporting a mass inferred from a weight; this lesson will still keep the two words apart. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 19.6 N is a weight (a force). The mass in the earlier example was 2 kg.
  2. Weight would be 0; the mass would still be the same mass. (This is a first look, not a lesson in weightlessness as a full topic.)
  3. A fair set: weight can change, because it is mg and g can change; but mass in this model does not, so a 2 kg body is still 2 kg on the Moon.
  4. g is 9.8 N/kg near the Earth; G is the constant 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² in F = G Mm / r².
  5. Earth: 19.6 N. Moon (about one sixth): about 3.3 N.

History · A later name: the Julio-Claudian line, and the same source habit

Check. 1. 68 CE; this lesson refuses to call it a single constitutional “fall” day (it is the end of this line, and a year of civil war, in the conventional narrative). 2. Tiberius 14–37; Claudius 41–54. 3. Model: Julio-Claudian line (conventional). Own words should match this idea: A later name for the line of principes from Augustus through Nero, conventionally: Augustus (to 14 CE), Tiberius (14–37), Gaius (“Caligula,” 37–41), Claudius (41–54), and Nero (54–68). The name and the year-ranges are conventional labels, not a claim that the men used this family title in a modern way, and not a classroom proof of every year. Near-miss: Not a license to treat later anecdotes (in Suetonius or in Tacitus) as straightforward fact, and not a new “fall of the Republic” date at 68 CE. 68 CE is the end of this line in the conventional list, and a year of civil war; it is not taught here as a single constitutional day. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A fair set: later anecdotes can be useful, because they preserve claims and stories we would otherwise lack; but they are later and often shaped, so they are not simple facts only because they are vivid.
  2. The Republic’s “fall” was already refused as a single day last week; 68 CE is a later crisis of this line, not that missing day.
  3. A short paragraph that keeps that promise.
  4. Early second century CE, later than Tiberius; a later narrative, not an eyewitness diary of 14–37.
  5. The list, plus a caution about later writers or anecdotes.

Art history · The Pantheon in Rome, with a dating caution

Check. 1. The open circular eye in the dome. This lesson refuses an invented diameter or height. 2. Around 118–125 CE, labeled conventional, early second century CE. 3. Model: Pantheon (Hadrianic building, Agrippan inscription). Own words should match this idea: The Pantheon in Rome is a circular temple with a concrete dome and a central oculus, as we have it, generally treated as a rebuild under Hadrian in the early second century CE (a conventional range often given around 118–125 CE). The word comes from Latin Pantheon, from Greek Πάνθειον (Pantheion), “a temple of all gods,” from pan-, “all,” and theos, “god.” Near-miss: Not a work to be dated to 27 BCE just because the inscription names Agrippa, and not a work to be dated to 27 BCE just because that year was last week’s conventional start of the principate. A later rebuild can keep an earlier name. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Prima Porta is a marble copy of a type, not dated here to a single year; the Pantheon’s inscription is an earlier name on a later fabric. In both, a public text or image is not automatically the making-date of what we see.
  2. 27 BCE is the conventional start of the principate, and also the conventional association of Agrippa’s earlier temple. The political date does not date the present dome.
  3. A short paragraph that keeps that promise.
  4. No unsourced span, and no pretend-complete later history.
  5. A complete copied reminder.
