Mathematics · Algebra I: identities and factoring

Check. 1. 201² = (200 + 1)² = 200² + 2·200·1 + 1² = 40,000 + 400 + 1 = 40,401. 2. x³ − 8 = x³ − 2³ = (x − 2)(x² + 2x + 4). 3. Model: identity. Own words should match this idea: An equality that is true for every allowed value of its letters, and that must be proved by expanding or by using a known identity. Near-miss: (a + b)² = a² + b² is not an identity: the middle term 2ab is missing, so it fails as soon as a and b are not zero. factor. Own words should match this idea: To write an expression as a product; or one of those pieces. Identities used backwards are the usual method. Near-miss: Factoring is not ‘guessing a number that divides the constant term and hoping’ without a reason that the product matches. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (50 − 3)(50 + 3) = 2500 − 9 = 2491; (100 − 1)² = 10000 − 200 + 1 = 9801; 1000² − 1 = 999999; (60 + 5)² = 3600 + 600 + 25 = 4225.
  2. (x − 3y)(x + 3y); (x − 1)(x + 1)(x² + 1); (2a − 3)(4a² + 6a + 9); (x + 3)²; (a + 1)(a² − a + 1).
  3. (x² + 2x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 1) = 4x.
  4. a² + b² = (a + b)² − 2ab = 49 − 20 = 29. a³ + b³ = (a + b)³ − 3ab(a + b) = 343 − 210 = 133. (Check with a = 2, b = 5: 4 + 25 = 29, 8 + 125 = 133.)
  5. 2¹⁶ − 1 = (2⁸ − 1)(2⁸ + 1) = 255 × 257, and 255 = 3 × 5 × 17. ∎
  6. (2k + 1)² − (2k − 1)² = 8k. ∎
  7. Multiply out the right side: the nine terms a³, b³, c³ survive and the mixed terms cancel except −3abc. If a + b + c = 0, the right side is 0, so a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc. ∎

Writing & Rhetoric · Appositives and subordinating conjunctions

Check. 1. A noun phrase that renames the noun beside it. 2. It is a subordinate clause and needs a main clause. 3. Model: appositive. Own words should match this idea: A noun phrase set beside another noun to rename it: ‘Leonardo, the draughtsman, …’. Near-miss: An appositive is not a subordinate clause (it has no verb of its own), and it is not a separate sentence. subordinate clause. Own words should match this idea: A clause that cannot stand alone as a sentence; it starts with a word such as because, although, when, who, or that, and it depends on a main clause. Near-miss: A subordinate clause is not an appositive, and it is not a kernel. A kernel can stand alone. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Models: (a) Kiselev, a Russian mathematics teacher, wrote … (b) Erasmus, the Dutch humanist scholar, wrote De Copia. (c) The Iliad, Homer's epic of the Trojan War, begins …
  2. Models: (a) After the candle goes out, the gas in the jar cools and contracts. (b) Because vertical angles are equal, knowing one angle at an intersection gives all four. (c) When the plate is placed beside the drawing at the same size, errors of proportion become visible directly.
  3. (a) The stele, first set up in Babylonia, was taken to Susa as plunder, where it was excavated in 1901–02. (b) Although the runner covered 400 m in one lap, the runner's displacement was zero.

Physics · Acceleration and velocity–time graphs

Check. 1. Acceleration. 2. Acceleration is 0; displacement is 5 × 4 = 20 m. 3. Model: acceleration. Own words should match this idea: The rate of change of velocity: how quickly velocity increases, decreases, or changes direction. Near-miss: Acceleration is not ‘going fast’, and a negative acceleration is not a different kind of quantity from a; it is acceleration in the negative direction (or a slowing down, if velocity was positive). velocity–time graph. Own words should match this idea: A graph with time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis. The slope is acceleration; the area between the graph and the time axis is displacement. Near-miss: It is not a position–time graph. A straight line sloping down on a v–t graph is constant (nonzero) acceleration, not ‘slowing only if the line is curved’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

History · Babylon: the laws of Hammurabi

Check. 1. Prefer the source with better access to the object and modern geological analysis (the museum that holds it), and note that the older term was standard at the time. Neither source is 'lying'; knowledge changed. 2. Model: stele. Own words should match this idea: An upright stone slab with a relief or an inscription, set up to be seen in public. Near-miss: A stele is not a book and not a clay tablet, though the same laws may also survive on tablets. lex talionis. Own words should match this idea: The rule of retaliation in kind: the penalty matches the injury, ‘an eye for an eye’. In Hammurabi’s code it applies fully only to a victim of full status. Near-miss: It is not automatically ‘the harshest possible law’. A limit to one eye also restrains a feud from taking more than an eye. It is also not applied equally to every rank in this code. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Made for Hammurabi, displayed publicly in a temple context, addressed to his subjects, to the gods and to future kings. Purposes: to present the king as a just ruler chosen by the gods, to publish standards for judgment, and to preserve his reputation. Stone is permanent and public.
  2. Retaliation in kind (lex talionis) for injury to a man of full status. For lower-status free people the penalty becomes a fixed payment, and for a slave half the slave's value, payable (it is implied) to the owner. Punishment depended on the social rank of the victim: the society was formally hierarchical.
  3. Identify which source has the better access to the evidence (the museum holding the object, with modern geological analysis) and prefer the more recent expert identification, while noting that the older term was the standard description at the time. Neither source is "lying"; knowledge changed.
  4. Either side is acceptable if argued from the text. A strong answer notes that the penalty is limited to equivalence (no more than an eye), which restrains blood feud, while also noting that it is applied unequally by class.

Modern language · Spanish: ser and description

Check. 1. somos. 2. ser (La ciudad es antigua). 3. Model: ser. Own words should match this idea: The Spanish verb used for what something is: identity, origin, characteristics, and time. (It will be contrasted with estar later.) Near-miss: Ser is not the verb for place or temporary condition; that will be estar. Do not treat them as one English ‘is’. present tense of ser. Own words should match this idea: The forms of ser used for now: soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son. Each form belongs to a subject. Near-miss: Es does not go with nosotros, and soy is not a word for ‘I have’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Art history · How to look; the first images

Check. 1. Any four from: line, shape (or mass), value, color, texture, space, composition. (The list in the lesson is the full set.) 2. Model: formal analysis. Own words should match this idea: A description of what is visibly there in a work — line, shape, mass, value, color, texture, space, composition — before any interpretation. Near-miss: Formal analysis is not ‘this was probably hunting magic’, and it is not a biography of the artist. Interpretation comes second and must be labeled. interpretation. Own words should match this idea: A claim about meaning or purpose, clearly marked as such, and paired with the evidence that would test it. Near-miss: An interpretation is not a formal analysis, and it is not a fact just because it is often repeated. Scholars still disagree about the purpose of cave art. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Check that every sentence describes something visible: e.g., a continuous dark contour of varying weight along the back; flat areas of red-brown; a head turned in profile; the belly line following a natural ridge of rock.
  2. Interpretations (e.g., hunting magic, seasonal records) must be labeled and paired with testable evidence (e.g., do depicted species match the bones found in the cave's food debris?). Note that scholars still disagree about the purpose of cave art.
