Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 2: the relative quī, quae, quod

Check. 1. Puer quī in hortō est Mārcus est. 2. Liber quem Mārcus habet magnus est. 3. Model: relative pronoun (quī, quae, quod). Own words should match this idea: A pronoun that introduces a relative clause and refers back to a noun (its antecedent). The form agrees with the antecedent in gender and number, but takes the case required by its own clause. A first working set: quī (masc. nom.), quae (fem. nom.), quod (neuter nom./acc.), quem, quam. Near-miss: Not the interrogative quis? / quid? (‘who?’ / ‘what?’), though the forms overlap in part. A relative clause does not, by itself, ask a question. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. quī (he is the subject of labōrat).
  2. For example, Rosa quam Iūlia Mārcō dat pulchra est.
  3. quis? asks a question (‘who?’). quī introduces a relative clause and refers back to a noun.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. Gender and number from the antecedent; case from the job inside the relative clause.

Mathematics · Algebra IX: choosing an AP or a GP, and a first applied sum

Check. 1. A GP with r = 1/2 (invented readings). S = 80((1/2)^4 − 1)/(1/2 − 1) = 80(1/16 − 1)/(−1/2) = 80(−15/16)/(−1/2) = 80 × (15/16) × 2 = 150 L, as a sum of readings only. 2. S = 12. Both S = n a (r = 1) and S = n/2 · (2+2) (d = 0) give 12. 3. Model: mixed progressions. Own words should match this idea: Problems that ask you to decide whether a sequence is arithmetic, geometric, or neither, and then to use the matching formula for a term or a sum — not to apply one formula to every list of numbers. Near-miss: Not ‘if it is increasing, it is a GP,’ and not a claim that every useful sequence is one of the two. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. GP, a=4, r=3, n=5, S=4(3^5−1)/(3−1)=4(243−1)/2=4×242/2=484.
  2. AP, a=4, d=8, n=5, last=4+32=36, S=5/2×(4+36)=100.
  3. n/2·(2+54)=2×56=112, but the true sum is 80. The pairing formula needs a constant difference.
  4. Any sequence with neither constant d nor constant r, with both tests shown.
  5. a=7, ℓ=−20, n=10, S=5×(7−20)=5×(−13)=−65.

Writing & Rhetoric · Reconstruction from a three-paragraph outline

Check. 1. A short hint-list that stores joints, not full sentences from a book. 2. Three short paragraphs plus a specific kept/missed list (for example, “I never returned to the thesis in new words”). 3. Model: reconstruction (three paragraphs). Own words should match this idea: A Franklin step in which you write a short three-paragraph piece from a hint-list only, then compare it with your outline or with a model, and note the joints you kept or missed. Near-miss: Not a first draft written with the outline open the whole time (that is ordinary drafting). Reconstruction is from the hints after they have been set aside for a moment, then a comparison. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A local repair of the named miss.
  2. A précis restates a source’s meaning in short form. A reconstruction is your argument (or your exposition) built from your own hints, then compared with your plan or a model of form.
  3. A cut if needed; otherwise a note that the draft is already short enough to reread.
  4. A note that the quotation is introduced, attributed, and explained — or a cut if it was ornamental.
  5. A packet of hints + draft + one named miss, ready for Friday’s essay, still short.

Science · Paper chromatography of leaf pigments

Check. 1. A concentrated spot of extract sits above the solvent; the solvent climbs and carries pigments, which form bands. 2. It explains why the pigments travel differently, without giving a number: different balances of sticking and dissolving. 3. Model: paper chromatography. Own words should match this idea: A separation method in which a solvent moves up a piece of paper by capillary action and carries dissolved substances at different rates, so they form bands. A leaf extract can show more than one pigment, even when the leaf looked simply green. Near-miss: Not a proof of every pigment’s chemical formula, and not a measurement this course will invent as an Rf value (the ratio of a band’s travel to the solvent’s travel). Those ratios depend on the paper, the solvent, and the conditions. carotenoid (leaf). Own words should match this idea: A class of yellow and orange leaf pigments, already present in many green leaves, usually masked by chlorophyll in midsummer views of the leaf. In a chromatogram they may appear as a yellow or orange band. Near-miss: Not “the” single yellow chemical, and not an invented percentage of the leaf’s pigment mass. Some leaves also make red or purple anthocyanins, which are a different class and will be named carefully on Day 29. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Many extracts show more than one band, but a particular leaf, solvent, or faint extract might show little separation. The method can fail or can be faint; that is not a reason to invent bands, or to require them.
  2. If a yellow (or other) band appears, a second pigment was in the extract. The paper shows presence by separation, not a percentage by mass.
  3. It will not copy or invent those numbers as if they were constants of nature, and it will not treat another course’s sheet as this course’s measurement.
  4. A short plan: real protocol, adult care for solvent, a spot above the liquid line, observation of bands, no invented Rf.
  5. If yellow pigments are already in a green leaf, they might remain visible when chlorophyll is no longer dominating the leaf’s color — a hypothesis to treat carefully on Day 29, not a conclusion yet.

Literature · Odyssey, Books 9–12: the wanderings, told in Scheria

Check. 1. Any four from: Cyclops; Circe; the visit to the dead; the Sirens; Scylla and Charybdis; the cattle of the Sun (and the arrival, in the tale, toward Calypso). 2. The Phaeacians host a stranger and hear his name; the Cyclops, in the tale, traps and eats guests. The contrast is in the poem; it need not be preached as a single moral for every episode. 3. Model: nostos. Own words should match this idea: A homecoming or return, especially the return of a Greek hero from Troy. The Odyssey is, among other things, a poem of Odysseus’s nostos, delayed and endangered, and of the home he must still recover when he arrives. Near-miss: Not already an arrival in Ithaca (that is later). A nostos can be narrated before it is completed. wanderings (Books 9–12). Own words should match this idea: The adventures Odysseus narrates to the Phaeacians in Books 9–12, which belong earlier in the story’s time than Books 5–8. They include, in a usual school list, the Cyclops, Circe, the underworld, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the cattle of the Sun, after which Odysseus reaches Calypso’s island alone. Near-miss: Not a complete catalogue you must memorize as twelve named episodes in a test of trivia, and not a historical itinerary. Hold the design (a story inside the story) and a few named episodes, carefully. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. You already know a house in trouble and a son looking for a living father, so a delay in the wanderings is not only adventure: it is time stolen from that house. (Other modest connections are acceptable.)
  2. A short summary of the tale told in Scheria, ending before Ithaca’s hall.
  3. A short attributed quotation, or a labeled paraphrase.
  4. The poem is a designed narrative of wanderings, not a sailing chart this course can verify as history.
  5. The return to Ithaca, the test of the bow, and the fighting in the hall — unless already assigned, they should not be narrated as if they had been read.

Atelier · The nose and mouth, in the context of the head

Check. 1. Front, two sides, and an underside (a small under-plane). Nostrils belong in that under-plane. 2. The feature triangle is wrong, and the likeness to the plate or plaster fails, however tidy the lips are. 3. Model: nose and mouth in context. Own words should match this idea: The nose and mouth placed as part of the feature triangle on the existing head, with the under-plane of the nose, the center line through the mouth, and their value families, rather than a separately perfected nostril or lip on a blank mid-face. Near-miss: Not a smile invented to make a plaster “friendly,” and not a nostril hole punched darker than the head’s large shadow without a look at the light. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A real measurement from today’s sitting, not a memorized ideal fraction.
  2. More of one side plane, less of the other; the underside may show as a tapering shape, not a symmetrical front view.
  3. Where the plane turns firmly, or where a lip meets a lit or dark neighbor with a clear value cut — after the large families are true.
  4. The subject wins; a plaster’s expression is part of the form. Inventing a smile is inventing a different object.
  5. A specific guess (for example, lost: the far lip into the cheek; found: the underside of the nose against the lit upper lip, if the values actually cut).

Modern language · Spanish: este, ese, and aquel

Check. 1. este libro, esta casa, estos libros, estas casas; aquel libro, aquella casa, aquellos libros, aquellas casas (nouns may vary). 2. Esta clase… aquella clase… (or esta / aquella with a verb). 3. Model: demonstratives (este, ese, aquel). Own words should match this idea: Adjectives (and, with small changes, pronouns) that point: este / esta / estos / estas (this, near the speaker), ese / esa / esos / esas (that, near the listener or a mid distance), aquel / aquella / aquellos / aquellas (that, farther away). They agree in gender and number with the noun. Near-miss: Not the definite article (el / la), which does not point in this way, and not English “this/that” used without agreement. Neuter pronouns esto, eso, aquello point to a thing or an idea not named as a noun; they are invariable and do not take a noun after them. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. For example: ¿Me das ese lápiz? Esa frase es tuya.
  2. For example: Esto es importante. ¿Qué es eso?
  3. Likeness: pointing words that change with gender (and here, number). Difference: Spanish, in this lesson, has a three-way set (este / ese / aquel); Latin was taught as a near/far pair (hic / ille), with is as a weaker reference pronoun.
  4. Three heard, correctly agreed noun phrases, one for each distance.
  5. Me gusta este libro. Me gustan aquellas casas.

Music theory · I, IV, and V in a major key, and a first authentic cadence

Check. 1. A short listening sentence: V–I feels more like a close than IV–I, for many ears in this pattern; either a honest “I hear a close” or a modest “I hear a difference” is acceptable. 2. Because G major has F♯ (the leading tone), and V is a major triad: D–F♯–A. D–F–A would be a minor triad, the wrong quality for V in a major key as taught today. 3. Model: I, IV, and V (major key). Own words should match this idea: The primary triads of a major key: the tonic (I, built on degree 1), the subdominant (IV, on degree 4), and the dominant (V, on degree 5). In C major, I is C–E–G, IV is F–A–C, and V is G–B–D — three major triads. Near-miss: Not the only chords in a key, and not yet a full set of minor-key primary chords (which would need more than last week’s natural minor in many common-practice uses). Today is a major key only. authentic cadence (first look). Own words should match this idea: A close or ending in which V moves to I, so the dominant triad resolves to the tonic. This course uses it as a first, audible cadence, not as a complete catalogue of cadence types (plagal, half, deceptive, and the rest can wait). Near-miss: Not every V–I on a page is the end of a piece, and not a claim that popular or folk music must use this close. It is a common-practice school pattern, taught as such. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Degrees 1, 4, and 5. First naming: tonic is the home chord; dominant is built on 5 and leads, in this pattern, toward I; subdominant is built on 4, below the dominant in name and often used before V or as a neighbor to I. Keep this modest.
  2. The close is still V–I; the first I is a starting tonic, not itself the cadence. The cadence is the ending move.
  3. A more careful later definition can require both chords in root position and a soprano ending on the tonic. Today only the V–I motion is required.
  4. I: F–A–C. IV: B♭–D–F. V: C–E–G.
  5. Today’s V is the triad G–B–D. The added F is a seventh above G; the chord would be a dominant seventh, which this lesson names only as an extra note, not as a new required type.
