Own words should match this idea: An interrogative word meaning “how many?” It does not decline in the use taught today. Near-miss: Not quis (who) and not quod (the relative or the neuter of qui, already taught). quot asks for a number. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  5. An original quot question and answer.

Mathematics · Geometry XVI: loci — a circle, and the perpendicular bisector

Check. 1. The circle with center C and radius 6 cm. 2. The midpoint M satisfies MA = MB (each is half of AB), so M is on the locus; it is where the bisector meets AB. 3. Model: locus. Own words should match this idea: The set of all points that satisfy a stated condition. (The plural is often written loci.) From Latin locus, “place.” Near-miss: Not a single point chosen at random, and not a graph of a function unless that graph is being described as a set of points (x, f(x)). A locus is named by a condition, not by a plotted table alone. perpendicular bisector as a locus. Own words should match this idea: The locus of points equidistant from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of the segment AB. Near-miss: Not “any line through the midpoint,” and not the circle with diameter AB (that is a different locus: the angle in a semicircle, already taught, is a property of points on that circle, not this equidistance condition). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. No. On the bisector, the distances to A and to B are equal. 5 cm and 7 cm are not equal.
  2. The circle on diameter AB is the set of points from which AB subtends a right angle (III.31). The bisector is the set of points equidistant from A and B. They share the midpoint of AB, but they are not the same set.
  3. Equal openings make a crossing equidistant from A and from B, which is the condition of the locus.
  4. Yes: the circle with center O and radius 4 cm.
  5. A marked construction with a labeled P, and PA = PB stated.

Science · From the retina to a signal

Check. 1. At the retina (the signal leaves from there toward the brain). 2. Light-sensitive receptors (no image is received at that exit). 3. Model: optic nerve. Own words should match this idea: The nerve that carries a signal from the retina toward the brain. In this first look, it is named as the path of that signal, not as a counted bundle of fibers. Near-miss: Not the cornea, and not a second “eye” at the back. It is the path out, not the receiving layer. blind spot. Own words should match this idea: A place on the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eye, and where there are no light-sensitive receptors, so no image is received there. Near-miss: Not a dark patch you should see in ordinary vision, and not a disease. It is a normal consequence of the nerve’s exit. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. This lesson has no sourced count to teach; a number would be an invention.
  2. A camera sensor need not have a nerve-exit gap; the retina does.
  3. The blind spot is at the nerve’s exit on the retina, after the image is formed there.
  4. A fair set: there is a blind spot, because the nerve exits there; but we do not usually notice it, so a first look must name the gap rather than trust everyday noticing.
  5. A short paragraph that keeps that promise.

Modern language · Spanish: dates

Check. 1. For example: Es el 8 de julio. Es el primero de abril. 2. Es el 17 de noviembre. 3. Model: dates in Spanish (present). Own words should match this idea: A present-tense way to give a date: Es el + number + de + month (for the first, often Es el primero de + month). The year may be added after de, if needed, as a number. Near-miss: Not *Es el uno de mayo for the first (use primero), and not an English ‘November 17th’ word order as the Spanish pattern. Months are not capitalized in Spanish the way English month names are. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Spanish puts the day first: Es el 5 de mayo, not an English month-first order, and mayo is not capitalized here.
  2. Son las dos: the clock. Es el 2 de mayo: the date.
  3. A heard pattern used in a correct new date sentence.
  4. A short, correct three-sentence string.
  5. enero, febrero, marzo, abril, mayo, junio, julio, agosto, septiembre, octubre, noviembre, diciembre — checked, uncapitalized.
