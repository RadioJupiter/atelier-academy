The answer is a place, not a person. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Mathematics · Algebra IV: systems of linear equations

Check. 1. Bars: three equal units make 90 cm, so one unit is 30 cm. The pieces are 30 cm and 60 cm. Check: 30 + 60 = 90 and 60 = 2 × 30. 2. Add: 3x = 21, so x = 7 and y = 8. Check: 7 + 8 = 15 and 14 − 8 = 6. ✓ 3. Model: system of equations. Own words should match this idea: Two or more equations that must be true at the same time, about the same unknowns. A solution is a pair (or triple, …) that fits every equation. Near-miss: A system is not ‘two problems side by side that may use different unknowns’. And a pair that fits only one of the equations is not a solution of the system. substitution. Own words should match this idea: A method for solving a system: use one equation to express one unknown in terms of the other, and put that expression into the second equation. Near-miss: Substitution is not guessing a number and checking, and it is not changing the unknown in only one place and leaving it in another. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 3y + 2y = 20, 5y = 20, y = 4, x = 12.
  2. Add: 3x = 12, x = 4, y = 3. Check: 8 + 3 = 11 and 4 − 3 = 1.
  3. Let x be the number of $8 tickets. Then 8x + 12(20 − x) = 200, 8x + 240 − 12x = 200, −4x = −40, x = 10. So 10 at $8 and 10 at $12. Check: 80 + 120 = 200.
  4. The first pair is the same line twice: infinitely many solutions (all pairs with x + y = 4). The second pair contradicts: no solution.
  5. Legs a and 24 − a, with (1/2)a(24 − a) = 72, so a(24 − a) = 144, a² − 24a + 144 = 0, (a − 12)² = 0. Both legs are 12 cm (an isosceles right triangle).

Writing & Rhetoric · The semicolon and the conjunctive adverb

Check. 1. The two tangent segments are equal; therefore, they are drawn from the same outside point. (Or: …equal; they are drawn… if the relation is left implicit.) Because they are drawn from the same point is also a legal one-sentence version, but today’s form asked for a conjunctive adverb. 2. A colon: The three questions are these: ubi, quō, and unde. 3. Model: semicolon. Own words should match this idea: The mark (;) that can join two closely related complete sentences without a coordinating conjunction. Each side must be able to stand as a sentence on its own. Near-miss: A semicolon is not a fancy comma, and it cannot join a sentence to a fragment. It is also not a colon: a colon often introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation after a complete clause. conjunctive adverb. Own words should match this idea: A word such as however, therefore, moreover, meanwhile, or otherwise that shows the relation between two complete sentences. When it joins them, a semicolon stands before it and a comma after it. Near-miss: A conjunctive adverb is not a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so). You cannot legally write ‘The locative looks like a genitive, however it does not show possession’ — that is a comma splice. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Each pair must be two complete sentences, with the semicolon before the adverb and a comma after it.
  2. A construction uses a compass; a ruler with marks is not allowed. Or use and or a period.
  3. A colon would present the second clause as an explanation or unfolding of the first, not as a contrast. Contrast wants but, however, or a semicolon with that relation clear.
  4. Lincoln’s opening is mostly commas and dashes in some texts; the student should describe the marks actually in the copy being used and judge whether they fit.
  5. For example: The three place questions are these: ubi, quō, and unde.

Physics · Vectors and components

Check. 1. √(12² + 5²) = √(144 + 25) = √169 = 13 m. 2. Each component is 20/√2 = 10√2 N (or 20 × √2/2). 3. Model: vector. Own words should match this idea: A quantity that has both a size (magnitude) and a direction, such as displacement, velocity, acceleration, or force. It can be drawn as an arrow, and it can be added by placing arrows tip to tail. Near-miss: A vector is not just an arrow on the page, and it is not the same as its magnitude. Distance and speed are not vectors; displacement and velocity are (Day 1). component. Own words should match this idea: The projection of a vector along a chosen axis. In a plane, vx and vy are the horizontal and vertical components, and the magnitude is √(vx² + vy²) when the axes are perpendicular. Near-miss: A component is not a second, independent object riding along with the vector. It is the same vector, described along an axis. You may not add magnitudes of perpendicular components as if they were parallel. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  2. The 3 m and 4 m are perpendicular, so they are components, not parallel lengths. The displacement is 5 m, not 7 m.
  3. Magnitude 10 m/s; the y-component points in the negative y-direction (down or south, depending on the axes).
  4. B. The forces are perpendicular vectors, so the net magnitude is √(3² + 4²) = 5 N, not 7 N.
  5. The drawing should match the Learn diagram: the vector as the diagonal, components along the axes.

History · Kingship and the afterlife: the Pyramid Texts of Unas

Check. 1. Religious texts for the king’s afterlife, first in the royal pyramid of Unas (late 5th Dynasty) at Saqqara. 2. A question that asks for the translator, the edition, and whether the passage is complete or selected. 3. Model: Pyramid Texts. Own words should match this idea: Religious texts, in the Egyptian language, carved on the walls of royal pyramids beginning with the pyramid of Unas (Wenis), last king of the 5th Dynasty, at Saqqara. They are among the oldest substantial religious texts from Egypt, and they concern the king’s afterlife, offerings, and ascent. Near-miss: The Pyramid Texts are not a novel, not a single ‘book of the dead’ in the later New Kingdom sense, and not a text you can date to Khufu’s reign. They begin, in the royal pyramids, at the end of the 5th Dynasty, later than the Great Pyramid. kingship (Egyptian, this period). Own words should match this idea: The office of the Egyptian king (often called pharaoh in later usage) as both a political ruler and a figure in religion: the king’s tomb, titles, and afterlife texts present him as the one who maintains order and who will join the gods. Near-miss: Kingship is not ‘tyranny’ as a slogan, and it is not the same in every century of Egyptian history. The Old Kingdom is not Ramesses II’s Egypt (Day 15). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Likenesses might include royal voice and a claim to order or permanence; limits include century, language, genre (law code vs afterlife text), and audience. A passing paragraph names both sides.
  2. Djoser (step pyramid, earlier), then Khufu (4th Dynasty), then Unas (end of 5th).
  3. ‘Pharaoh’ is a later common term; for this period ‘king’ plus the personal name is often more precise, and later usage should not be read back without a note.
  4. You cannot source the wording, so you cannot use it as a quotation in the course’s sense (Day 18 of writing).
  5. Ramesses II, with Hattusili III — the next lesson.

Atelier · The sphere, and hatching that follows the form

Check. 1. Hatching is value built with rows of strokes that follow the form. It is not a scribble, and not a curtain of straight lines that ignore the surface. 2. The cube changes at an edge between planes, fairly suddenly. The sphere’s terminator is a curve on a continuous surface, usually a softer band (core shadow), not a folded paper edge. 3. Model: hatching. Own words should match this idea: A way of building value with rows of strokes, rather than with a smudge alone. In this course the strokes follow the surface: they run as if they were lying on the form, changing direction as the surface turns. Near-miss: Hatching is not a scribble, and it is not a set of straight, parallel rain-lines that ignore the form (unless you are deliberately making a flat diagram). Cross-hatching — a second layer at an angle to the first — is tomorrow’s refinement, not a way to hide a careless first layer. the sphere as a form. Own words should match this idea: A solid whose surface is everywhere curved, with no plane edges. Under one light it shows the same families as the egg, but it is fully round: the terminator is a closed curve, and the cast shadow is typically an ellipse on the table. Near-miss: A sphere is not a shaded disc (a flat circle filled in), and a hard outline all the way around will flatten it. The contour is a turning edge: often sharper toward the light, softer or lost in the shadow. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A constructed sphere with form-following hatch and the families honestly placed, plus a cast shadow.
  2. The five landmarks are marked in the right places, not as a list floating off the form.
  3. Three sentences that report a real highlight, a real turn of hatch, and a real correction.
  4. A small, even patch that turns, rather than a rushed whole.
  5. On a cube or on any true plane, a nearly even tone (or a hatch that stays flat on that plane) is still right; curved hatch on a flat plane would invent a turn that is not there.

Modern language · Spanish: ser and estar contrasted

Check. 1. somos (origin); está (place). 2. Es aburrido: he/it is boring. Está aburrido: he is bored. Gender and number must agree with the subject the student chose. 3. Model: ser versus estar. Own words should match this idea: Two verbs that both translate as ‘to be’, used for different kinds of being. Ser is for identity, origin, characteristics, and time. Estar is for place and for a condition or state. Near-miss: Ser is not ‘permanent’ as a magic rule, and estar is not ‘temporary’ as a magic rule. A place can be permanent (Madrid está en España) and still take estar, because it is a location. adjective with ser or estar. Own words should match this idea: Some adjectives change their sense according to the verb: ser + adjective names a characteristic; estar + adjective names a condition. Near-miss: This is not permission to swap the verbs at random, and it does not apply to every adjective in the same way. When in doubt, go back to the two jobs: characteristic versus condition or place. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Ella es estudiante, y (ella) está en casa.
  2. A condition of the door (open) takes estar: La puerta está abierta.
  3. es; estamos.
  4. E.g. París está en Francia. or El museo está en la ciudad. Estar is required.
  5. Three grammatical sentences, with at least one real ser use and one real estar use, not a random swap.

Art history · The Aegean: Cycladic, Minoan, Mycenaean

Check. 1. A triangular space above the lintel that lightens the load on the lintel and holds the relief. The relief shows two lionesses flanking a column; the heads are lost. 2. It is earlier, from a different culture and set of conventions, and it is not made in the later Greek city-state world of kouroi and the Classical figure (Day 18). Date, place, and style all disagree with that label. 3. Model: Aegean Bronze Age. Own words should match this idea: The Bronze Age cultures of the Aegean Sea and its islands and shores, treated here in three parts: Cycladic (the Cyclades islands), Minoan (Crete), and Mycenaean (the mainland palatial centers, such as Mycenae). The name Aegean is geographic, not the name those people used for themselves. Near-miss: ‘Aegean’ is not a single kingdom, and it is not ‘Greece’ in the later Archaic or Classical sense (Day 18). It is also not Egyptian art, though contact existed. the name ‘Minoan’. Own words should match this idea: A modern name, coined by Arthur Evans in the early twentieth century from Minos, the mythical king of Crete, for the Bronze Age culture he was excavating at Knossos. It is not a name recovered from those people’s own writing in a form we can read with certainty. Near-miss: ‘Minoan’ is not a word we should treat as an ancient self-name, and a heavily restored fresco is not a photograph of the second millennium BCE. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A paragraph that describes pose and form and that marks a limit (use, original paint, identity).
  2. Pigment worked with plaster (true fresco, on wet plaster, is the strict sense). Restoration can complete, repaint, or even invent passages; a photograph may not separate those layers.
  3. A fair contrast: a fortified citadel gate with a relieving triangle and a heraldic relief, versus the Egyptian temple’s emphasis on a processional stone architecture and a different system of figure convention (composite view). Do not invent a claim about which is ‘better’.
  4. Because we cannot read Linear A as we read Linear B, claims about Minoan institutions have to rest on other evidence and should be labeled as such.
  5. A real although-sentence: contact is not the same as identity of style, or: shared materials do not make a Cycladic figure an Egyptian one.
