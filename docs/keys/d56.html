Check. 1. puella is the subject (nominative). rosam is the object (accusative). 2. The object, the thing given, is rosam (accusative). puellae is the person to whom (dative). 3. Model: cases as jobs, a Term 1 close. Own words should match this idea: A close of the case jobs already taught in Capitula I–X: nominative for the subject, accusative for the object, genitive for ‘of,’ dative for the person to or for whom, and ablative in already-taught phrases such as in with the ablative. Mathematics · A quadratic inequality, from the graph: a Term 1 close

Check. 1. 4 < x < 10. 2. No real solution. A square is never negative; it is 0 only at x = 3. 3. Model: a quadratic inequality, a Term 1 close. Own words should match this idea: A close of the already-taught method: read a quadratic inequality from the graph of the already-taught parabola. For an upward-opening product such as (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0, the solution is between the roots: 2 < x < 6. The method is not new today. Near-miss: Not a linear inequality, not Euclid III.36, and not a licence to invent a complex solution for a square that is never negative. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. x ≤ 2 or x ≥ 8 (include the roots, where the expression is 0).
  2. The downward-opening companion is below the axis outside the roots: x < 2 or x > 8.
  3. III.36 is a tangent and a secant from an exterior point, a different picture. The 2 and the 8 here are roots, not automatically PA and PB.
  4. A note that complex numbers are not begun.

Logic · Valid forms, a Term 1 close: ponens, tollens, and disjunctive syllogism

Check. 1. If P then Q; P; therefore Q. If it is a square, then it is a rectangle; it is a square; therefore it is a rectangle. 2. Modus tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Denying the antecedent uses not-P to infer not-Q, and is invalid. The same non-square rectangle blocks that invalid form. 3. Model: valid “or” and conditional forms, a Term 1 close. Own words should match this idea: A close of three already-taught valid forms: modus ponens (If P then Q; P; therefore Q), modus tollens (If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P), and disjunctive syllogism (P or Q; not P; therefore Q). This course’s default “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one. They are different forms, not three names for the same move. Near-miss: Not affirming the consequent, not denying the antecedent, and not inferring not-Q from P under an inclusive “or.” Necessary and sufficient conditions remain Day 16’s lesson and are not reteached as new. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. That is affirming the consequent, already taught as invalid. A non-square rectangle is a rectangle and not a square.
  2. A sound invented or already-taught pair, with not-P therefore Q (or not-Q therefore P).
  3. Those conditions were taught on Day 16 and are not a new Term 1 close today.
  4. A note that the seminar is not a reward.
