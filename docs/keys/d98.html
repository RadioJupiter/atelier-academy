Latin · Deponent perfect participles in the absolute

Check. 1. A deponent perfect participle, used with a noun in the ablative, already complete when the main verb happens, and translated as active. 2. With the boy having spoken, the schoolmaster answers. 3. Loquor is deponent, so the participle is active in meaning, even though the form looks passive. 4. With the enemies having followed, the farmers flee. 5. Model: deponent perfect participle in the absolute. Own words should match this idea: A deponent verb, already taught in the third person, the infinitive, and the present participle, also has a perfect participle. In the absolute, that participle stays active in meaning, as deponents do: hīs locūtīs, magister tacet means “with these things having been said,” in the sense that someone has spoken, not “having been spoken” as a passive of an ordinary verb would. Near-miss: Not a claim that the form looks different from an ordinary perfect participle; the form still looks passive. The translation is active because the verb is deponent. Not a first look at first- or second-person deponents, which remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A sound sentence will keep both words ablative and an active translation. Sample: Hīs locūtīs, magister tacet, “with these things having been said, the schoolmaster is silent.”
  2. The first is contemporaneous (present). The second is already complete (perfect). Both are active in meaning.
  3. Those forms have not been taught.
  4. With the city having been captured, the farmers flee. Capiō is not deponent; captā is an ordinary perfect participle, passive in meaning.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of dormir, o → u in the third person

Check. 1. Dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron. Only durmió and durmieron change o to u. 2. Duermió uses the present ue in a preterite form. Dirmió uses pedir’s i on the wrong verb. 3. Sample: Anoche dormí bien. Ellos durmieron temprano. 4. Both -ir verbs change the stem vowel only in the two third-person preterite forms. Pedir goes e to i; dormir goes o to u. 5. Model: preterite of dormir. Own words should match this idea: Dormir, already taught in the present with the o → ue stem change, has a preterite in which the stem vowel becomes u only in the third persons: dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron. The other four forms keep o. The pattern is the same shape as yesterday’s pedir, but the vowels are o and u, not e and i. Near-miss: Not a first look at the present of dormir (o → ue), already taught. Not a copy of pedir’s e → i vowels onto this verb. Not a first look at the regular -ir preterite endings. Instruction remains in English. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
