Latin · Friday mix: the future perfect of sequor and ēgredior, with already-taught deponent tenses

Check. 1. Sequor, I follow; sequar, I shall follow; secūtus sum, I followed, or I have followed; secūtus eram, I had followed; secūtus erō, I shall have followed. 2. He will have gone out, and then we shall have followed him. 3. Ēgressus est is the perfect, already completed in the past, not a later completion.

Exercises. 1. A fair pair keeps Puer or Puella and the right tense, with he or she in the gloss: for example, Puer mē secūtus erit; Puella ēgrediētur. 2. Secūtus eram is the pluperfect, I had followed, already completed before another past time. 3. Later deponent tenses not yet taught remain reserved.

Mathematics · Friday mix: the ambiguous case, no, one, or two triangles

Check. 1. When A is acute, compare a with h = b sin A: no triangle if a < h; one right triangle if a = h; two triangles if h < a < b; one triangle if a ≥ b. When A is obtuse, no triangle if a ≤ b, and one triangle if a > b. 2. None; one right triangle; two triangles, 30°-45°-105° and 30°-135°-15°; one isosceles triangle, 30°-30°-120°. 3. If a = 2, no triangle. If a = 2√3, one triangle, 120°-30°-30°.

Exercises. 1. sin B = 2 × (1/2) / √2 = 1/√2, so B is 45° or 135°, and the triangles are 30°-45°-105° and 30°-135°-15°. 2. Because a < b, the obtuse B also occurs. When A is already obtuse, a second obtuse angle cannot stand in the same triangle, so B is 30°, not 150°. 3. Do not invent official measured sides.

Writing · Friday mix: a confirmation distinguished from a refutation, a maxim, a chreia, a narrative, and a fable

Check. 1. A confirmation shows why a claim holds. A refutation shows why a claim does not hold. 2. “If two sides of a triangle are equal, the angles opposite those sides are equal.” Equal sides make an isosceles triangle, and the already-taught base angles, opposite those sides, are equal. 3. A maxim is a short precept. A chreia needs a person, an occasion, and a saying or act. A narrative tells who, what, when, and where, in order, without a stated moral. A fable is a short story with a moral, not a history. None of those is a reasoned defense of a stated claim.

Exercises. 1. A fair confirmation of the radian claim recalls that s = rθ uses θ in radians, and that a degree measure in place of θ was a miss. Choosing the isosceles claim is also fair, if the reason is the already-taught base angles. 2. The reasons are already-taught methods, not a person’s fame. 3. A longer later elaboration remains reserved. No official Aphthonius or Heath wording is claimed.

Physics · Friday mix: closed and open air columns, and official Example 17.5

Check. 1. Closed: f_n = n v_w / (4L), n = 1, 3, 5, …. Open: f_n = n v_w / (2L), n = 1, 2, 3, …. 2. v_w = 344 m/s, L = 0.672 m, and the fourth overtone is 1.15 kHz. 3. Closed: 344 / 4 = 86.0 Hz. Open: 344 / 2 = 172 Hz. The length 1.00 m is invented, so those frequencies are invented checks, not official example values. 4. Model: resonance in an open air column. Own words should match this idea: In OpenStax 17.5, a tube open at both ends has antinodes of air displacement at both ends. The resonant frequencies are f_n = n v_w / (2L), with n = 1, 2, 3, …. The fundamental is twice the fundamental of a closed tube of the same length. Official instrument lengths are not invented. Near-miss: Not a closed tube, whose odd-n series was already taught. Not official Example 17.5 reused as if it were an open tube. Not an official length of a flute or organ pipe. Not a copyrighted score or an official concert pitch. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. They have different overtones, as the book notes, even if the fundamentals match. 2. 340 m/s is the official speed in a different example, 17.4, not in Example 17.5. 3. Official instrument lengths, official concert pitches, and later optics remain reserved.

History · Friday mix: 107 leagues along Juana, 188 leagues on one side of Española, and Villa de Navidad

Check. 1. “One hundred and seven leagues” along Juana, west to east. “Larger than England and Scotland together,” the letter’s comparison, not an official modern area. 2. “One hundred and eighty-eight great leagues” on one of its four sides, west to east, in the letter’s body. The footnote “188” was the editor’s note on a different sentence, the earlier 178, and was not taught as official. 3. He says he gave a town the name “Villa de Navidad,” in a place “well adapted for the working of the gold mines.” Do not invent official gold weights, official areas, official identifications, or conversions of the leagues.

Exercises. 1. A fair quotation keeps “larger than England and Scotland together,” or “a greater circumference than all Spain, from Catalonia by the sea-coast to Fuenterabia in Biscay.” 2. The letter is a voyage report, earlier than the June 7, 1494 treaty. 3. Later parts of the letter, official modern identifications, official areas, official gold quantities, and conversions remain reserved.

Atelier · Friday mix: two objects, the scale, the interval, and reflected light

Check. 1. Two simple forms, in the four paints, under one light, value first. The scale shows whether the two lights and the two shadows agree, and whether a bounce is as light as the true light. 2. If the interval is left unpainted or as bright as the lights, the objects float or the study splits. Bounces stay quieter steps in the shadow, checked on the scale. 3. Not a portrait, and not a fifth tube. Cinnabar, azurite, malachite, and ultramarine stay out of the box.

Exercises. 1. A fair paragraph names two simple objects, a cloth, an interval, a short scale, and a quieter bounce, all under one light, still in the four paints. 2. Reflected light is still the bounce in the shadow, now on more than one form and in the interval. The mix also includes the second object, the scale, and the painted gap. 3. A portrait, a fifth tube, and a master copy in color remain reserved.

Modern language · Spanish Friday mix: haya with sea, vaya, esté, regular subjunctives, the future, and the conditional

Check. 1. Haber: haya, hayas, haya, hayamos, hayáis, hayan. Ser: sea, seas, sea, seamos, seáis, sean. Ir: vaya, vayas, vaya, vayamos, vayáis, vayan. Estar: esté, estés, esté, estemos, estéis, estén. 2. Haya. Vaya. 3. Haber as a helping verb has not yet been taught as a first look.

Exercises. 1. Fair invented examples: “Espero que haya silencio.” “Quiere que sea puntual.” “Mañana iremos.” 2. Hay is the present indicative of haber; the subjunctive in that frame is haya. Esté is estar, not haber. 3. Haber as a helping verb, and other irregular subjunctives not yet taught, remain reserved.

Seminar · Anatomy: the first thoracic vertebra, from Gray 1918, a first look

Check. 1. “An entire articular facet for the head of the first rib, and a demi-facet for the upper half of the head of the second rib.” “The body is like that of a cervical vertebra, being broad transversely.” 2. The spinous process is “thick, long, and almost horizontal.” The transverse processes “are long,” and the upper vertebral notches “are deeper than those of the other thoracic vertebræ.” 3. The lesson is artistic anatomy from a public-domain text, not a medical diagnosis, and no body is being described from life. Official lengths are not invented. T9–T12, and the lumbar vertebrae, remain reserved. 4. Model: the first thoracic vertebra (Gray, 1918). Own words should match this idea: In Gray’s 1918 Anatomy, via Theodora, the first thoracic vertebra “has, on either side of the body, an entire articular facet for the head of the first rib, and a demi-facet for the upper half of the head of the second rib.” “The body is like that of a cervical vertebra, being broad transversely.” Official lengths are not invented, and the lesson is not a diagnosis. Near-miss: Not last week’s typical thoracic vertebra as if it had no peculiarities. Not vertebra prominens, already taught as the seventh cervical. Not T9–T12, which remain reserved. Not an official length, and not a diagnosis of a living person. Not a body being described from life. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Last week’s middle thoracic bodies were “heart-shaped.” Today’s first thoracic body is “like that of a cervical vertebra, being broad transversely,” and has the entire facet and demi-facet Gray names. 2. Vertebra prominens is the seventh cervical vertebra, marked by a long, not bifurcated, spinous process, already taught. Today’s first look is the first thoracic vertebra, marked by its rib facets. 3. Official lengths, a diagnosis, a body from life, T9–T12 in detail, and the lumbar vertebrae remain reserved.
