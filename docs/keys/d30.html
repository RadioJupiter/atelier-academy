History · Two-week synthesis: how we know the Roman Republic

Check. 1. Livy: late narrative for early events. Tables: later fragments, not a complete original. Polybius: second-century analysis. Caesar: participant and advocacy. 2. 146 BCE is a standard date for a documented end of a war and of a city in a better-sourced period; 753 BCE is a traditional founding year for a thinly documented early city. 3. Model: how we know the Republic. Own words should match this idea: A synthesis question: early Rome and early law are known through later tradition, archaeology, and fragments; the mid-Republic can be analyzed with writers such as Polybius; the late Republic speaks in participant sources that are also advocacy. The same habit of dating and classifying sources runs through all three. Near-miss: Not a claim that we know nothing, and not a claim that we know the early city as clearly as the 50s BCE. The quality of the evidence changes, and the wording should change with it. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Settlement and early public space, sometimes; not, by itself, every named story of a king.
  2. Because a Rome-centered literary record can quietly drop the other side; a synthesis should not pretend to a balance of archives we do not have.
  3. For example: even a careful source-critic still depends on what happened to survive, and large parts of the record are gone.
  4. The biographer is later and not a participant; the commentary is primary and also a defense of the author’s conduct.
  5. A short, accurate paragraph with all three bands and the habit named, no new invented facts.

Elective · Poetry 3: imagery and metaphor, with a public-domain poem

Check. 1. For example, “fearful symmetry” might be described as a compressed image of a patterned, fearsome form; “What immortal hand or eye” is a question about a maker, not yet itself a complete metaphor. Other honest labels of a short quoted phrase are acceptable. 2. It can be literal imagery (a dark forest) without speaking of the forest as if it were something else. A metaphor would carry another domain across (for example, treating the forest as a building or a body), which this clause does not yet do. 3. Model: imagery. Own words should match this idea: Language that appeals to the senses — seeing, hearing, touch, and the rest — so a reader can picture or otherwise sense a scene or an object. Imagery can be literal description, and it can also support a metaphor; the two are not the same word. Near-miss: Not only visual pictures (sound and texture count), and not a list of every adjective in a poem as if each were a finished image. metaphor. Own words should match this idea: A comparison made by speaking of one thing as if it were another, without a comparing word such as like or as (those usually mark a simile). A metaphor carries a description or a name across from one domain to another. Near-miss: Not a simile (which still names the comparison with like or as), and not every strong adjective. “The forest is dark” can be imagery without being a metaphor. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Two original sentences, clearly different: one sensory and literal, one carrying a description across.
  2. It shows whose words they are, and that the text is public domain (1794), not a later copyrighted commentary.
  3. Imagery is sensory language; metaphor is a carrying-across of name or description. A line can offer both when a sensory phrase also treats one thing as another.
  4. A simile that matches the metaphor’s idea but uses like or as.
  5. A short filed note: quotation, attribution, label, and a sentence on the job of the language.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. S = n/2 · (first + last), or n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d). Pairing first with last makes each pair sum to a + ℓ, so 2S = n(a + ℓ). The formula is that counting, not a separate trick. 2. ille, illa, illud is the far demonstrative, “that.” hic, haec, hoc is the near one, “this.” They are different pointing words; the adverb hīc (“here”) is a third, long-ī form. 3. 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2. It is a net summary, not a one-step mechanism, and C6H12O6 is a representative carbohydrate. 4. With verbs such as cōnstāre, the price is ablative without a preposition (decem sēstertiīs). When the price is the predicate of pretium est, it is nominative. 5. A solvent carries the pigments up the paper at different rates because they cling to the paper and dissolve in the solvent differently, so they form colored bands. Rf values depend on the exact setup; this course will not invent them. 6. Money is taught as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. A numbered paper that cannot do those jobs (or a token in a game that is not exchangeable) is not money in this working sense, even if it looks like a bill. 7. nostos is a homecoming or return. Books 9–12 are earlier wanderings narrated by Odysseus after we have already met him at Calypso’s and among the Phaeacians, so the earlier years arrive as a story inside the story. 8. Any two careful cautions: Livy is late for the kings; Polybius is a second-century analysis, not a transcript of 509 BCE; the Twelve Tables survive as later fragments; Caesar writes as a commander about his own campaigns (primary and advocacy); a Doryphoros in marble is a later copy.
