Latin · Capitulum VII, part 3: reflexive sē, versus eum and eam

Check. 1. Puerī sē lavant. 2. Mārcus eum videt. eum points to a different male; sē would still be Marcus himself. 3. Model: reflexive sē. Own words should match this idea: The pronoun sē (accusative; also used as ablative) refers back to the subject of its own clause: the subject acts on himself, herself, or themselves. Near-miss: sē is not eum or eam. Mārcus eum videt means Marcus sees him — some other male already in view, not Marcus himself. eum / eam. Own words should match this idea: The accusative of is / ea: ‘him’ or ‘her’, referring to someone other than the subject (in the ordinary case taught here). Near-miss: Not a reflexive. If Marcus hears himself, the form is sē, not eum. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Julia sees herself in the water.
  2. Puer sē lavat.
  3. For example: Iūlia sē videt. Mārcus eam videt.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. The one form sē covers himself, herself, and themselves; the verb (videt / vident) and the subject tell you who is meant.

Mathematics · Geometry XII: cyclic quadrilaterals

Check. 1. ∠C = 92°; ∠B = 80°. 2. No. Opposite angles are 60° and 60°, and 60° + 60° = 120°, not 180°. (The 60°–120° pair are adjacent, not opposite, in a parallelogram.) 3. Model: cyclic quadrilateral. Own words should match this idea: A quadrilateral whose four vertices all lie on a single circle. It is inscribed in that circle. Near-miss: Not every quadrilateral is cyclic. A square is; a non-rectangular parallelogram, in general, is not (its opposite angles are equal, but they need not be supplementary). opposite angles supplementary. Own words should match this idea: In a cyclic quadrilateral, each pair of opposite angles sums to 180°. That is the usable form of Euclid III.22 in this course. Near-miss: Adjacent angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are not forced to be supplementary by this theorem (they may or may not be). Do not apply ‘interior angles on the same side’ from parallels unless you have parallels. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 2x + (x + 30°) = 180°, so 3x = 150°, x = 50°. ∠A = 100°, ∠C = 80°.
  2. Every angle of a rectangle is 90°, so opposite angles are 90° + 90° = 180°, which is what III.22 requires. (The angle in a semicircle also shows each right angle sits on a diameter — a diagonal.)
  3. No: opposite 70° + 70° = 140° ≠ 180°. (A square, which is a rhombus with right angles, is cyclic.)
  4. ∠C = 90°. Then ABCD has two opposite right angles. If one of them is inscribed in a semicircle on a diagonal, that diagonal is a diameter; you may say that only when the right angle is the angle on that diagonal as hypotenuse. Here both A and C are right, so both diagonals can serve as diameters — consistent with a rectangle, but this item alone does not force the other two angles to be 90° unless more is given. (If only A and C are known to be 90°, the figure can still be cyclic, by III.22, without yet being a rectangle.)
  5. Opposite angles: A+C = 80+100=180, B+D=70+110=180. Yes, the angle test is satisfied (the course treats this as the usable test from III.22).

Writing & Rhetoric · Participial phrases, and dangling modifiers

Check. 1. No: the implied object of “given” is the geometric situation, and the sentence is in the official style of a proposition (“given…, [a result]”). If you prefer a stricter school rule, rewrite as “When AB is a diameter, the angle at C is 90°.” Either explanation is acceptable if it is consistent. 2. For example: Inscribed in a single circle, the cyclic quadrilateral has opposite angles that sum to 180°. 3. Model: participial phrase. Own words should match this idea: A phrase headed by a participle (often an -ing or a past form used as a modifier) that adds a circumstance or a description to a noun or to a clause, without itself being the main verb. Near-miss: Not a finite clause (which has a subject doing a tensed verb: “The angle stands on the same arc”). And not a second independent sentence spliced with a comma. dangling modifier. Own words should match this idea: A participial phrase that is not clearly attached to the noun it is meant to describe, so the sentence seems to say the wrong thing. Near-miss: Not every opening -ing phrase is a mistake — only those whose implied subject is not the subject (or the noun) that follows. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The sum is not doing the pairing. Repair: Pairing the first term with the last, we can see the sum of the AP. Or: The sum of the AP is easy to see once the first term is paired with the last.
  2. Two sentences, one participial and one finite, on the same idea; the participle must attach to the right noun.
  3. A comma splice joins two independent clauses illegally; it does not attach a modifier to a noun. The repair is attachment or subordination, not a second main clause jammed on with a comma.
  4. For example: Going into the garden, the boy then works there. Check: the boy is the one going. (Better: The boy, going into the garden, works there — if the working and the going are meant to combine. If they are two times, a when clause is clearer.)
  5. A dangling example plus a repair, with sē clearly attached to the subject who acts on himself or herself.

Physics · Kinetic energy, and the work–energy theorem

Check. 1. ½ × 4 × v² = 18, so 2v² = 18, v² = 9, v = 3 m/s. (Speed is positive.) 2. ΔKE = 0 − 25 = −25 J, so W_net = −25 J. 3. Model: kinetic energy. Own words should match this idea: The energy an object has because it is moving. For a single object treated as a particle, KE = ½ m v², in joules when m is in kilograms and v is in meters per second. Near-miss: Not the same as momentum (p = m v). Momentum is a vector and is linear in v; kinetic energy is a scalar and depends on v². A sign on v is squared away: speed is what enters KE. work–energy theorem. Own words should match this idea: The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy: Wnet = ΔKE = KEfinal − KEinitial. This is the usable form in this course for a single particle, or an object that can be treated as one. Near-miss: Not “all forces always increase speed.” Negative net work decreases KE. And the theorem uses net work: the sum of work by every force, or the work of the net force along the path. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ½ × 6 × 4 = 12 J.
  2. KE_i = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 J; KE_f = ½ × 3 × 16 = 24 J; ΔKE = 18 J = W_net.
  3. KE = ½ m v² is the same, and m is twice, so v² is half: the heavier object’s speed is 1/√2 times the lighter object’s speed (or v_heavy = v_light / √2).
  4. v = p/m = 6/2 = 3 m/s; KE = ½ × 2 × 9 = 9 J.
  5. W_net = 0, so ΔKE = 0, so the speed does not change. That matches Day 16: uniform circular motion has constant speed and a perpendicular force.

History · The Republic: consuls, Senate, and Polybius as a source

Check. 1. A council of leading Romans with great weight in policy, finance, and foreign affairs; not a modern elected legislature, and not the same as a popular assembly. 2. Like 753 BCE, it is a traditional bookend from later narrative, not a year this course can treat as a contemporary record. 3. Model: Roman Republic. Own words should match this idea: The period of Roman government, after the traditional end of the kingship, in which annually elected magistrates (including two consuls) and assemblies, together with the Senate, shared the public business of the city and, later, of an empire. The traditional beginning is the late sixth century BCE; the Republic ends in the first century BCE with the rise of one-man rule. Those bookends are useful labels, not a claim that every year is equally well documented. Near-miss: Not a modern electoral democracy, and not the same as the later rule of emperors. “Republic” here translates the Romans’ rēs pūblica — the public thing — not a later party name. consul. Own words should match this idea: One of the two chief annual magistrates of the Roman Republic. The pair of consuls is a central fact in the tradition and in later Republican practice; the consulship is not a lifelong kingship. Near-miss: Not a senator as such (a consul was typically a senator, but the offices are different), and not an emperor. Senate. Own words should match this idea: The council of leading Romans that advised magistrates and, in Republican practice, carried great weight in policy, finance, and foreign affairs. It was not a modern elected legislature. Near-miss: Not the same as an assembly of the people, and not a king’s private household. The name is connected with senex, ‘old’: a council of elders in origin, at least in the word. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Second century BCE; to explain Rome’s rise (to a Greek audience), including an analysis of its government.
  2. Likeness: both are literary sources later readers depend on. Difference: Polybius is closer in time to the mid-Republic he analyzes than Livy is to the early kings; Livy’s early books are more deeply a tradition about a distant past.
  3. Romans often named a year by its two consuls, which also reminds us that the office was annual and paired.
  4. Magistrates, Senate, and people each hold a share of the public business, in Polybius’s analysis — not a single kind of rule.
  5. Any caution that asks which century the map describes, and whether it is Polybius’s model, a later handbook, or a picture of the early tradition.

Modern language · Spanish: indirect-object pronouns

Check. 1. Te doy un lápiz. 2. Nos sirven la comida. 3. Model: indirect-object pronoun. Own words should match this idea: A small word that stands in for the person to whom or for whom something is given, said, or shown: me, te, le, nos, os, les. In a simple sentence it goes immediately before the conjugated verb: Le doy el libro. With an infinitive or a present participle it may also attach: Voy a darle el libro / Le voy a dar el libro; Estoy dándole el libro / Le estoy dando el libro. Near-miss: Not a direct-object pronoun (lo, la, los, las), which stands in for the thing given. “I give it to her” will later need both; today, keep the thing as a noun and practice the person as a pronoun: Le doy el libro. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. me, te, le, nos, os, les.
  2. For example: Te pido agua. Les doy los libros.
  3. The pronoun is required in this pattern; the a + name specifies or emphasizes who le refers to. It is not a substitution that lets you drop le in the pattern taught today.
  4. Lo veo: lo is a direct object (the thing or person seen, already taught as a DOP). Le doy el libro: le is the recipient; el libro is the direct object still named as a noun.
  5. Someone (le) is asked; a book is what is asked for. (The asker is “I,” pido.)

Art history · High Classical Greece: the Parthenon and the Doryphoros

Check. 1. You are describing a later version, in a different material, not the lost bronze itself. Stance and design may still be studied; “this surface was carved in the fifth century” may be false. 2. About 447–432 BCE, said as a conventional scholarly dating, not as a measurement this course performed. 3. Model: Parthenon. Own words should match this idea: The temple of Athena on the Acropolis in Athens, built in the mid-fifth century BCE (a conventional dating is about 447–432 BCE). In this course it is the type example of a High Classical Greek Doric temple, studied from photographs and plans, not as a building you are assumed to have stood in. Near-miss: Not a complete “as-new” object in the fifth century’s own photograph — we have later damage, removals, and restorations. And not a generic word for every Greek temple. Doryphoros (Polykleitos). Own words should match this idea: The “Spear-Bearer,” a bronze athlete statue designed by Polykleitos in the fifth century BCE, known mainly from later marble copies. It is a standard example of High Classical contrapposto and of a written canon of proportions that does not survive as a complete treatise in Polykleitos’s own words. Near-miss: Not an original bronze you can simply photograph in a fifth-century state, and not a proof that every Classical statue followed one surviving table of numbers. The canon is reported; the complete text is lost. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. For example: fluted Doric columns, a simple capital, or a frieze of triglyphs and metopes.
  2. That Polykleitos was reported to have written a canon of proportions, and that the complete treatise does not survive in his own words; the statue type is evidence of a design, not a printed table of numbers we can quote from him.
  3. Kouros: even weight, one foot forward, Archaic. Kritios Boy: early contrapposto, early fifth century, with a traditional attribution. Doryphoros: a High Classical development of that stance, known through copies.
  4. Because the removal is a real later history that changes what the building holds, and because a slogan would replace looking and dating with a ready-made judgment this lesson has not argued through.
  5. A caption that says “marble copy of a fifth-century BCE bronze type associated with Polykleitos,” and that points to the weight leg or the turn of the head.
