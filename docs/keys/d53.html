Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: is, ea, eum, and eam

Check. 1. Ea rosam habet. 2. Puella eum videt. 3. Model: is, ea, eum, and eam. Own words should match this idea: Third-person pronouns already taught in Capitula I–X (Day 7): is and ea are nominative (he, she); eum and eam are accusative (him, her). Reviewed today, not as a new chapter. Near-miss: Not the same as the interrogatives quis and quem, and not a new pronoun set from a later chapter. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Puella eum videt (eum, not is).
  2. Both heard and used.
  3. quis asks “who?”; eum points back to a male person already named, as an object.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Algebra XVIII: a downward-opening parabola, and a square

Check. 1. 2 < x < 8. 2. No real solution. 3. Model: a downward-opening quadratic inequality. Own words should match this idea: When the x² coefficient is negative, the already-taught parabola opens downward, so the regions swap: y > 0 between the roots, and y < 0 outside them. Near-miss: Not a change of method: we still read the graph. We do not flip inequality signs by a memorized rule that forgets the shape. a perfect-square inequality. Own words should match this idea: A quadratic that is a square, such as (x − 3)², is never negative. (x − 3)² ≥ 0 for all real x, and (x − 3)² = 0 only at x = 3. (x − 3)² < 0 has no real solution. Near-miss: Not a claim that every quadratic is a square, and not a claim about complex numbers, which this course has not begun. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. All real x. (A square is always ≥ 0.)
  2. 2 ≤ x ≤ 8.
  3. A square of a real number is never negative. We are not beginning complex numbers in this lesson.
  4. The minus flips the shape: that quadratic opens downward, so it is positive between the roots, which is the opposite of the upward-opening product (x − 1)(x − 5) > 0 (that one is positive outside the roots).

Physics · Weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass

Check. 1. 37 N on Mars; 16 N on the Moon. Invented mass. 2. Mass is the same on either body. 19/50 (about 0.38) is the comparison of the g values, and so of the weights, not a shrinking of the mass. 3. Model: weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass. Own words should match this idea: Weight as the already-taught W = m g, using today’s Mars g ≈ 3.7 m/s² and an invented classroom mass that stays labeled invented. The mass is not a NASA figure. Near-miss: Not a change of mass with the planet, and not an invented person or identity. Mass stays the same; weight changes because g changes. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 49 N on Earth; 18.5 N on Mars. Invented mass.
  2. The 50 kg was an invented classroom mass, not a sourced astronaut’s mass, and we will not invent a person for it.
  3. About 0.38. A weight on Mars is about 0.38 of the same mass’s weight on Earth. The mass itself is unchanged.
  4. A note that no orbital speed was invented.

History · The pine-tree cloud, and what the set extract does not yet give

Check. 1. A checked copy of Firth’s pine-tree wording, attributed to Pliny the Younger to Tacitus, trans. Firth (1900). 2. The identification as Vesuvius was not the first thing people at a distance could make out, as the letter tells it. 3. Model: the pine-tree cloud. Own words should match this idea: In Firth’s translation of Pliny’s letter, the unusual cloud is at first not clearly tied to one mountain; it is afterwards discovered to have been Vesuvius, and in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, with a long, high trunk and a spread of branches. Near-miss: Not a drawing, not a later oil painting of the eruption, and not a licence to invent a measured height in meters for the cloud. eruption, as a later name. Own words should match this idea: A later English name for the event the letter describes. The set extract itself describes an unusual cloud, afterwards tied to Vesuvius; we may say “eruption” as a conventional event-name, labeled as such, without inventing a modern scientific log the letter does not give. Near-miss: Not a measured column height, not a modern volcanic classification invented in this lesson, and not a death toll. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The voyage, Rectina, Stabiae, and the uncle’s death, unless a later lesson sets them.
  2. It is a conventional event-name. It is not a measured scientific log, and it does not add figures the letter does not give.
  3. A painting is a later picture, with its own date and maker, not Firth’s sentences about a cloud.
  4. A note that no death toll was invented.

Under one lamp the two shadows lean the same way, meet the forms at a consistent place, and are not as dark as an invented hole in the paper. Near-miss: Not the form’s own dark plane, not a second lamp’s shadow, and not a traced outline left as the finished shadow.   4. A note that the sitting is not a reward.

Modern language · Spanish: antes de and después de + infinitive

Check. 1. Antes de escribir, lee. 2. Después de escribir, lee. 3. Model: antes de and después de, with an infinitive. Own words should match this idea: Two present-tense time phrases: antes de + infinitive (before doing something) and después de + infinitive (after doing something). Instruction stays in English. They do not, today, begin a past-tense clause with a conjugated verb after de. Near-miss: Not a past-tense lesson, and not antes de que (a later pattern this course has not taught, and one that would pull in a new mood). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Antes de leer (infinitive, not lee).
  2. Both heard and used with an infinitive.
  3. That pattern would pull in a new clause-type, and often a new mood, which this course has not taught. Today the infinitive after de is enough.
  4. Two copied model sentences.

Art history · The Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum), from Platner

Check. 1. The stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea (Platner). 2. The games are taught as Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among other authorities Platner lists. This course does not pretend to have read Suetonius independently this sitting. 3. Model: the Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum). Own words should match this idea: The amphitheater ordinarily known as the Colosseum, in Platner and Ashby’s entry Amphitheatrum Flavium (1929): built by Vespasian on the site of the stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea; carried to the second arcade and dedicated before Vespasian’s death in 79; Titus added the third and fourth stories and celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with games that lasted one hundred days. A later building, not a photograph of 79. Near-miss: Not a finished monument of 79 in the form later seen, and not a claim that this course has independently read Suetonius. The hundred-day games are taught as Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7 (and other authorities Platner lists). Not a death toll, and not a picture of the pine-tree cloud. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Last week’s seminar was the Arch of Titus, a later commemoration of a siege, with a nineteenth-century rebuild. Today is the amphitheater from the same dictionary, with its own stages.
  2. A capacity was not quoted from Platner here, so it is not invented. The entry is a topographical source, not a caption.
  3. A sound sentence: Vespasian dedicated work before his death in 79, but Titus added stories and Platner places the enlarged dedication in 80, so a single year 79 does not describe the later building.
  4. A note that the seminar is not a reward.
