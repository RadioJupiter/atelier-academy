Latin · Capitulum IX, part 2: the accusative of the new family

Check. 1. Canis rēgem videt. 2. Puella vōcem rēgis audit. 3. Model: third-declension accusative. Own words should match this idea: For many third-declension nouns, the accusative singular adds -em to the stem (the stem is seen in the genitive: rēg- from rēgis, so rēgem; vōc- from vōcis, so vōcem). Near-miss: Not the genitive rēgis, and not a first-declension -am (that is puellam). Do not write *rēxam or *canis as the object if the sentence needs “the dog” as object. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. vōcem (stem vōc-); canem (stem can-).
  2. canis would be nominative; the object should be canem. Word order may vary, but the endings mark the jobs.
  3. Clear reading.
  4. rēgem object; rēgis “of the king.”
  5. A marked original sentence.

Mathematics · Algebra IX: graphs of linear functions

Check. 1. f(0) = −1, f(2) = 5; m = 3, b = −1. 2. Slope (10−2)/(4−0) = 8/4 = 2. A matching rule is f(x) = 2x + 2. 3. Model: linear function. Own words should match this idea: A function whose graph is a straight line. In the form f(x) = mx + b, b is the y-intercept (the output when x = 0), and m is the slope (the rise over the run between any two points on the line). Near-miss: Not every graph is a line (tomorrow’s parabola is not). And m is not automatically the y-intercept; that job belongs to b in this form. slope. Own words should match this idea: The constant rate of change of a linear function: rise over run, (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), between any two points on the line. Near-miss: Not the intercept, and not a turn in degrees (that was an angle). A vertical line would have an undefined slope; we do not need that case today. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. m = 1, b = 0; a line through the origin at equal rise and run.
  2. Recompute: f(1) = 3, not 4. Do not bend the line to visit a wrong point.
  3. Slope is rise over run, a rate on the graph, not a turn. Degrees belong to angles.
  4. For (0, 4) and (2, 2): (2−4)/(2−0) = −2/2 = −1.
  5. A clear table, a straight sketch, and m and b named.

Near-miss: Not copying the sentence and changing one noun, and not a paraphrase presented as your own without attribution of the model. The quotation itself stays short and named.   4. The rest of the essay is not today’s model.

Science · How we see: the eye as a kind of camera

Check. 1. The clear front; from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn,” for a tough tissue, not because the eye is horn. 2. The light-sensitive layer at the back; from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.” 3. Model: cornea. Own words should match this idea: The clear front surface of the eye, which begins the bending of incoming light. The name comes from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn,” for a tough, horn-like tissue — not because the eye is made of horn. Near-miss: Not the colored iris, and not the retina at the back. The cornea is at the front, clear, and in contact with the outside air (under the tear film). retina. Own words should match this idea: The light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, where an image is formed and a signal begins. The name comes from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.” Near-miss: Not the pupil (an opening), and not the optic nerve itself (the path of the signal leaving the eye — a first name today, treated more carefully on Thursday). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The opening in the iris through which light passes after the cornea.
  2. This lesson has no optical-bench measurement to report; a number would be an invention.
  3. Likeness: a lens-like front and a receiving surface. Unlikeness: living tissue, no shutter, no manufactured film.
  4. The retina.
  5. A short paragraph that keeps the order cornea–pupil–lens–retina.

Modern language · Spanish: the present of ir, dar, ver, and hacer

Check. 1. Vas, das, ves, haces. 2. For example: Vamos a practicar (near future). Vamos a la clase (we go to class). Labels required. 3. Model: present of ir, dar, ver, hacer. Own words should match this idea: Four high-frequency irregular presents, taught as a set today: ir (voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van); dar (doy, das, da, damos, dais, dan); ver (veo, ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven); hacer (hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen).   3. Two heard forms, each in a correct new sentence.
  4. A short, correct four-sentence string.

Music theory · Intervals: the perfect fifth and the major third

Check. 1. G–B major third; G–D perfect fifth — or the C-major pair named again in words, if G major is not yet firm. 2. An interval is a distance between two notes; I, IV, and V are chords built on degrees of the scale. Related, not the same word. 3. Model: interval. Own words should match this idea: The distance in pitch between two notes. The word comes from Latin intervallum (a space between, from inter, “between,” and vallum, “rampart”). In this lesson an interval is named by a number (a third, a fifth) counted along the notes of a scale, inclusive of both ends. Near-miss: Not the everyday sense of a break in time, and not a claim that every fifth is the same size in every tuning system. Today we name two intervals in a major scale as this course has used it. perfect fifth and major third (first look). Own words should match this idea: In a major scale, the interval from the first degree to the fifth is a perfect fifth (for example, C up to G). The interval from the first degree to the third is a major third (C up to E). These are first names; other qualities (minor, diminished, augmented) are not taught as a full system today. Near-miss: Not “any two notes five letter-names apart are always a perfect fifth in every context,” if an accidental has changed the size — a caution named, not a full accidentals lesson. In the unaltered major scale taught here, 1-to-5 is a perfect fifth and 1-to-3 is a major third. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. F–A is a major third (F, G, A); F–C is a perfect fifth (F, G, A, B♭, C) in F major — or a clearly labeled choice to stay in C major, with C–E and C–G.
  2. No full quality table, and no invented frequency proof.
  3. The closer step (major third) and the wider one (perfect fifth) correctly labeled.
  4. Here it is a distance in pitch, from Latin intervallum as taught, not a lunch break.
  5. A complete copied reminder.
