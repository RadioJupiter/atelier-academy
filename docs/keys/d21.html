Latin · Capitulum VII, part 1: the dative, ‘to’ and ‘for’

Check. 1. Mārcus servīs aquam dat. 2. Mārcō is dative (to Marcus). Mārcum would make Marcus the thing given, which is not the meaning. 3. Model: dative. Own words should match this idea: The case that typically marks the person or thing to whom or for whom something is given, shown, or said. In the first and second declensions the singular endings are -ae (first) and -ō (second); the plural for both is -īs. Near-miss: The dative is not the thing given (that is usually accusative), and it is not ‘from’ (ablative). Mārcum and Mārcō are different jobs. indirect object. Own words should match this idea: The person or thing that receives the direct object, or for whom the action is done. In this lesson it is expressed by the dative. Near-miss: Not the direct object (the rose / rosam), and not the subject (Iūlia). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. puellae; servō.
  2. Iūlia Mārcō rosam dat.
  3. For example, Puer Iūliae rosam dat.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. The form is the same; the job is not. Here Julia is the one to whom the rose is given, so Iūliae is dative, not ‘Julia’s rose’ as a genitive tag on a noun.

Mathematics · Geometry X: central and inscribed angles

Check. 1. 32°. 2. 70°. 3. Model: central angle. Own words should match this idea: An angle whose vertex is the center of a circle, and whose sides pass through two points of the circle. It stands on the arc between those two points. Near-miss: Not an inscribed angle, whose vertex lies on the circle, not at the center. inscribed angle. Own words should match this idea: An angle whose vertex lies on the circle, and whose sides are chords of the circle. It stands on the arc between its two endpoints (the arc not containing the vertex). Near-miss: Not a central angle. The vertex is on the circumference, not at the center. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 45°.
  2. 96°.
  3. No. If D is on arc AB, then ∠ADB does not stand on arc AB; the ‘same arc’ condition fails. (Tomorrow treats related cases more carefully.)
  4. 75°. 150° would be the central angle, twice the inscribed one.
  5. They are equal: each is half of the same central angle, so they equal each other.

Writing & Rhetoric · Parallelism in a series

Check. 1. No. Two nouns and then an infinitive. Repair: Clear thinking, careful evidence, and a real concession. (Or: to think clearly, to weigh evidence, and to concede a limit.) 2. to dissolve… and to assume… Own pair: for example, to define the term and to give a near-miss. 3. Model: parallelism. Own words should match this idea: A series of words, phrases, or clauses written in the same grammatical shape, so the parts can be heard as a matched set. Near-miss: Not mere repetition of a word, and not a list whose third item changes construction (to plan, to draft, and revising). coordinated series. Own words should match this idea: Two or more elements joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, or, but) or by punctuation that does the same job, which must be parallel if they share a single grammatical slot. Near-miss: Subordination (because, although, who) is a different join; it does not have to repeat the same shape, but a series inside a subordinate clause still does. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. He is responsible for gathering sources, for drafting a précis, and for proofreading the typography.
  2. For example: The first paragraph states the claim, the second paragraph weighs a source, and the last sentence returns to the claim in new words.
  3. The first two parts are clauses with finite verbs (came, saw); the third is a participle phrase. The slot is not held in one shape.
  4. No. That is the main clause of the sentence, after the long “When…” opener. The infinitive series lives inside the opener, not in the main verb “requires.”
  5. Any clearly labeled broken series plus a repair that puts every part in the same shape.

Physics · Work done by a constant force

Check. 1. W = 5 × 4 × cos180° = 20 × (−1) = −20 J. 2. Zero. The displacement is zero, so W = 0, however tired the arms feel. 3. Model: work. Own words should match this idea: In this course, a force does work on an object when the object is displaced and the force has a component along that displacement. When the force is constant, W = F s cosθ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. If the force is along the displacement, θ = 0° and W = F s. Near-miss: Not ‘effort’ in the everyday sense, and not the time you spent pushing. A heavy box you hold still (s = 0) has no work done on it by that upward force, even if your arms tire. joule. Own words should match this idea: The SI unit of work (and of energy): one joule (J) is one newton-meter, the work done by a one-newton force along one meter of displacement in its own direction. Near-miss: Not a newton, which is a unit of force, and not a watt, which is a unit of power (work per time) and is not required today. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 96 J.
  2. 0 J, because cos90° = 0.
  3. W = 4 × 3 × ½ = 6 J.
  4. The centripetal force is perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement (the motion is tangent to the circle). cos90° = 0, so W = 0 at each instant.
  5. Only the along-track component does work: W = 5 N × 2 m = 10 J. The perpendicular component contributes 0.

History · Sources for early Rome: Livy, tradition, and archaeology

Check. 1. Livy wrote in the late first century BCE and early first century CE, centuries after the traditional period of the kings. 2. Archaeology (or, later, inscriptions): it can show settlement, building, and sometimes public space, without automatically naming Livy’s characters. 3. Model: Livy. Own words should match this idea: Titus Livius, a Roman historian writing in the late first century BCE and early first century CE. His Ab Urbe Condita (“From the Founding of the City”) is a major narrative source for early Rome, and it is a late source: Livy wrote centuries after the kings and the early Republic he describes. Near-miss: Not a contemporary of early Rome, and not an archaeological report. Using Livy well means dating him, and asking what kind of claim he can support. early Rome (tradition and evidence). Own words should match this idea: The period of the Roman kings and the first generations of the Republic, for which later literary tradition (Livy and others) must be read beside archaeology and inscriptions. Famous founding dates, such as the traditional 753 BCE for the city’s founding, are traditions, not measurements we can treat as eyewitness facts. Near-miss: Not “myth, so ignore it,” and not “Livy said it, so it happened in that year.” The skill is to separate tradition, later narrative, and material evidence. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A late source can still be the best narrative we have, and it is evidence of later memory and argument. The task is to use it as what it is, not to discard it.
  2. Similar: a literary source later than the events, still indispensable. Different: for Alexander we also have other historians and a tighter event-date range (336–323 BCE); early Rome’s first centuries are thinner in contemporary writing.
  3. No. It can confirm that people lived and built there in an early period. It does not, by itself, confirm a particular founding story or a named founder.
  4. Any sentence that calls 753 BCE a traditional date, not a measured eyewitness year.
  5. For example: “How late is this source, and is there any evidence that is not the same story retold?”

Atelier · The head: a first block-in

Check. 1. The neck is a cylinder (or a simple solid) that inserts under the skull. The near-miss is a thin stick holding up a mask-like face, with no volume and no insertion. 2. As a starting construct on an upright frontal academic head, then immediately checked against the plate. Never as a reason to move the plate’s features toward an ideal. 3. Model: block-in (head). Own words should match this idea: The first large-shape statement of a head: the skull as an egg or a simple solid, the tilt and turn, the center line, and the placement of the features as a group, before any finished eye or mouth. Near-miss: Not a tiny finished feature floating in a blank page, and not a tracing of hair as the first act. Hair is a later surface; the skull is first. center line of the face. Own words should match this idea: A line drawn through the middle of the face, following the turn and tilt, from which the eyes, nose, and mouth are placed symmetrically in the head’s own plane (or clearly offset, if the head is turned). Near-miss: Not a vertical ruled down the middle of the sheet unless the head is truly facing you and upright. The line belongs to the head, not to the page. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Oval and tilt; neck insertion; center line and brow line (feature placement as a group); hair as a mass, late in this list.
  2. The remembered “upright head” overrode the plate. The tilt in front of you is the fact.
  3. After the skull and feature placement, and as a mass (an envelope), not as a first layer of named curls.
  4. Planes and foreshortening (cube), the ellipse and a cylindrical neck (cylinder), and a rounded skull with a consistent light (sphere).
  5. A plan that starts with tilt, center line, and overall proportion, not with a single feature that looked unfinished.

Modern language · Spanish: stem-changing verbs, e → i

Check. 1. sirves; servimos. 2. Yo pido un libro. Nosotros servimos la comida. 3. Model: e → i stem change. Own words should match this idea: A small set of present-tense verbs (taught today: pedir, servir, repetir, vestir) whose stressed stem vowel e changes to i in all forms except nosotros and vosotros. Near-miss: Not the e → ie pattern of pensar, querer, or tener (week 3). Pedir is not pensar: pido, not *piedo and not *pedo. And not a past tense: this is still the present. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. pido
  2. pienso (I think) vs pido (I ask for / I order). Different changes, different verbs.
  3. For example: El camarero sirve el agua. Pedimos un libro.
  4. “We repeat” is repetimos; the stem does not change in nosotros. Repito is “I repeat.”
  5. pido, sirvo, repito, visto — said from memory after hearing the infinitives.

Logic · Converse, inverse, and contrapositive

Check. 1. Converse: If the street is wet, then it is raining. It need not be true (a spilled bucket can wet the street). The original can be true while the converse is false. 2. Converse and inverse are equivalent to each other; original and contrapositive are the other equivalent pair. 3. Model: converse. Own words should match this idea: The statement obtained by swapping a conditional’s antecedent and consequent. The converse of “If P, then Q” is “If Q, then P.” A converse is not automatically true just because the original is true. Near-miss: Not the inverse (which negates both sides and keeps the order), and not the contrapositive (which both swaps and negates). And not a claim that conversation is taking place. inverse. Own words should match this idea: The statement obtained by negating both sides of a conditional, without swapping them. The inverse of “If P, then Q” is “If not P, then not Q.” Like the converse, it is not guaranteed by the original. Near-miss: Not the converse, and not the contrapositive. In ordinary English, “inverse” has other meanings (inverse operations in algebra); here it is a technical name for this related conditional. contrapositive. Own words should match this idea: The statement obtained by swapping the two sides of a conditional and negating both: “If P, then Q” has contrapositive “If not Q, then not P.” The contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original: they are always both true or both false. Near-miss: Not the converse (swap only) and not the inverse (negate only). Those two are not equivalent to the original. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Converse: If opposite angles sum to 180°, then ABCD is cyclic. Inverse: If ABCD is not cyclic, then opposite angles do not sum to 180°. Contrapositive: If opposite angles do not sum to 180°, then ABCD is not cyclic. (The original is Euclid III.22 as taught; this course has used the converse as a usable test, with the caution that it is a different implication — do not blur that it is the converse.)
  2. The converse is a different claim. A proof of the converse, even a correct one, is not automatically a proof of the original.
  3. Because the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original: they are always both true or both false.
  4. For example: If a figure is a square, then it is a rhombus (true); converse, if it is a rhombus, then it is a square (false).
  5. The converse would say that P is necessary for Q (or that Q is sufficient for P) — a different necessity claim.
