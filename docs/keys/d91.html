Latin · Capitulum XVI: the ablative absolute, a first look

Check. 1. A loosely attached ablative phrase giving a circumstance, here a noun plus a present participle, both ablative. The present participle, already taught, is the form used today. 2. With the sun rising, the boys go out. 3. The perfect-participle kind of ablative absolute has not been taught and is reserved. 4. Model: ablative absolute. Own words should match this idea: An ablative absolute is a phrase in the ablative, loosely attached to the main sentence, that gives a circumstance — often time or attendant situation. In this first look the phrase is a noun (or pronoun) plus a present participle, both in the ablative: Magistrō recitante, discipulī tacent. — With the schoolmaster reciting, the pupils are silent. Near-miss: Not a first look at the present participle (Capitulum XIV), and not a first look at the ordinary ablative after ē or in. Not a perfect-participle absolute (urbe captā and the like), which is reserved. The phrase should not have the same person as the subject doing the same work twice (avoid “the master, the master reciting, recites”). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete sentence such as Magistrō recitante, discipulī audiunt.
  2. A complete sentence such as Puerīs loquentibus, magister tacet.
  3. The form recitante / loquentibus was already taught in Capitulum XIV. Today is the absolute construction, not the participle as a new form.
  4. The phrase gives a circumstance beside the main clause. If the same person merely repeats the same verb, the phrase has no separate work.

Mathematics · Geometry XLVI: the volume of a right pyramid

Check. 1. B = 64; V = (1/3) × 64 × 3 = 64 cubic units. 2. V = (1/3) × 24 × 5 = 40 cubic units. 3. B × h is the volume of a right prism, already taught. The pyramid is one-third of that, for the same base and height. 4. Model: volume of a right pyramid. Own words should match this idea: A right pyramid has its apex directly above the center of the base, and its volume is V = (1/3) × B × h, where B is the area of the base and h is the perpendicular height from the apex to the base. A classroom cube of edge a can be divided into three square pyramids, each with base a by a and height a, and the cube’s volume a³ shows that each pyramid has volume (1/3)a³ = (1/3)Bh. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s prism volume V = B × h, and not a claim that a pyramid has the same volume as a prism of the same base and height. The prism would have three times this volume. Not a cone, which is tomorrow, and not a sphere. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The cube is 125; each pyramid is 125/3 cubic units, or (1/3) × 25 × 5 = 125/3.
  2. 80 = (1/3) × 16 × h, so 80 = 16h/3, h = 15.
  3. B = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24; V = (1/3) × 24 × 9 = 72 cubic units.
  4. B is still an already-taught area, and h is still perpendicular to the base. The new factor is the 1/3.

Physics · Energy in simple harmonic motion: ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX²

Check. 1. ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX². 2. ½ × 80 × 0.0025 = 0.10 J total; KE at x = 0 is 0.10 J. Invented figures. 3. ½kx² is only the potential part. The total is ½kX²; at points other than the ends, some of that is already kinetic. 4. Model: energy in simple harmonic motion. Own words should match this idea: In undamped simple harmonic motion, OpenStax 16.5 writes the conservation of energy as ½mv² + ½kx² = constant. If the motion starts from rest at maximum displacement X, that constant is ½kX², so ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX². At the ends, all the energy is elastic potential; at equilibrium (x = 0), all of it is kinetic. Near-miss: Not a first look at PE_el = ½kx², or at ½mv², or at conservation of mechanical energy (Days 21–28 and last week). Not last week’s period T = 2π√(m/k) used as an energy, and not a claim that real springs have no damping. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 2.0 = ½ × 400 × X², so 2.0 = 200 X², X² = 0.010, X = √0.010 m, or about 0.100 m in a labeled school rounding. Invented figures.
  2. PE_el = ½ × 200 × (0.05)² = 0.25 J; KE = 1.0 − 0.25 = 0.75 J. Invented figures.
  3. T is a period, a time, from OpenStax 16.3, not an energy.
  4. PE_el = ½kx² was last week, from OpenStax 16.1. Today uses it inside the conservation statement of 16.5.

Elective · Logic: Baroco, a second-figure AOO syllogism

Check. 1. All P are M; Some S are not M; therefore Some S are not P. Second figure: M is the predicate of both premises. 2. An example such as the birds-and-pets one, or the squares-and-drawings one. The premises only place some S outside M, and every P is in M, so those S are not P. They do not place every S there. 3. Festino is EIO: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P. Baroco is AOO: All P are M; Some S are not M; therefore Some S are not P. The conclusions match in form; the premises do not. 4. Model: Baroco. Own words should match this idea: Baroco is a second-figure syllogism of the form AOO: a universal affirmative premise, a particular negative premise, and a particular negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: All P are M; Some S are not M; therefore Some S are not P. Near-miss: Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2), Camestres (AEE-2), or Festino (EIO-2), already taught. Festino begins with No P are M; Baroco begins with All P are M, and its second premise is a particular negative. The conclusion of both is Some S are not P, but the premises differ. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A valid classroom AOO-2, with the three vowels marked.
  2. The particular negative premise only gives some S. A universal conclusion would claim more than the premises warrant.
  3. That arrangement is Festino, already taught, which begins with a universal negative about P.
  4. The second figure, Cesare, Camestres, and Festino were already taught. Today is a new mood in that figure.
