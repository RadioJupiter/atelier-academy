Latin · Capitulum XV: prior, posterior, and īnferior

Check. 1. The first of two, the former. It orders a pair. This lesson does not use it for the first item in a long unordered list. 2. The latter pupil is silent, or the pupil at the back is silent, depending on the sense in context. In the Watch-me pair, it was “the latter.” 3. Sella īnferior. 4. Model: prior, posterior, and īnferior. Own words should match this idea: These three comparatives order or locate, rather than only comparing a quality such as longior. Prior, prius means the first of two, the former. Posterior, posterius means the one behind, or the latter of two. Īnferior, īnferius means the one below, the lower. Near-miss: Not a first look at ordinary comparatives of quality, already taught in Capitulum XII (longior, brevior). Prior is a true comparative with no ordinary positive taught in this course; do not invent a positive form for it here. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A pair such as Liber prior longus est. Liber posterior brevis est.
  2. A complete sentence such as Sella īnferior vacua est.
  3. Posterior orders a pair or names the one behind. Īnferior locates the one below. A seat at the back is posterior; a seat down a step is īnferior.
  4. Longior and the quality comparatives were taught in Capitulum XII. Today’s words order or locate.

Mathematics · Geometry XLII: the volume of a right rectangular prism

Check. 1. V = 8 × 2 × 5 = 80 cubic units. 2. 60 = 5 × 4 × h, so 60 = 20h, h = 3. 3. 18 is the number of unit cubes in one layer, the area of the base counted in squares, not the volume of the solid. The height 4 is still needed: 72 cubic units. 4. Model: volume of a solid. Own words should match this idea: The volume of a solid is the amount of space it occupies, measured in cubic units. A classroom cube of edge 1 has volume 1 cubic unit. Volume is not the same as area, which measures a face, and not the same as last week’s density, which is mass per unit volume. Near-miss: Not the area of a face, and not last week’s circle area πr², which is a measure of a flat region. Not a mass, and not a density. volume of a right rectangular prism. Own words should match this idea: A right rectangular prism, or classroom box, has three edges that meet at right angles: a length ℓ, a width w, and a height h. Its volume is V = ℓ × w × h, in cubic units. That product is the number of unit cubes that fill the box. Near-miss: Not the surface area (the total area of the faces), which is reserved. Not a slanted solid, and not a cylinder, which comes later this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. V = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cubic units. A cube is a right rectangular prism whose three edges are equal.
  2. Both are 60 cubic units. Equal volumes, different shapes.
  3. V = 7 × 3 × 6 = 126 cubic units.
  4. 36π is an area, in square units (with π). Volume is a measure of space, in cubic units. The two are not interchangeable.

Physics · Elastic potential energy, PE_el = ½kx²

Check. 1. ½ × 50.0 × 0.0225 = 0.5625 J, which OpenStax reports as 0.563 J after rounding. 2. PE_el = ½ × 80 × (0.050)² = 40 × 0.0025 = 0.10 J. Invented figures. 3. kx is the size of the force, in newtons, not the stored energy. The energy is the area ½kx², in joules. 4. Model: elastic potential energy. Own words should match this idea: Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in a system that obeys Hooke’s law when it is stretched or compressed from equilibrium. OpenStax 16.1 gives PE_el = ½kx², which is the area of the triangle under a straight-line graph of the applied force kx against the displacement x. Near-miss: Not a first look at Hooke’s law, F = −kx, or at the force constant k, taught last week. Not a first look at work, kinetic energy, or potential energy as kinds, already taught in Days 21–28. Not gravitational potential energy, and not a claim that every spring stores this energy once it has left Hooke’s-law behavior. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 2.0 = ½ k (0.10)², so 2.0 = 0.005 k, k = 400 N/m. Invented figures.
  2. It becomes four times as large: ½ × 200 × 0.040 = 4.0 J, not 2.0 J.
  3. PE_el = ½ × 200 × (0.05)² = 100 × 0.0025 = 0.25 J. Invented classroom figures, using last week’s k only as a contrast.
  4. Work, kinetic energy, and potential energy, and the conservation of mechanical energy, were already taught in Days 21–28. Today is the Hooke’s-law formula for one kind of stored energy.

History · The one device: four-cornered courses and lead in the joints (Dewing I.1.50–53)

Check. 1. Only one of the many devices, for the present, from which the strength of the whole work may be impressed. Some devices he finds hopeless to understand in their entirety and impossible to explain in words. 2. To the angles when they formed the projecting corners of the pier; in rectangles when they stood between the angles. They were rough by nature but worked smooth. The courses formed a four-cornered shape. 3. Lead poured into the interstices and hardened in the joints. Not lime, “which they call ‘asbestus’,” and not asphalt, “the material which was the pride of Semiramis in Babylon.” 4. Model: lead in the joints (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing I.1.53, the pier stones “were held together neither by lime (titanos), which they call “asbestus”, nor by asphalt, the material which was the pride of Semiramis in Babylon, nor by any other such thing, but by lead (molibdos) poured into the interstices, which flowed about everywhere in the spaces between the stones and hardened in the joints, binding them to each other.” Near-miss: Not a first look at Justinian, Anthemius, or Isidorus, already named in earlier weeks, and not a first look at last week’s four piers as supports. Not an invented weight of lead, and not a claim that Dewing has described every device that secured the church. four-cornered courses (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing I.1.51–52, the piers “are not constructed in the same way as other structures.” “The courses of stone were laid down so as to form a four-cornered shape, the stones being rough by nature but worked smooth; and they were cut to the angles when they were destined to form the projecting corners of the sides of the pier, but when they chanced to be assigned to a position between the angles, they were cut in rectangles.” Near-miss: Not a first look at the four piers of I.1.37–38, already read last week. Not an official height or thickness invented for the courses. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It is Dewing’s reported name for lime (titanos), in his wording. The binding material he names is lead. Quotation keeps his spelling and his claim from being rewritten as a modern materials label.
  2. They were already named in earlier weeks as the master-builders. Today they appear again as those who, with Justinian, secured the church by many devices.
  3. I.1.54–57, the gold ceiling and the two stoas.
  4. A short account that keeps the many devices, the one example, the four-cornered courses, and the lead, with no invented height or weight.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of decir

Check. 1. Dije, dijiste, dijo, dijimos, dijisteis, dijeron. The they-form is dijeron, with no i after j. 2. Yesterday I told the truth. (Invented.) 3. The third-person plural of this stem does not add i after j. The form taught is dijeron. 4. Model: dije. Own words should match this idea: Dije is the first-person singular preterite of decir, to say or to tell. The stem is dij-: dije, dijiste, dijo, dijimos, dijisteis, dijeron. There is no i in dijeron (not dijieron). Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s pude, puse, supe, or quise, and not the present digo. Not a spelling dijieron. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Three complete invented sentences, each with the requested form, in a completed-event sense.
  2. Dijo is from decir (said / told). Quiso is from querer (wanted / tried), taught last week. Different verbs, different stems.
  3. Dije (dij-); pude (pud-). Both are irregular preterites, but the stems and the verbs are different.
  4. Supe was taught last week as the preterite of saber. Today’s first look is decir.

Elective · Logic: Festino, a second-figure EIO syllogism

Check. 1. No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P. Second figure: M is the predicate of both premises. 2. An example such as the birds-and-pets one, or the squares-and-drawings one. The premises only place some S in M, and no P is in M, so those S are not P. They do not place every S there. 3. Camestres is AEE, with a universal negative conclusion (No S are P). Festino is EIO, with a particular negative conclusion (Some S are not P). 4. Model: Festino. Own words should match this idea: Festino is a second-figure syllogism of the form EIO: a universal negative premise, a particular affirmative premise, and a particular negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P. Near-miss: Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2) or Camestres (AEE-2), already taught. Festino’s conclusion is particular (Some S are not P), not universal. Not Baroco, which is reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A valid classroom EIO-2, with the three vowels marked.
  2. The particular premise only gives some S. A universal conclusion would claim more than the premises warrant.
  3. Festino’s first premise is a universal negative, No P are M, and its second is a particular affirmative, Some S are M. That other arrangement is not the mood taught today, and a later mood is reserved.
  4. The second figure, Cesare, and Camestres were already taught. Today is a new mood in that figure.
