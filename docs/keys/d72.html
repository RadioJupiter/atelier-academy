Latin · Capitulum XIII: Kalendae, Nonae, and Īdūs

Check. 1. 13 February, because February is not March, May, July, or October, so the Īdūs fall on the thirteenth. 2. Its genitive plural is Īduum, the fourth-declension genitive plural, not *Īdārum. Last week’s first look at the fourth declension (exercitus, metus) is continued, not retaught as new nouns. 3. Model: Kalendae. Own words should match this idea: The first day of a Roman month (Kalendae, Kalendārum, feminine plural). Near-miss: Not the Nonae or the Īdūs, and not a name for the whole month. Kalendae is always a plural in form, even though it names one day. Nonae. Own words should match this idea: A named day in each Roman month: the seventh of March, May, July, and October, and the fifth of the other months (Nonae, Nonārum, feminine plural). Near-miss: Not the first of the month (that is Kalendae), and not the Īdūs. Nonae are not on the same numbered day in every month. Īdūs. Own words should match this idea: A named day in each Roman month: the fifteenth of March, May, July, and October, and the thirteenth of the other months (Īdūs, Īduum, feminine plural, fourth declension). Near-miss: Not Kalendae or Nonae, and not a second-declension noun. Īdūs, Īduum continues the fourth declension begun last week with exercitus and metus, but Īdūs is used in the plural for this date. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Kalendae Mārtiæ: 1 March; Nonae Mārtiæ: 7 March; Īdūs Mārtiæ: 15 March.
  2. A model: Kalendae Iānuāriae prīmum diem mēnsis nōminant. Other original sentences are acceptable if Kalendae and a month name are used correctly.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXI: a height from Pythagoras, then the area

Check. 1. A = ½ × 5 × 12 = 30. The hypotenuse 13 is not the height to either leg. 2. 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10², so it is right-angled opposite 10. A = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24. 3. Model: a height found by the Pythagorean theorem. Own words should match this idea: When a perpendicular height of a triangle is not given, but it forms a right triangle with known sides, that height can be found from a² + b² = c², and then the area is ½bh. Near-miss: Not using the hypotenuse as if it were a height to a leg, and not adding the legs to make an area. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Hypotenuse: 9² + 12² = 81 + 144 = 225, so c = 15 (the positive square root). Area: ½ × 9 × 12 = 54. The 15 is not needed for this area, but it names the third side.
  2. 5 is the hypotenuse, not a perpendicular height to the side 3. The height to the side 3 is the other leg, 4, so A = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6.

Writing & Rhetoric · The straw man

Check. 1. A précis keeps the author’s points, order, and proportion, in your own words. It does not replace the points with a weaker invented version. 2. B has chosen a claim that is easier to dismiss, but it is not A’s claim. The method does not test the argument that was actually offered. 3. Model: straw man. Own words should match this idea: A fallacy in which a person answers a weaker, distorted, or invented version of someone else’s claim, as if that version were the claim that had been made. Near-miss: Not an accurate restatement of a claim followed by a real objection, and not a précis of what was actually said. A genuine disagreement with the claim as made is not a straw man. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model straw man (other honest inventions accepted): “You think Roman dates have no numbered days at all.” A sound reply: “Yes: prīdiē is only the day immediately before the named day; other days are counted separately.”
  2. A did not deny that slanted sides have lengths. B should answer whether the height in A = ½bh must be perpendicular, which is the claim that was made.

Science · Water: solutions, solute, and solvent

Check. 1. The solute is the component regarded as present in the smaller share, dissolved by the solvent. Official percentages are not invented here; “minor” is the Gold Book’s wording. 2. No official mass or percentage is being measured. The picture is for the names. 3. Model: solution. Own words should match this idea: A liquid or solid phase containing more than one substance, when for convenience one (or more) substance, called the solvent, is treated differently from the other substances, called solutes. Near-miss: Not a mere pile of two dry solids that have not formed one phase, and not last week’s surface tension under a new name. A solution is one phase with more than one substance. solute. Own words should match this idea: The minor component of a solution, regarded as having been dissolved by the solvent. Near-miss: Not the solvent, and not a second name for the whole solution. solvent. Own words should match this idea: The substance in a solution that is treated differently from the solutes; often, and in this week’s water work, the major component regarded as having dissolved the solute. Near-miss: Not the solute, and not a claim that water is the only solvent in chemistry. Today’s module is still Water. dissolution. Own words should match this idea: The mixing of two phases with the formation of one new homogeneous phase, the solution. In school language, this is the dissolving itself. Near-miss: Not a change of name for melting (a solid becoming a liquid of the same substance), which belongs to earlier Water work on states, and not a surface-tension effect under a new name. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A solution is one liquid or solid phase with more than one substance, the solvent treated differently from the solutes. Dissolution is the mixing of two phases that forms that one phase.
  2. No. Surface tension is a property of a liquid surface, already taught from IAPWS last week. A solution is a kind of phase with more than one substance.

Atelier · Value in the sockets

Check. 1. Last week: placing the socket as a plane. Today: the value of that recess. 2. Where the plane of the well turns out of the light, not as a drawn outline around an almond. 3. Model: value in the eye socket. Own words should match this idea: The well of the already-placed socket is treated as a recess: usually darker than the forehead and the brow, with a terminator where the plane turns, not a outlined almond drawn on the surface. Near-miss: Not a new placement of the socket, not a flat shaded patch with no plane, and not a drawing of eyelashes as a substitute for the recess. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The patch does not show the plane turning; a recess has a turn, and the values should follow that turn.
  2. The nose, already placed as a wedge, in value: a lighter front plane and a darker side plane, if the light is from the front and side.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of hacer

Check. 1. Hacer does not use the regular -er yo ending -í on a stem hac- in this tense. The yo form is the irregular hice. 2. A model: Ayer hice la tarea. Anoche ella hizo un dibujo. Other short correct sentences accepted. 3. Model: the preterite of hacer. Own words should match this idea: The preterite of hacer is hice, hiciste, hizo, hicimos, hicisteis, hicieron. The él/ella/usted form is hizo, with z, not *hico. Near-miss: Not the shared fui set of ser and ir, and not the regular -er yo form comí. Hacer is a different irregular set. The z in hizo is part of the form, not a decoration. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. hiciste; hicimos; hicieron.
  2. Each heard form used in a short preterite sentence. Model: Hice la tarea; ella hizo un dibujo; ellos hicieron un proyecto.

Elective · Music: authentic and plagal cadences

Check. 1. Both arrive at I (the tonic). They differ in the leading chord: V for authentic, IV for plagal. 2. C–F–G–C. The last two, G to C, form the authentic cadence. 3. Model: authentic cadence. Own words should match this idea: A cadence that arrives from V to I: the dominant chord moves to the tonic chord. In last week’s I–IV–V–I progression, the last two chords are an authentic cadence. Near-miss: Not a plagal cadence, which arrives from IV to I, and not a first look at I–IV–V–I as a progression (that was last week). Today names the arrival V–I. plagal cadence. Own words should match this idea: A cadence that arrives from IV to I: the subdominant chord moves to the tonic chord. Near-miss: Not an authentic cadence (V–I), and not a claim that last week’s I–IV–V–I ended with IV–I. That progression ended V–I. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Authentic: C major to F major (V–I in F). Plagal: B-flat major to F major (IV–I in F).
  2. That progression was taught last week. Today names the V–I arrival and the IV–I arrival inside work already begun.
