Latin · Capitulum V, part 2: cum and sine; rooms of the villa

Check. 1. ‘With the slave’; ablative singular. 2. Līberī sunt in cubiculīs. 3. Model: cum and sine. Own words should match this idea: A pair of opposite prepositions, both used with the ablative: cum means ‘with’ (accompaniment), and sine means ‘without’. Near-miss: cum is not the English word come, and sine is not ‘sin’. Both take the ablative, not the accusative. cum servīs (with the slaves) is not servōs (the slaves, as objects). rooms of the villa. Own words should match this idea: The main named parts of the house and garden in this chapter: ātrium, cubiculum, tablīnum, peristylium, hortus, ōstium. Near-miss: These are not English room-names with a Latin accent, and the atrium is not ‘any hall’. Learn each word as a Latin word, with its ending. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. cum līberīs; sine pecūniā; ab ōstiō.
  2. The boy comes out of the bedroom.
  3. Any five correctly named: ātrium, cubiculum, tablīnum, peristylium, hortus, ōstium, and so on.
  4. Sentences must use the ablative after the preposition and make sense.
  5. Clear reading of the new section.

Mathematics · Geometry VI: the angle bisector and a parallel through a point

Check. 1. Arc from Q cutting QP and QR at A and B; equal arcs from A and B meeting at C; draw QC. Then △AQC ≅ △BQC by SSS, so ∠AQC = ∠BQC. 2. Draw a transversal through S cutting m; copy a corresponding (or alternate interior) angle at S. The lines are parallel because corresponding (or alternate interior) angles are equal (Day 6). 3. Model: angle bisector. Own words should match this idea: The ray that divides an angle into two equal angles. Every point on the angle bisector is equidistant from the two sides of the angle (the distances being perpendicular distances to the sides). Near-miss: It is not a line that merely ‘looks in the middle’, and the distances to the sides are perpendicular distances, not distances along the sides. parallel through a point. Own words should match this idea: The unique line through a given point P, not on a line ℓ, that is parallel to ℓ. It can be constructed by copying a corresponding (or alternate interior) angle made by a transversal through P. Near-miss: It is not ‘any line through P that does not meet ℓ on the page’. Parallelism is a property in the plane, justified by equal corresponding angles, not by the drawing happening to stop short. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A perpendicular to the original line at the vertex (the bisector of 180° is 90° to each side).
  2. Construct a perpendicular, then bisect one of the right angles.
  3. The two bisectors of the adjacent straight angles (180°) lie along the same line, perpendicular to the given line.
  4. Both constructions produce the same unique parallel (parallel axiom).
  5. The three bisectors meet at one point; the compass opening from that point to each side (perpendicular) is the inradius.

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: a new model passage

Check. 1. The past gives the standard; ‘now’ tests whether the nation can live up to it. The sentence stops being history and becomes a present claim. 2. The occasional opening locates a real moment and a purpose; ‘I am going to write about…’ only announces a topic and delays the work. 3. Model: model passage. Own words should match this idea: A short, complete stretch of writing chosen for imitation: first copied exactly, then analyzed for its choices, then varied. The model for this lesson is the opening of Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address (1863). Near-miss: A model passage is not a source to be silently recycled in your own essay, and copying it is not the same as understanding it. Copy first; then name the moves. opening that places the occasion. Own words should match this idea: A first sentence that locates the writing in time, place, or circumstance before it states a claim, so the reader knows why the words are being spoken or written now. Near-miss: It is not a throat-clearing preface (‘I am going to write about…’), and it is not a dictionary definition of the topic. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The copy should match the public-domain text exactly.
  2. Four moves, in complete sentences, in the student’s own words.
  3. Time, proposition, present turn, purpose; no borrowed phrasing.
  4. Most of the verbs do work (brought forth, dedicated, engaged, testing, met, dedicate); the observation should be accurate.
  5. Any borrowed phrase is replaced.

Science · Phenomenon: why oil paint dries

Check. 1. A drying oil is a plant oil that can form a solid film by a process other than water leaving; water as a binder leaves mainly by evaporation. 2. No. The solvent can leave by evaporation, but the oil remains and is what forms the solid film. The phenomenon of the oil’s hardening is still not evaporation of the oil. 3. Model: drying oil. Own words should match this idea: A plant oil, such as linseed oil (from flax seed), that can form a solid film by reacting with oxygen in the air, rather than by the evaporation of water. Painters use it as the binder of oil paint. Near-miss: A drying oil is not an oil that is already dry, and it is not the same as a volatile solvent (such as spirits of turpentine) that does leave by evaporation. Watercolor ‘dries’ mainly by water leaving; oil paint does not. evaporation. Own words should match this idea: The change in which a liquid becomes a gas at its surface. A watercolor wash becomes a dry layer mainly because water evaporates; that is not the main way a drying oil forms a film. Near-miss: Evaporation is not boiling (which happens throughout the liquid at a characteristic temperature), and it is not the same as a chemical reaction with oxygen. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A labeled, dated record, with honest observations (wet / tacky / firm), not a guessed clock.
  2. A complete because/but/so sentence that names a process other than ‘the oil left’.
  3. Linseed (flax) is the usual drying oil; walnut and poppy are also used. A non-drying or slow-drying table oil (for example, olive oil) does not form a painter’s film in the same way — only if the student has a source; otherwise ‘I need a source before I name one’ is an acceptable cautious answer.
  4. The later chemistry is a reaction with oxygen; a sealed drop is cut off from the air.
  5. A chemical reaction with the air (to be named: autoxidation, and then polymerization).

Literature · Iliad, Books 7–9: the embassy to Achilles

Check. 1. The duel is broken off without a death; the two sides bury their dead and (in the poem’s action) the war is not ended. Hector is still alive; Achilles is still withdrawn. 2. He moves in the course of the book: a threat to leave can give way to a refusal that still stops short of returning to fight at once. A good answer tracks that shift in the text you read, with a short attributed phrase if needed, and does not invent a reconciliation. 3. Model: embassy (to Achilles). Own words should match this idea: The episode in Iliad Book 9 in which Odysseus, Ajax, and Phoenix go to Achilles’ camp with Agamemnon’s offers, and Achilles refuses to return. It is a scene of speeches, not a battle. Near-miss: The embassy is not a ‘committee meeting’ in a modern sense, and it is not the end of Achilles’ anger. Book 9 leaves him still withdrawn, even if his position shifts a little in the course of the speeches. paradeigma (the Meleager story). Own words should match this idea: A story told inside another story as an example meant to persuade: Phoenix tells Achilles the tale of Meleager, who also withdrew in anger and was later begged to save his city, as a warning and a model. Near-miss: A paradeigma is not a random digression, and it is not the same as a simile (Day 4). It is a narrative example used as an argument. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A half-page that treats the speech as an argument, with at least one specific moment from the book.
  2. The Greeks are hard pressed (Book 8), so the leadership tries words and gifts; necessity for them is not the same as persuasion of Achilles.
  3. The insult of Book 1 is still the core; the speech may enlarge, reorder, or revalue it. The student should show, not only assert, a reshaping.
  4. A real although-sentence: although the gifts are many, Achilles still treats the insult as unbought.
  5. A sentence that matches the ending: the envoys leave without a return to battle, and the hush or tension of the hut should be felt, not overwritten with a later book.

Atelier · The cylinder: the ellipse

Check. 1. A foreshortened circle: a symmetrical rounded shape with a long and a short axis, not a pointed boat. 2. If the sides miss the ellipses, the end is no longer the end of that cylinder; the contour should touch and turn with the ellipse. 3. Model: ellipse. Own words should match this idea: The shape a circle appears to take when it is foreshortened — the end of a cylinder, or a wheel seen at an angle. In a careful drawing it is a symmetrical, rounded shape with a long axis and a short axis, not a pointed boat and not a flattened sausage with corners. Near-miss: An ellipse is not two flat lines with bumps (a ‘football’ with points), and it is not a circle you have squashed only on one side. The ends of the long axis are curves, not corners. axis of the cylinder. Own words should match this idea: The imagined center line running through the cylinder, from the center of one end to the center of the other. The short axis of each end ellipse should align with this axis; if it does not, the end will look tilted off the form. Near-miss: The axis is not a decorative center stripe, and it is not the same as a highlight. It is a construction line you may erase later. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A constructed cylinder with true ellipses (no points), a consistent axis, and light running around the form.
  2. Axes marked correctly: short axes on the cylinder’s axis, long axes at right angles.
  3. Three sentences that report real measurements and a real correction.
  4. A short second study, or a written note of what happens to an ellipse nearer eye level (it closes).
  5. The cube changes value at a plane edge (suddenly, relative to a curve). The cylinder changes gradually around the curve, with a terminator as a band, not as a hard fold, unless the light is extremely sharp and you have honestly seen a harder edge.

Modern language · Spanish: stem-changing verbs, o → ue

Check. 1. encuentro, encuentras, encuentra, encontramos, encontráis, encuentran. 2. Puedo estudiar, pero quiero dormir. 3. Model: stem-changing verb (o → ue). Own words should match this idea: A verb whose stem vowel o becomes ue in the present, in every form except nosotros and vosotros. The infinitive still shows o. Near-miss: This is not the e → ie change from Day 11, and it is not a past tense. Nosotros and vosotros keep o: podemos, not ‘puedemos’. diphthong (ue). Own words should match this idea: Two vowel sounds in one syllable, spelled ue, in the stressed stem of these verbs. Near-miss: ue is not the same diphthong as ie, and it is not a silent letter. Hear the difference between puedo and pienso. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. vuelve; volvemos.
  2. It must be dormimos: nosotros does not take ue. (podemos is already correct.)
  3. Two grammatical sentences, e.g. Puedo leer. No podemos salir.
  4. ¿Puedes encontrar el libro?
  5. E.g. Ellos quieren dibujar. Ellos vuelven el lunes. The they-forms must show ie and ue respectively.

Music theory · Intervals

Check. 1. Major 2nd, major 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th, major 6th, major 7th, perfect octave. 2. The major third is larger by one half step (two whole steps versus a whole step plus a half step). 3. Model: interval. Own words should match this idea: The distance between two pitches, named by counting letter-names inclusively, then qualified as perfect, major, or minor (and, later, augmented or diminished). Near-miss: An interval is not ‘how hard the piece is’, and the number is not a count of steps alone (C to E is a third, whether you count two whole steps or not). Name the letter-span first, then the quality. perfect, major, and minor. Own words should match this idea: Qualities of intervals. Unison, fourth, fifth, and octave are called perfect in the major scale from the tonic (C–C, C–F, C–G, C–C′ in C major). Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths are major in that scale; a major interval shortened by a half step becomes minor. Near-miss: ‘Perfect’ is not a compliment, and ‘minor’ is not ‘sad’ as a definition. Quality is a measured size, not a mood. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. D–A is a fifth; on white keys it is a perfect fifth (seven half steps). F–B is a fourth in letters (F, G, A, B) and is larger than a perfect fourth (the tritone, six half steps); today’s course can name it as an augmented fourth if the student has met the word, or as ‘a fourth that is one half step larger than perfect’ if not — do not call it a perfect fourth. E–C up is a sixth (E, F, G, A, B, C); it is a minor sixth (eight half steps).
  2. G–D a perfect fifth; G–E a major sixth; G–A♭ a minor second (G–A would be a major second).
  3. The letters C and E make a third; the flat changes the quality to minor, not the number.
  4. A perfect fourth above E is A (E–F–G–A, five half steps). A major third above E is G♯ (four half steps); E–G is only a minor third (three half steps).
  5. C–G is a perfect fifth; C–A is a major sixth.
