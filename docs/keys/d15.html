Latin · Place where and motion: a Friday check

Check. 1. ubi? — e.g. Iūlia est in hortō. quō? — e.g. Mārcus ad vīllam ambulat. 2. Reject it as an answer to ubi? ad vīllam is motion toward. Place where is in vīllā. 3. Model: motion toward. Own words should match this idea: A construction that answers quō? (where to?): in this chapter, usually ad + accusative (ad vīllam), as opposed to place where (in + ablative) and motion from (ab or ex + ablative). Near-miss: Motion toward is not place where. ad vīllam and in vīllā are not interchangeable. quō. Own words should match this idea: The Latin question-word meaning ‘where to?’, asking for a motion-toward answer. Near-miss: quō is not ubi (where?) and not unde (where from?), which you will name formally tomorrow. The three questions must not be collapsed into English ‘where’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The three lines as in Learn, or equivalent correct forms.
  2. For example, Mārcus cum līberīs ad vīllam ambulat.
  3. Clear reading.
  4. ablative; accusative; ablative; accusative; ablative; accusative.
  5. Puerī ex hortō ad ōstium ambulant.

Mathematics · Geometry VIII: rectangle, rhombus and square

Check. 1. 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13², so the diagonal is 13. 2. The diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other, so each half of the known diagonal is 5, and a side is 13. The missing half-diagonal is √(13² − 5²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12, so the other diagonal is 24. 3. Model: rectangle. Own words should match this idea: A parallelogram with a right angle. Then all four angles are right angles, and the diagonals are equal and still bisect each other. Near-miss: A rectangle is already a parallelogram; you do not need to prove the opposite sides are parallel again. It is not ‘any four-sided figure with a square corner’ if the opposite sides fail to be parallel. rhombus. Own words should match this idea: A parallelogram with all four sides equal. Its diagonals are perpendicular and bisect the vertex angles; they still bisect each other. Near-miss: A rhombus need not be a square: the angles need not be 90°. A square is the special rhombus (and special rectangle) that has both a right angle and four equal sides. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Each half-diagonal is 6 and 8, so a side is 10. Area = (1/2) × 12 × 16 = 96.
  2. The two right triangles formed by one diagonal and two sides have the same legs (opposite sides of a rectangle are equal), so the hypotenuses (the two diagonals) are equal.
  3. A side is 10/√2 = 5√2. Area = (5√2)² = 50. (Or: the diagonals of a square are perpendicular and equal, and the area is half their product: (1/2)×10×10 = 50.)
  4. No. Equal diagonals in a rhombus force the right triangles to be isosceles, so the angles are 90° and the rhombus is a square.
  5. Each side of the midpoint figure is parallel to a diagonal of the original (midline of a triangle) and half as long, so opposite sides are equal and parallel. It is a rhombus when the original diagonals are perpendicular.

Writing & Rhetoric · Summary and précis

Check. 1. A passing précis is three sentences or fewer, in new words, in order, with proportional weight, and with both word counts stated. 2. The heaviest move should have a sentence (or the main clause) devoted to it. If not, the revision should correct the proportion. 3. Model: summary. Own words should match this idea: A shorter account, in your own words, of what a passage says, in the same order, without new examples and without your opinion of it. Near-miss: A summary is not a list of favorite sentences, and it is not a response (‘I found this moving’). It does not add facts the passage did not contain. précis. Own words should match this idea: A very tight summary, often a third of the original length or less, that keeps the argument’s structure and proportion: what the passage spends most time on stays most visible, and what it mentions once stays brief. Near-miss: A précis is not a telegram that drops all the connections, and it is not a paraphrase of the same length. If every clause of the original still has a partner, the précis is not yet tight enough. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Any leftover author wording is replaced.
  2. The summary should be accurate and free of ‘I’.
  3. Combining joins sentences you write; a précis shrinks someone else’s argument without changing its shape.
  4. The present war would look unmeasured; Lincoln’s test would have no standard.
  5. The checks in the Learn section all hold.

Physics · Projectile motion

Check. 1. 4.9 = 4.9 t², so t = 1.0 s. Range = 15 × 1.0 = 15 m. 2. Time is still 2.0 s, so range = 16 m. A faster throw would not land later: the time is set by the height. 3. Model: projectile. Own words should match this idea: A body in free flight under gravity alone (air resistance neglected in this course), with a constant horizontal velocity and a vertical acceleration of g downward. The two components are treated separately, then combined. Near-miss: A projectile is not ‘anything that is thrown’ once it is still being pushed, and the path is not two independent objects. It is one body, described by two components. Horizontal velocity does not ‘run out’. range (of a projectile). Own words should match this idea: The horizontal distance traveled from the launch point to the landing point, equal to vx times the time of flight, when the landing is at the same vertical level or at a stated lower level. Near-miss: Range is not the length of the curved path (that would be the distance traveled), and it is not the greatest height. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. vx = 5√3 m/s, vy = 5 m/s.
  2. x = vx t is linear in t; y is quadratic in t; eliminating t gives y as a quadratic in x, a parabola.
  3. A. The vertical motions are the same free fall from the same height; the horizontal push does not change the time.
  4. t = 2.0 s, so vx = 30/2 = 15 m/s.
  5. Air resistance would reduce the speed (especially the horizontal speed) and the range, and the time of flight would no longer follow the simple free-fall formula.

History · The Egyptian–Hittite treaty of year 21 of Ramesses II

Check. 1. Ramesses II of Egypt and Ḫattušili III of Ḫatti. The treaty is a later settlement, dated year 21, not an on-the-day account of the battle. 2. The Akkadian cuneiform tablet from Ḫattuša, and the Egyptian hieroglyphic version at Karnak (with a damaged Ramesseum copy). 3. Model: treaty of year 21. Own words should match this idea: A peace treaty between Ramesses II of Egypt and Ḫattušili III of Ḫatti, dated on the Egyptian side to year 21 of Ramesses II. A cuneiform version (in Akkadian, the diplomatic language) comes from Ḫattuša; a hieroglyphic version is carved at Karnak (and a damaged copy was at the Ramesseum). Near-miss: The treaty is not a newspaper report of the Battle of Kadesh, which had been fought years earlier, and it is not a neutral minutes-of-the-meeting. Each court is still presenting itself. diplomatic copy. Own words should match this idea: A version of an agreement written for one court, often in a lingua franca (here Akkadian on the tablet from Ḫattuša) or in the court’s own script (Egyptian at Karnak), after the terms have been negotiated. The two copies can be compared, but neither is a transparent window onto ‘what really happened’ in every clause. Near-miss: A diplomatic copy is not an eyewitness diary, and a later replica is not itself the primary source. Use the Karnak inscription and the tablet as the sources; use the replica as a modern display. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A passing answer attributes a translation, names shared terms (peace, mutual help, later generations — as the translation supports), and notes royal self-presentation (who is named first, which gods witness).
  2. Two complete sourcing sentences.
  3. New Kingdom, 19th Dynasty — centuries after the 4th-Dynasty pyramids.
  4. Akkadian was the diplomatic lingua franca of the Late Bronze Age; the tablet is a diplomatic copy, not necessarily the Hittite language itself.
  5. An arguable thesis, not ‘this essay is about the treaty’.

Atelier · A second Bargue plate: the feature

Check. 1. A symbol is a remembered sign (a line, a heart, a cartoon lip). The form is the measurable shapes, planes, and values on the plate, including thickness, foreshortening, and shadow. 2. So that a small error is visible and correctable, and so that you are not pushed into a tiny symbol. 3. Model: feature study. Own words should match this idea: A drawing of a single facial feature from a plate (here, a Bargue lithograph), treated as a constructed form with planes, not as a symbol of an eye or a mouth. The aim is proportion, angle, and value families, not a finished portrait. Near-miss: A feature study is not a cartoon symbol (an almond with a circle in it), and it is not a whole head rushed at a small scale. It is one feature, large enough to measure. symbol versus form. Own words should match this idea: A symbol is the remembered sign for a thing (an eye as an almond, a mouth as a line). Form is what is actually on the plate: a set of measurable shapes, planes, and values. The study begins with form, and only later will a symbol be unlearned if it has crept in. Near-miss: This is not a ban on knowledge: you may know that an eyeball is a sphere. Knowledge is tested against the plate, not used to override it. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A large, measured block-in of a real plate, with a first modeling and a fixed viewpoint. Not a finished copy and not a cartoon.
  2. Three real measurements marked, not three invented ones.
  3. Four honest sentences, including something still wrong.
  4. A second block-in compared to the plate, with two real differences named.
  5. A feature is still planes, possibly ellipses, and value families. The geometry lessons are the same tools, applied to a more particular shape.

Elective · Artistic anatomy 2: muscles and tendons

Check. 1. The belly is the fleshy, shortening middle; the tendon is a tougher, narrower cord. Shading a tendon like a belly invents a swelling that is not there. 2. The deltoid caps the shoulder. It is tied to the clavicle (and to the scapula and humerus); the clavicle was a Day 5 landmark. 3. Model: origin and insertion. Own words should match this idea: The two attachments of a muscle. The origin is the usually more stable attachment; the insertion is the usually more movable one. A simple first rule: the muscle pulls the insertion toward the origin. (Some muscles can work with the two ends reversed; do not force that exception into every case.) Near-miss: Origin and insertion are not ‘top and bottom’, and they are not the same as the belly (the fleshy middle of the muscle). tendon. Own words should match this idea: The tough, cord-like or flattened connective tissue that often joins a muscle to a bone. It is not the muscle belly, and it does not bulge in the same way when the muscle works. Near-miss: A tendon is not a bone, and it is not a ligament (which joins bone to bone). You do not need a full classification of connective tissue for today’s check; you do need not to shade a tendon as if it were a swollen muscle. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A study that starts from bone, then bellies, then narrower tendons, with honest labels. No invented anatomy.
  2. Gastrocnemius, and the Achilles tendon at the heel.
  3. Because some muscles can work with the roles reversed, or have more than one action; the first rule is a starting picture, not a claim about every case.
  4. A tendon joins muscle to bone (often); a ligament joins bone to bone. Using them as synonyms would put the wrong structure in the drawing and in the explanation.
  5. Four careful sentences: the weight-bearing side supports, the relaxed knee can unlock, and the surface muscles will not look the same on the two sides. If Day 18 has not yet been read, the same observation holds of any standing pose with a weight shift.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. A locus is the set of all points (and only those points) with a given property. The perpendicular bisector of AB is the locus of points equidistant from A and B, which is why the two-arc construction works. 2. Friction is a force that resists slipping along a surface; the normal force is the support force perpendicular to the surface. Friction is present when a block is sliding, or when it is only just kept from slipping; a smooth ice problem may treat friction as absent, if the lesson says so. They are not the same force. 3. A verb whose stem vowel e becomes ie in the present when that stem is stressed. Nosotros and vosotros do not change: pienso / pensamos. 4. The episode in which Odysseus, Ajax, and Phoenix go to Achilles with offers, in speeches, and he refuses. It is not a battle, and it is not yet his return to the war. 5. A plane is a flat face (or a flattened patch used to understand a form). Foreshortening is the apparent shortening of a receding shape as seen from a fixed point, which you measure; running out of paper is not a reason and not a measurement. 6. For example ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q. A distinguishing row for ¬P ∧ Q versus ¬(P ∧ Q) is P true, Q false: the first is false, the second is true.
