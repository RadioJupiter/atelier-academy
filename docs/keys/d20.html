Latin · Capitula V–VI: two-week check

Check. 1. cum + ablative (cum servō); ad + accusative (ad vīllam); prope + accusative (prope oppidum); in + ablative (in hortō). 2. Mārcus Rōmae habitat. Mārcus Rōmam it. Mārcus Rōmā venit. (Other names are fine if the forms are right.) 3. Saccus ā servō portātur. 4. Model: case after a preposition. Own words should match this idea: The rule that the preposition, not a translation from English, chooses the case: one closed list takes the ablative (in, ex, ab, cum, sine), and one takes the accusative (ad, ante, post, inter, prope, circum, apud, per). Town names are the exception that drops the preposition. Near-miss: It is not ‘use the accusative for motion and the ablative for rest’ in every sentence: cum and sine take the ablative even though they are not places, and prope takes the accusative even though it can describe a rest. three place questions. Own words should match this idea: The trio ubi? (where?), quō? (where to?), unde? (where from?), each with its own construction for common nouns and for town names. Near-miss: English ‘where’ is not one Latin word. Mixing the three questions is the usual error, and it produces the wrong case. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Līberī cum servō ex hortō ad vīllam ambulant.
  2. Mārcus Rōmae habitat; Iūlia in hortō est; saccus ā servō portātur.
  3. Ablative Rōmā (from Rome); locative Rōmae (at Rome).
  4. Clear reading of the weak section.
  5. Ablative: in, ex, ab, cum, sine. Accusative: ad, ante, post, inter, prope, circum, apud, per.

Mathematics · Two-week problem set: constructions to tangents, and a first look at progressions

Check. 1. 10th term = 7 + 9 × (−3) = 7 − 27 = −20. 2. √45 − √20 = 3√5 − 2√5 = √5. And 2⁵ · 2⁻³ = 2² = 4. 3. OP = √(9² + 12²) = √(81 + 144) = √225 = 15. 4. Model: arithmetic progression. Own words should match this idea: A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by adding the same fixed number, the common difference d. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d, where a is the first term. Near-miss: An arithmetic progression is not a list that merely ‘goes up’. If the difference changes, it is not arithmetic. It is also not a geometric progression, where you multiply by a constant each time. geometric progression. Own words should match this idea: A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by multiplying by the same fixed number, the common ratio r. The nth term is a rⁿ⁻¹, where a is the first term. Near-miss: A geometric progression is not a list of geometry measurements, and it is not arithmetic: 3, 6, 12 does not have a constant difference (the differences are 3, then 6). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Draw a transversal through P that cuts ℓ; copy a corresponding (or alternate interior) angle at P. The lines are parallel because those angles are equal (Day 6 / Day 12).
  2. Add: 3x = 12, x = 4, y = 3.
  3. Half-diagonals 6 and 8, so a side is 10. Area = (1/2) × 12 × 16 = 96.
  4. Half-chord 8, distance √(10² − 8²) = √(100 − 64) = √36 = 6.
  5. 5 × (−2)⁵ = 5 × (−32) = −160.
  6. a + 4d = 18 and a + 8d = 30. Subtract: 4d = 12, d = 3, a = 6. The 20th term is 6 + 19 × 3 = 63.

Writing & Rhetoric · Two-week check: a two-paragraph essay

Check. 1. All three should be marked on the page; any missing sentence should be added, not just promised. 2. Each quotation should now have all four parts, or should be turned into a summary if the exact words were not needed. 3. A specific sentence is named, or a specific sentence is added. 4. Model: two-paragraph essay. Own words should match this idea: A complete short argument with one thesis, two paragraphs (each with a topic sentence and support), at least one introduced and explained quotation or a precise précis of a source, a real concession or a limit, and a concluding sentence that returns to the thesis in new words. Near-miss: It is not two unconnected paragraphs, not a summary of a lesson, and not a page of quotations. Length is not the test; the jobs on the list are the test. revision pass. Own words should match this idea: A second writing, after the draft is complete, in which you check a short list — thesis, topic sentences, evidence, attribution, concession or limit, concluding sentence, and punctuation of joins — and change the sentences that fail, rather than recopying the whole for neatness. Near-miss: A revision pass is not a first draft written more slowly, and it is not a hunt for a better vocabulary word in a sentence that already works. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete two-paragraph essay on a real question from the term, not a summary of a lesson.
  2. The revision pass should show changes, not only a neater copy.
  3. Every marked semicolon joins two complete sentences; every colon introduces an explanation, a list, or a quotation.
  4. Rambling joins are repaired; a designed Johnson-like period may stay.
  5. The revised version is the work that counts.

Physics · Two-week check: friction to momentum

Check. 1. v = 3 m/s in the original direction (total p = 12, total mass = 4). 2. a = 20 m/s², T = 40 N. 3. J = Δp = −6 N·s; F_avg = −20 N. 4. Model: impulse. Own words should match this idea: The product of a net force and the time it acts, equal to the change in momentum: J = F_net Δt = Δp, when the force is constant (or using an average force). The SI unit is N·s, which is the same as kg·m/s. Near-miss: Impulse is not the same as a force (it includes time), and it is not the same as energy. A small force over a long time can deliver the same impulse as a large force over a short time. independence of components. Own words should match this idea: The rule, used for projectiles, that the horizontal motion (constant v_x, if air is neglected) and the vertical motion (acceleration g) can be solved separately, and that a change in one component does not by itself change the other. Near-miss: Independence is not a claim that the body is two bodies, and it fails once air resistance (or another force with both components) matters. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. a = 2.55 m/s² (Day 11’s check, redone).
  2. t = 1.0 s, range = 15 m.
  3. v = 1 m/s in the first direction.
  4. B. Time is set by the height; range = v_x t doubles when v_x doubles.
  5. A model answer: total momentum is a vector that internal pairs cannot change; kinetic energy can leave the motion as heat or deformation.

History · Two-week synthesis: sources for kingship, from Khufu to the Ptolemies

Check. 1. An arguable thesis, plus a sourcing sentence that names a real document and its period. 2. A precise limit (no complete social history; no invented wording; mixed firmness of dates). 3. A complete short essay that meets the list in Learn. 4. Model: synthesis (this week). Own words should match this idea: A short argued piece that puts more than one source, from more than one period, under a single thesis, with sourcing sentences and a limit. It is not a timeline in paragraph form. Near-miss: A synthesis is not a retelling of every lesson this week, and a quotation left to speak for itself is not evidence (Day 18 of writing). periodization. Own words should match this idea: The decision to cut a long past into named periods (Old Kingdom, New Kingdom, Hellenistic) so that change can be talked about. The cuts are tools, not walls in the ground, and the years attached to them may be firmer in one period than in another. Near-miss: Periodization is not a claim that nothing continued, and it is not a license to use one year-number as if all periods were equally exact. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  5. The revised version is the work that counts.

Atelier · Critique and a two-week plan

Check. 1. Two specific sentences that point at real passages in real drawings, not at ‘talent’ or at ‘messiness’ in general. 2. A plan with one or two skills, a set-up, and a yes/no check, small enough to keep. 3. Model: two-week critique. Own words should match this idea: A structured look at these two weeks’ work (cube, cylinder, sphere, Bargue feature, still life, plaster feature), naming what is constructed, what is still a symbol or a wire, and what the next two or three sittings should fix. It is specific, and it uses the terms taught, not a vague ‘good’ or ‘bad’. Near-miss: A critique is not a score, and it is not a general comment on talent. It is a reading of particular drawings against particular skills. plan for the next sittings. Own words should match this idea: A short, written plan that names one or two skills to practice, the set-up that will make that practice possible, and how you will know the sittings have done their work. It is small enough to keep. Near-miss: A plan is not a list of ten ambitions, and it is not ‘draw better’. If it cannot be checked, it is not yet a plan. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A four-part critique that uses these two weeks’ terms and refers to particular drawings.
  2. A reasoned choice between a small correction and a new start, tied to a named skill.
  3. An honest transfer (e.g. ellipses into an iris, or not yet) and an honest gap.
  4. Step 1: walk the contour and name found or lost from the set-up. Step 2: bring the background up where an edge must be lost, and keep a sharp ridge found. (Wording may vary; the two steps must be actual Day 18 work.)
  5. The slip has only the two-sitting plan, and the sitting stops there.

Elective · Poetry 2: caesura, enjambment, and the volta

Check. 1. A caesura is a pause inside a line; a line break is the end of the metrical line, whether or not the sense stops. Enjambment is a designed run-on of sense, not a missing period the typist forgot. 2. abab cdcd efef gg. Look first at the start of the third quatrain (line 9) and at the couplet (line 13), then follow the thought. 3. Model: caesura and enjambment. Own words should match this idea: A caesura is a pause inside a line of verse, often (but not only) in the middle, and often marked by punctuation. Enjambment is the running-on of a sentence or clause across the end of a line, without a pause at the line break. Near-miss: A caesura is not a stanza break, and enjambment is not ‘the poet forgot to punctuate’. Both are tools for controlling breath and sense against the metrical line you already scanned on Day 10. volta. Own words should match this idea: The turn in a sonnet: the place where the argument or the figure shifts. In a Petrarchan sonnet this often comes after the first eight lines. In a Shakespearean sonnet (abab cdcd efef gg) it is often felt at the closing couplet, and sometimes earlier, around line 9. Look for a ‘but’, a ‘yet’, or a change of thought; do not treat a line number as a superstition. Near-miss: A volta is not a change of typography, and it is not automatically ‘line 9 in every sonnet’. If you cannot hear a turn, say so, and point to what the poem does instead. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A marked stretch that shows real scansion, a real pause, and an honest placement of the turn if it is nearby.
  2. The poem claims that the verse itself will give life, so the summer (and the beloved) will not fade while men can read. That answers, and changes, the opening comparison with a passing season.
  3. Two attributed lines with a marked mid-line pause.
  4. End-stopped: breath and sense finish together. Enjambed: the breath may continue, and the sense is completed on the next line, against the meter’s turn.
  5. Four scannable iambic pentameter lines (with ordinary license) that actually include the required tools.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. A small word (me, te, lo, la, nos, os, los, las) that stands in for a known direct object. Lo veo (or, with an infinitive, Voy a verlo / Lo voy a ver). Not Veo lo. 2. The personal a marks a specific person as a direct object (Veo a Ana). Voy a estudiar is the near future (ir a + infinitive). They are different jobs of a. 3. A set-piece description of a work of art inside a poem. The shield holds a wider world than the battlefield (cities, a lawsuit, harvest, dance), so the poem stops the war in order to show what the war is not, or what it can destroy — not to pad the book. 4. Centripetal acceleration is v²/r, toward the center, for uniform circular motion. This course does not treat a ‘centrifugal force’ as a real third force on the moving object in an inertial frame; the inward acceleration is the one the second law uses with the inward net force. 5. A volta is the turn of thought in a sonnet (often at line 9 or at the couplet — follow the thought). Enjambment is the sentence running over a line end; a caesura is a pause inside a line. 6. Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (in the table, $4 and 30). A shortage is quantity demanded greater than quantity supplied (price below equilibrium); a surplus is the reverse. 7. P is necessary for Q when Q → P (Q only if P). ‘If P then Q’ is P → Q, which is sufficiency of P for Q, not the same unless both directions hold. 8. A sound answer names a real caution: Knossos frescoes may include modern restoration; ‘Minoan’ is Evans’s name; the Ramesses–Ḫattušili treaty should be cited as year 21, not as an exact BCE year treated as certain; the Lion Gate’s heads, or paint on a kouros, are missing and should not be invented. The caution limits what a claim can say.
