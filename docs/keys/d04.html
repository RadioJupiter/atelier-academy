Latin · Capitulum II, part 1: gender and the genitive

Near-miss: The genitive is not the same as gender, and cuius is not itself the genitive ending of every noun. quis and quid. Own words should match this idea: Question words: quis, ‘who?’, for a person; quid, ‘what?’, for a thing. Near-miss: Quis is not used for a town, and quid is not used for a person. Oppidum is neuter, not masculine. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Mathematics · Geometry II: congruent triangles and the isosceles triangle

Check. 1. The two base angles are each 72°, so the vertex angle is 180° − 72° − 72° = 36°. 2. No. AAA shows the same shape, not necessarily the same size (similar, not necessarily congruent). 3. Model: congruence. Own words should match this idea: A matching of two figures so that corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal; one can be placed exactly on the other. Near-miss: AAA is not a congruence test: it shows the same shape, not necessarily the same size. isosceles. Own words should match this idea: A triangle with two sides equal. The angles at the base (opposite those sides) are equal. Near-miss: Isosceles does not mean ‘all three sides equal’ (that is equilateral, which is a special isosceles triangle). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. In triangles AOC and BOD: AO = OB, CO = OD (bisected), ∠AOC = ∠BOD (vertical). SAS ⇒ congruent ⇒ AC = BD. ∎
  2. Triangles OAD and OBC: OA = OB, OD = OC, angle O common. SAS ⇒ AD = BC. ∎
  3. Triangles ABM and ACM: AB = AC, BM = MC, AM common. SSS ⇒ congruent, so ∠BAM = ∠CAM (bisector) and ∠AMB = ∠AMC; these are adjacent and supplementary, so each is 90°. ∎
  4. Compare triangle ABC with ACB: ∠B = ∠C, BC = CB, ∠C = ∠B. ASA ⇒ congruent ⇒ AB = AC. ∎
  5. Not valid. With ∠A = 30°, AC = 10 and BC = 6 (opposite A): the height from C to the line of AB is 10 · sin 30° = 5 < 6 < 10, so a circle of radius 6 around C cuts that line at two points on the same side of A, giving two different triangles (∠B ≈ 56.4° or ≈ 123.6°).
  6. AB = AC ⇒ ∠B = ∠C; AB = BC ⇒ ∠A = ∠C. So all three are equal. ∎

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 1: making hints

Check. 1. Jot brief hints and put the original away. 2. If the notes are a copy, you are still copying, not reconstructing. 3. Model: Franklin's method. Own words should match this idea: A practice from Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography: take a passage, jot brief hints of the ideas in order, put the passage away, and rewrite from the hints; then compare with the original. Near-miss: The hints are not allowed to be a full copy of the sentence. If you can read the original off your notes, the method has failed. hint (in Franklin's method). Own words should match this idea: A brief cue for an idea, written in order, just strong enough to recall the thought and not the original wording. Near-miss: A hint is not a quotation, and it is not an outline of a whole essay (that comes on Day 6). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Computation: calculation or estimate. Threescore: sixty (so "threescore thousand" = 60,000). Speculation: here, an essay or reflection (the paper itself). Fallow: ploughed but left unsown. Assiduous: persistent, diligent.
  2. The paragraph has seven sentences (the second runs on past a colon). Example hints: (1) City eager for my papers. (2) 3,000 daily × 20 readers = 60,000. (3) Big audience: agreeable, useful. (4) Wit and morality combined. (5) Refresh memories daily against folly. (6) Idle mind sprouts follies. (7) Socrates: heaven to men; me: clubs, coffee-houses.
  3. —
  4. To announce the paper's aim (moral improvement made agreeable) and flatter its readers into continuing; the imagined reader is the educated London public at tea-tables and coffee-houses.

Science · Combustion and gases: the quantitative model

Check. 1. Combustion also produces gases and heat; the candle goes out before the oxygen is gone; and the observed rise, and when it happens, match heating, escape of gas, and cooling more closely than a 21% oxygen hole. A true chemical fact can still be the wrong explanation of a measurement. 2. Model: combustion. Own words should match this idea: The chemical burning of a fuel with oxygen, producing new gases (and often water) and releasing heat. Near-miss: Combustion is not ‘using up air and leaving an empty space of the same volume as the oxygen’. The new gases and the heat both matter. quantitative. Own words should match this idea: Described with measured numbers and a relation, not only with words such as ‘more’ or ‘less’. Near-miss: A number without a unit, or a calculation that does not match an observation, is not yet a quantitative explanation. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O.
  2. 25/38 ≈ 0.66 CO₂ per O₂. Each O₂ removed is replaced by about 0.66 CO₂, so the gas count falls by about one third of the oxygen used, not all of it.
  3. 1.0/24 ≈ 0.042 mol; 21% of 1.0 L ≈ 0.21 L of oxygen.
  4. V₈₀/V₂₀ = 353/293 ≈ 1.20. The jar holds only its own volume, so 0.20/1.20 ≈ 17% of the heated gas escapes.
  5. Typical household candles burn several grams per hour (often roughly 5–10 g/h, depending on size and wick). Any reasoned estimate with stated assumptions (wick size, flame size) is acceptable; the measurement is the real check.

Model: catalogue. Own words should match this idea: A formal list built into a poem. The Catalogue of Ships in Iliad Book 2 is a roll-call of the Greek contingents. Near-miss: It is not a random inventory, and it is not required that you memorize every name. This course allows the Catalogue to be skimmed. simile. Own words should match this idea: A comparison that names itself with ‘like’ or ‘as’, often extended over several lines in Homer, usually from nature or ordinary work. Near-miss: A simile is not an epithet, and it is not a metaphor (a metaphor does not say ‘like’ or ‘as’). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Atelier · Value: the nine-step scale

Check. 1. How light or dark a surface is. 2. Uneven steps will throw off later judgments of mid-grays and shadows.

The steps should appear equally spaced when squinting; the most common fault is too many light steps and a sudden jump to dark. A good scale makes step 5 a convincing middle gray. In the gradation, no bands or blotches should be visible at arm's length. 3. Model: value. Own words should match this idea: How light or dark a surface is, considered separately from its color. Near-miss: Value is not color, and it is not ‘how important the object is’. A pale yellow and a pale blue can have the same value. nine-step scale. Own words should match this idea: A row of nine even steps of gray from white to black, used so that values in a drawing can be chosen and compared. Near-miss: The steps must be visually even, not even only in how hard you pressed if the result looks uneven. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Economics & personal finance · Scarcity and opportunity cost

Check. 1. You could only have taken one of those alternatives, the best one. Adding them counts the same time (or the same resource) twice. 2. Model: scarcity. Own words should match this idea: The fact that resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up. Near-miss: Scarcity is not ‘poverty only’, and it is not ‘having zero of something’. A person with money still has scarce time. opportunity cost. Own words should match this idea: The value of the single best alternative given up when a choice is made, not the sum of every alternative. Near-miss: It is not ‘$60 plus the lecture’, because you could not have done both of the things you gave up at the same time. sunk cost. Own words should match this idea: A cost already paid that cannot be recovered, and that should not affect a forward-looking decision. Near-miss: A sunk cost is not an opportunity cost, and ‘I already paid’ is not a good reason to continue. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The single best alternative given up: whichever of the shift or the lecture you value more. You could only have done one of them, so the cost is one, not both.
  2. No. The $40 is spent whether you go or not (a sunk cost). Choose the option you value more from now on.
