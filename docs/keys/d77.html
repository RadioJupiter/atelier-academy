Latin · Capitulum XIV: the present participle

Check. 1. The girls, writing, are sitting. 2. Rīdet is the main verb; legēns describes the boy and the same-time reading. 3. Model: present participle (Latin). Own words should match this idea: The Latin present participle describes an action going on at the same time as the main verb. It is formed with -ns, -ntis (legēns, legentis), declines like a third-declension adjective, and agrees in gender, number, and case with the noun it describes. Near-miss: Not the main verb of the sentence, and not the Spanish present participle (-ando / -iendo) already taught on Day 22, which is a different language’s form. Not an English dangling participle from Day 23 treated as if it were Latin grammar. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: Puer audāx, legēns, tacet. is fine if agreement is right; other original sentences accepted.
  2. Spanish -ando / -iendo is a Spanish form used with estar. Today’s -ns, -ntis is Latin, and it agrees with a noun like an adjective.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXV: a triangle as half a parallelogram

Check. 1. 27. 2. The two triangles fill the parallelogram without overlap and are made by one diagonal, so they have equal area. (That is the fact used here; it is not a claim about every pair of triangles in a more crowded figure.) 3. Model: triangle as half a parallelogram. Own words should match this idea: A diagonal of a parallelogram splits it into two triangles of equal area. Each triangle has the same base and the same perpendicular height as the parallelogram, so its area is ½bh — the formula already taught last week, now seen as half the parallelogram. Near-miss: Not a new formula in place of last week’s ½bh, and not a claim that any two triangles in the figure have equal area — only the two made by one diagonal of the parallelogram. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The parallelogram has area 15 × 4 = 60 (twice the triangle’s 30).
  2. A 6-by-8 rectangle, area 48; half of 48 is 24.

Writing & Rhetoric · Post hoc

Check. 1. A model: ‘The bell rang, and then it rained; I have no reason, from that order alone, to say the bell caused the rain.’ Other clear refusals accepted. 2. Many things happen after other things without being caused by them. A cause needs more than succession. 3. Model: post hoc. Own words should match this idea: Post hoc (short for post hoc, ergo propter hoc: after this, therefore because of this) is the flaw of treating a later event as caused by an earlier one only because it came after. Near-miss: Not circular reasoning (yesterday’s restatement of a claim), and not a careful causal argument that offers a mechanism or a controlled comparison. Sequence alone is not a cause. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: ‘I opened a book, and then a dog barked; the book caused the bark’ (invented). Other clear sequence-as-cause examples accepted.
  2. A reason why the earlier event produced the later one: a mechanism, a comparison, or a sourced account, not order alone.

Science · Water: a saturated solution

Check. 1. A solution at the same concentration as one in equilibrium with undissolved solute, at a specified temperature and pressure. Close Gold Book wording accepted in the student’s own words. 2. It is a demonstration, not an official solubility measurement or a tabulated percentage. 3. Model: saturated solution. Own words should match this idea: In the IUPAC Gold Book (S05471), a saturated solution is a solution which has the same concentration of a solute as one that is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at specified values of the temperature and pressure. Near-miss: Not merely a ‘strong’ or concentrated solution. Last week’s concentrated meant relatively much solute in a given amount of solution, qualitatively; a solution can be concentrated without being in equilibrium with leftover solid, and a saturated solution is defined by that equilibrium at a stated temperature and pressure. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Dilute and concentrated remain qualitative comparisons of how much solute is present. Saturated is the equilibrium with leftover solute, a different job.
  2. The equilibrium concentration can change if the temperature or pressure changes. The definition is for specified values of those.

Literature · Inferno I, lines 91–111: the Greyhound

Check. 1. “’Twixt Feltro and Feltro shall his nation be.” 2. “The maid Camilla,” and “Euryalus, Turnus, Nisus, of their wounds.” They are not used here as a key that officially identifies the Greyhound with one historical person. 3. Model: the Greyhound (Inferno I). Own words should match this idea: In Longfellow’s translation of Inferno I, lines 100–111, Virgil says that the beast will go on until “the Greyhound / Comes, who shall make her perish in her pain.” The Greyhound “shall not feed on either earth or pelf, / But upon wisdom, and on love and virtue”; “’Twixt Feltro and Feltro shall his nation be”; and he shall hunt the beast “Until he shall have driven her back to Hell.” Near-miss: Not a claim that this course has identified a particular historical person as the Greyhound. The lesson reads the lines as they stand. Not a first look at Virgil’s later offer to be a guide (lines 112–136), already taught last week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. To the offer to “be thy guide” and to lead the speaker through “the eternal place” (lines 112–114), already taught.
  2. Worldly gain, in this wording, contrasted with wisdom, love, and virtue.

Atelier · Checking the terminator, on the head

Check. 1. Down the far side of the nose and into the cheek, and along the jaw, as a changing edge. Other honest descriptions accepted. 2. The sphere already taught the turn from midtone into core shadow. Today that meeting is applied across the head’s planes. 3. Model: terminator (on the head). Own words should match this idea: The terminator is the turning where the light family ends and the shadow family begins — the place the form stops facing the lamp. On the head it runs, in one held light, across forehead, cheek, and jaw as a changing edge, not as a cut-out outline. Near-miss: Not a first look at the turn of the form (Day 43), and not a new core shadow. Not a cartoon outline around the profile. Not a second lamp. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. If the lamp moves, the families and the terminator move, and yesterday’s check cannot be finished.
  2. A sharp break can make a firmer edge; a slow turn makes a softer meeting. Neither is a cartoon outline.

Modern language · Spanish: estar and andar, the same uv pattern

Check. 1. Estuve, estuviste, estuvo, estuvimos, estuvisteis, estuvieron. 2. Yesterday I was at home. He walked to the park. (Invented drills.) 3. Model: uv-stem preterite. Own words should match this idea: Tener, estar, and andar share a uv-stem in the preterite: tuve, estuve, anduve (and tuviste, estuviste, anduviste, and so on). The endings are the same set; the stems are tuv-, estuv-, and anduv-. Near-miss: Not the fui set of ser and ir, and not the present estoy or ando. Not a first look at the preterite tense itself. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Tener, estar, and andar.
  2. He/she was (or you formal was); I walked.

Elective · Music: the deceptive cadence

Check. 1. Authentic V–I; plagal IV–I; half cadence ends on V; deceptive V–vi (or V–VI in minor). 2. G to C is V–I, the authentic close already taught. 3. Model: deceptive cadence. Own words should match this idea: A deceptive cadence is a common-practice close that goes from V to vi (or V to VI in a minor key), so the ear, prepared for I, lands on the relative tonic instead. It is a cadence, but it is not an authentic V–I close, and it is not a half cadence, which ends on V. Near-miss: Not a first look at an authentic cadence (V–I) or a plagal cadence (IV–I), taught last week, and not a first look at a half cadence, which was taught on Day 52 as a pause that ends on V. Today’s new job is the V–vi landing. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. G major, then A minor.
  2. It is a named common-practice close. The ear is led to vi on purpose, not by accident.
