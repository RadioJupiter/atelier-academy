Mathematics · Geometry LI: the surface area of a right pyramid

Check. 1. The base plus the triangular faces. For a right square pyramid, B + 2bl, where l is the slant height of a face. 2. 96 square units. 3. l² = 4² + 3² = 25, so l = 5. The 4 is the perpendicular height, used in volume last week, not the altitude of a face. 4. 144 square units. 5. Model: surface area of a right pyramid. Own words should match this idea: The surface area of a right pyramid is the area of the base plus the areas of the triangular faces. For a right square pyramid, there are four congruent triangular faces. If l is the slant height of a face — the altitude of that triangle from the apex to the base edge — the four faces have total area 2bl, and the whole surface is B + 2bl, where b is a base edge and B = b². Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s volume V = (1/3)Bh, which used the perpendicular height, not the slant height. Not the surface area of a cone, which is tomorrow. Classroom lengths are invented and labeled invented. slant height of a pyramid face. Own words should match this idea: The slant height l of a face of a right square pyramid is the altitude of that triangular face, from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge. It is not the perpendicular height h from the apex to the base plane. If h and the half-edge of the base are known, l comes from the Pythagorean theorem: l² = h² + (b/2)². Near-miss: Not last week’s perpendicular height h in V = (1/3)Bh. Using h in place of l would understate the faces. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Four faces: 4 × (1/2) × 10 × 13 = 260. Base 100. Surface area 360 square units.
  2. l² = 3² + 4² = 25, l = 5. Faces 80, base 64, surface area 144 square units.
  3. That formula is volume, and it uses the perpendicular height, not the surface of the faces.
  4. A cube’s faces are squares, not four triangles meeting at an apex. The cube’s surface area is 6 × 6 × 6’s face area, 6 × 36 = 216, by last week’s earlier work on prisms, not by 2bl.

That form uses ω in radians, and this course has not yet taken the radian as a first look; the circle work used degrees only. 5. Model: projection of uniform circular motion. Own words should match this idea: OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.6, shows that if a point P moves at constant speed around a circle of radius X, its projection on a fixed axis undergoes simple harmonic motion of amplitude X. The time for one revolution is the period of that SHM: T = 2πX / vmax, where vmax is the constant speed around the circle, which is also the maximum speed of the projection. Combined with last week’s vmax = X√(k/m), this recovers the already-taught period T = 2π√(m/k). Not a first look at radians, or at the formula x = X cos(ωt), which OpenStax also writes; this course has used degrees only in the circle work, so that cosine form is reserved and is not computed this week. Damped motion (OpenStax 16.7) is also reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
