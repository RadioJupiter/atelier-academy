Latin · Capitulum XII, part 1: third-declension adjectives (fortis, -e)

Check. 1. Mīles nōn tristis est. 2. mīles is masculine nominative singular, so the adjective is fortis, not the neuter forte. 3. Model: third-declension adjective. Own words should match this idea: An adjective that uses third-declension endings and agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender. The first look in this chapter is the pair of forms such as fortis (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and forte (neuter nominative or accusative singular). Near-miss: Not a first-and-second-declension adjective such as magnus, magna, magnum, which was already taught. Fortis does not become *fortus. fortis, -e. Own words should match this idea: A third-declension adjective meaning strong or brave. The dictionary pair is fortis, forte: fortis for the masculine or feminine nominative singular, forte for the neuter nominative or accusative singular. Near-miss: Not *fortus, and not a change of meaning between the two example sentences: the endings change to match the noun, the sense stays “strong” or “brave.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Sentences such as Mīles fortis est.
  2. Scūtum nōn forte est.
  3. fortis, -e is a third-declension adjective, not a magnus, -a, -um adjective. The masculine nominative is already fortis.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Geometry XXIX: the Pythagorean theorem

Check. 1. 13, because 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13². 2. No. The theorem is about a triangle already known to be right. Here 3² + 5² = 34, which is not 6², so this triangle would not satisfy a² + b² = c² in any arrangement, and today’s statement does not call it right. 3. Model: Pythagorean theorem. Own words should match this idea: In a right triangle, the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. If the legs are a and b and the hypotenuse is c, then a² + b² = c². Near-miss: Not a claim about every triangle, only a right triangle. A triangle with sides 3, 5, and 6 (already used as a similarity example) is not made right by this theorem: 3² + 5² = 9 + 25 = 34, which is not 6². hypotenuse. Own words should match this idea: The side opposite the right angle in a right triangle. It is the longest side of that triangle. Near-miss: Not either leg (the two sides that form the right angle), and not a name for a side of an acute or obtuse triangle, which has no right angle and so has no hypotenuse. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The other leg is 12, because 15² − 9² = 225 − 81 = 144 = 12².
  2. c² = a² + b² is larger than a² and larger than b² (for a and b positive), so c is larger than each leg.

Writing & Rhetoric · The précis

Check. 1. A précis is in your own words. Leaving out a clause of the original can be part of shortening, but the wording still has to be yours, not a trimmed quotation. 2. The main claims should still be visible and should not swap their weight: both “greater in extent” and “more illustrious” belong, and a side clause should not become the whole précis. 3. Model: précis. Own words should match this idea: A short restatement of a passage in your own words, that keeps the author’s points and their order and proportion, without adding a new opinion, and without simply quoting. Near-miss: Not a quotation (which keeps the author’s wording), not a loose summary that drops the order or the main points, and not a reaction or a review. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: Justinian took over a state that was in disorder and, Procopius says, made it both larger and more illustrious, including by driving out barbarians who had long pressed upon it. (Other faithful wordings are acceptable if they keep these points and add no new verdict.)
  2. Those sections are reserved; a précis is of the set passage only, not of the rest of the chapter from memory.

Physics · The center of mass

Check. 1. 5 × 4 = 20, so 2 × d = 20 and d = 10 units. Invented. 2. The products of mass and distance must match. The larger mass must sit closer to the balance point, so the point is off-center. 3. Model: center of mass. Own words should match this idea: The balance point of a system of masses, treated as the single point at which the system’s mass may be taken to act for the purpose of translation, or of balance under gravity (when that point is also called the center of gravity, for a uniform gravitational field). Near-miss: Not necessarily the geometric midpoint, unless the masses (or a uniform object) are symmetric. A uniform bar does have its center of mass at its midpoint; a bar with unequal masses does not. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. This course has no sourced official figure of that kind to use, so one is not invented.
  2. The same mass × distance balance is used, now to name the balance point as the center of mass. Torque and Στ = 0 remain last week’s words, not a new first look.

History · Justinian, in Procopius, Buildings I.1.6

Check. 1. The extract gives Procopius’s wording, not a survey figure this course has opened or computed. 2. 476 was last week’s conventional marker for the western line in Italy, and the East continued. Justinian, in this extract, is emperor of that continuing eastern state, not the man who “ended Rome” in 476. 3. Model: Justinian. Own words should match this idea: The eastern Roman emperor in whose honour Procopius wrote The Buildings. In the set extract of I.1.6, Procopius says that Justinian, taking over a state harassed by disorder, made it greater in extent and much more illustrious, including by expelling barbarians who had long pressed upon it. Near-miss: Not a western emperor of the fifth century already marked by 476, and not a claim that this course has independently measured the empire’s area. The claims in I.1.6 are Procopius’s, in a work in praise of Justinian’s buildings. The Buildings (Procopius). Own words should match this idea: A work by Procopius on the building works of Justinian, here read in H. B. Dewing’s 1940 public-domain translation. It is a panegyric of those works, not a modern measured survey, and not Procopius’s Wars, which he refers to but which this week does not open. Near-miss: Not a modern guidebook, and not an excuse to invent official measurements of a church this course has not opened a plan of. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. He refers there the story of the expulsion of barbarians. This week does not open the Wars; it stays with the set extracts from The Buildings.
  2. The church called Sophia is reserved for a later extract in this chapter.

Atelier · The eye sockets, on the blocked-in head

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -er verbs

Check. 1. El lunes pasado comimos pan. 2. The yo form of a regular -er preterite ends in -í (comí), not the -ar ending -é. 3. Model: the preterite of regular -er verbs. Own words should match this idea: The past-tense forms of a regular -er verb, such as comer: comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron. These endings are not the -ar endings already taught (hablé, hablaste, habló…). Near-miss: Not the present (como, comes, come), and not the -ar preterite (hablé, habló). Comí is not *comé. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Ayer no comí fruta.
  2. Three heard forms, each used in a short preterite sentence.
  3. History stays in the English history lesson. Spanish here is a short picture past, with comer.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Logic · Darii, a first-figure AII syllogism

Check. 1. Darii is AII; Celarent is EAE. Celarent has a universal negative major and a universal negative conclusion; Darii has a universal affirmative major and a particular affirmative conclusion. 2. Soldiers (M): it appears in both premises and not in the conclusion. 3. Model: Darii. Own words should match this idea: A valid first-figure syllogism of the form AII: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The major premise is universal affirmative, the minor and the conclusion particular affirmative. Near-miss: Not Celarent (EAE), already taught, and not a particular major with a universal minor in this figure, which would not be Darii. “Some M are P; all S are M; therefore…” is a different arrangement, and is not today’s form. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The minor only gives “some S.” The conclusion cannot honestly say “all S.”
  2. Fallacies of a different kind remain for a later week.
