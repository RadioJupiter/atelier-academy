Latin · Capitulum XVI: the ablative absolute, used and contrasted

Check. 1. With the boys speaking, the schoolmaster is silent. 2. Magistrō recitante is the absolute. Ē lūdō is a prepositional ablative, the place left. 3. Ē plus the ablative was already taught and is a complement of ēgreditur, not a new absolute.

  1. Two complete original sentences; one main verb may be loquitur, cōnātur, sequitur, or ēgreditur.
  2. Ē lūdō has a preposition and names the place. An absolute of today’s kind is a noun plus a participle, without that preposition, giving a circumstance.
  3. The perfect-participle absolute has not been taught.
  4. A complete sentence such as Sī magister recitat, or better, Sī tempestās fit, puerīs loquentibus, discipulī tacent — so long as the absolute and the sī-clause are both complete and grammatical.

Mathematics · Geometry XLVII: the volume of a right cone

Check. 1. V = (1/3)π × 25 × 3 = 25π cubic units. 2. 36π = (1/3)π × 9 × h, so 36 = 3h, h = 12. 3. That is last week’s cylinder. The cone is one-third of it, for the same r and h. 4. Model: volume of a right cone. Own words should match this idea: A right cone has a circular base and its apex directly above the center of that base. Its volume is V = (1/3) × B × h, with B the already-taught area of a circle, πr². So V = (1/3)πr²h, in cubic units. Exact answers stay in terms of π. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s cylinder V = πr²h, and not a first look at yesterday’s 1/3. Not a sphere, and not a slanted cone. Not last week’s circle area taken as a volume. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. r = 5; V = (1/3)π × 25 × 6 = 50π cubic units.
  2. Cylinder 36π; cone 12π. The cone is one-third of the cylinder.
  3. 48π = (1/3)π r² × 9, so 48 = 3r², r² = 16, r = 4 (a length is positive).
  4. h is the perpendicular height from the apex to the base, as in the pyramid and the cylinder already taught.

Science · The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, from the Harvey/NIST table

Check. 1. It is the energy needed to turn one gram of ice directly into vapor at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, from Harvey’s NIST/CRC table. 2. 2 × 2834 = 5,668 J, or 5.668 kJ. Invented mass. 3. 2834 and 333.4 are both 0 °C figures. Their difference is not a fetched official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, and this course does not invent one. 4. Model: enthalpy of sublimation of ice. Own words should match this idea: The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, written ΔsublH in the Harvey/NIST table, is the energy needed to turn ice directly into vapor at the transition, per gram, at the stated temperature. At 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the table gives ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g, and not a first look at melting or at density. Not an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2834 − 333.4 is a 100 °C boiling figure. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 20 × 2834 = 56,680 J = 56.68 kJ. Invented mass.
  2. 2834 / 333.4 ≈ 8.50, about eight and a half times, using the two 0 °C Harvey figures.
  3. 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ is a specific heat, energy per gram per kelvin to change the temperature of ice Ih at 0 °C. 2834 J/g is a phase-transition energy for sublimation, with no temperature change in that account.
  4. 56,680 / 2834 = 20 g. Invented sample.

Literature · Charon, and the shore of Acheron (Inferno III.70–111)

Check. 1. On a great river’s bank. “The dismal shore of Acheron.” 2. “Woe unto you, ye souls depraved!” He tells the living speaker to withdraw from these people, who are dead; then, when the speaker does not, that he shall come by other ways and other ports, in a lighter vessel. 3. “Vex thee not, Charon; / It is so willed there where is power to do / That which is willed; and farther question not.” “Charon the demon, with the eyes of glede.” 4. Model: Charon (Inferno III). Own words should match this idea: Charon, in Longfellow’s Inferno III.82–99, is “an old man, hoary with the hair of eld,” coming in a boat, crying, “Woe unto you, ye souls depraved!” He is later “the ferryman of the livid fen, / Who round about his eyes had wheels of flame,” and “Charon the demon, with the eyes of glede.” The guide says, “Vex thee not, Charon; / It is so willed there where is power to do / That which is willed; and farther question not.” Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s neutrals, or at the gate. Not a later commentary biography imported as if it were in the set lines. Not yet the leaves simile of 112–117, which Thursday continues with, or the “My son” of 121, also reserved for Thursday. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. “The ferryman of the livid fen, / Who round about his eyes had wheels of flame.”
  2. Their color changed and they gnashed their teeth; they blasphemed God and their progenitors, and the human race, the place, the time, the seed of their engendering and of their birth; then they drew back, bitterly weeping, to the accursed shore.
  3. He beats with his oar whoever lags behind, after beckoning and collecting them.
  4. Lines 112–136: the leaves simile, the “My son” speech, and the close of the canto, when the speaker falls as one whom sleep has seized.

Elective · Music: a sequence, a first look

Check. 1. A short figure repeated at a different pitch. A rising sequence starts the repeat higher. 2. A cadence is a close, already taught. A sequence is a figure heard again, moved. They are different jobs. 3. A parallel period’s consequent begins like the antecedent, as last week taught. A sequence moves a figure to a new pitch, and may occur inside a phrase, not only at a period’s opening. 4. Model: musical sequence. Own words should match this idea: A musical sequence, in this lesson’s school sense, is a short figure or phrase that is then repeated at a different pitch, usually starting a step or more higher or lower, so that the shape is heard again, moved. Classroom examples in this lesson are invented school figures, labeled invented, not quotations from a copyrighted score. Near-miss: Not a first look at a period, or at last week’s contrasting and parallel openings, though a consequent may happen to sequence a figure from the antecedent. Not a first look at a cadence. Not every later textbook’s extra names for kinds of sequence, which are reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Two clearly labeled invented examples, one of each.
  2. The figures are classroom inventions for the method. This course does not copy a copyrighted score as the model.
  3. The period, with antecedent and consequent, and last week’s parallel and contrasting names, were already taught. Today is a different tool.
  4. This lesson teaches the sequence as a figure repeated at a new pitch, rising or falling. Further types remain reserved.
