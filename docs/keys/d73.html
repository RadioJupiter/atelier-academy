Latin · Capitulum XIII: the named day, and prīdiē

Check. 1. 31 December: the Kalends named are the first of January, so the day before them is the last day of December. 2. Prīdiē takes the accusative (Īdūs Mārtiās), not the ablative. The ablative is for the named day itself. 3. Model: the ablative of the named day. Own words should match this idea: The Kalends, Nones, or Ides themselves are expressed in the ablative, with the month adjective agreeing: Kalendīs Mārtiīs, Nonīs Aprīlibus, Īdibus Octōbribus. Near-miss: Not the accusative, which is used after prīdiē and in ante-diem phrases, and not a singular ablative *Kalendā. These words stay plural. prīdiē. Own words should match this idea: “On the day before,” used with the accusative of Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs (and the agreeing month): prīdiē Īdūs Mārtiās is 14 March. Near-miss: Not the named day itself (that is ablative), and not “two days before,” which is a later inclusive count. Prīdiē is only the day immediately before. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Nonīs Octōbribus (ablative), 7 October; prīdiē Nonās Octōbrēs (accusative), 6 October, because October is one of the four later months.
  2. A model: Īdibus Mārtiīs puella in hortō ambulat. Other original sentences are acceptable if the ablative date is formed correctly.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXII: the area of a right triangle

Check. 1. A = ½ × 7 × 24 = 84. 2. 24 is 4 times 6, which matches the square of the scale factor 2. Areas scale by the square of the length factor; this is a check, not a new theorem. 3. Model: area of a right triangle. Own words should match this idea: In a right triangle the two legs are perpendicular, so either leg may be taken as the base and the other as the height. The area is therefore ½ab, where a and b are the legs. Near-miss: Not ½ times a leg times the hypotenuse, and not the product of the two legs without the one-half. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A = ½ × 8 × 15 = 60.
  2. 17 is the hypotenuse, not a leg. The legs are 8 and 15.

Writing & Rhetoric · The false dilemma

Check. 1. For the property “is right-angled,” those two options really do exhaust the cases. No third value of that property has been left out. 2. A model: “A triangle may be a 3-4-5 triangle, or another right triangle, or not a right triangle.” Other honest rewrites accepted. 3. Model: false dilemma. Own words should match this idea: A fallacy in which two options are presented as if they were the only ones, when at least one other genuine option has been left out. Near-miss: Not a genuine either–or, in which the two options really do exhaust the cases (for example, a triangle either is or is not right-angled). Leaving out a real option is the flaw. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model (other honest inventions accepted): “Either the month is March, or the Īdūs fall on the 13th.” Left out: May, July, and October, whose Īdūs also fall on the 15th, and the other months whose Īdūs fall on the 13th without being “not-March” as a single lump if the claim is sloppy. A cleaner miss to name: “Either it is one of the four later months, or the Īdūs are on the 13th” is actually exhaustive and not a false dilemma. Prefer: “Either a month is named Iūlius, or it was never renamed,” which leaves out Augustus.
  2. A straw man distorts another person’s claim and answers the distortion. A false dilemma shrinks the field of options and pretends the remaining two are the only ones. They are different flaws.

Physics · Finding a center of mass by two suspensions

Check. 1. At the crossing of the two plumb lines. 2. If both hangings used the same point, you would draw the same line twice and would still have only a line, not a crossing. 3. Model: locating a center of mass by suspension. Own words should match this idea: A method for finding the center of mass of a flat object: hang it from one point, mark the vertical plumb line through that point; hang it from a second point, mark that second plumb line; the center of mass lies where the two lines meet. Near-miss: Not a single hanging, which only gives one line that the center of mass lies on, and not a claim that the crossing is an official laboratory measurement of a named object. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. No official mass is given; the demonstration is invented. The method locates the center of mass as a point on the card.
  2. Last week a vertical line was drawn down from a known center of mass to test the base. Today a vertical line is drawn up from a hanging in order to find that same kind of point. The vertical is the shared idea; the job (testing a base, versus locating the point) is different.

History · The church’s height, proportion, and light, in Dewing I.1.27–30

Check. 1. It “abounds exceedingly in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble,” and “one might say that its interior is not illuminated from without by the sun, but that the radiance comes into being within it.” 2. It is Dewing’s British spelling, kept inside the quotation. A paraphrase may use “marvelous.” 3. Model: proportion of breadth and length (Procopius I.1.28). Own words should match this idea: Procopius’s claim, in Dewing’s I.1.28, that the church’s breadth and length “have been so carefully proportioned, that it may not improperly be said to be exceedingly long and at the same time unusually broad.” Near-miss: Not an official measurement taken from a later editor’s plan, and not a height in numbers. This lesson invents no length, breadth, or height. light in the church (Procopius I.1.29–30). Own words should match this idea: Procopius’s claim, in Dewing’s I.1.29–30, that the church “abounds exceedingly in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble,” so that “one might say that its interior is not illuminated from without by the sun, but that the radiance comes into being within it.” Near-miss: Not a modern lighting study, and not a measurement invented from the word “abundance.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. “As from a watch-tower.” The image describes a view, not a measured height, and no official height is invented from it.
  2. Last week’s set lines named the fire and the decision to rebuild “so finely shaped.” These lines describe the rebuilt church’s form and light, still without numbers.

Atelier · Value on the nose, as a wedge

Check. 1. The head is under one light. A second invented light on the nose alone would break the pass. 2. Lashes and a new medium are not today’s job. The side plane of the nose, in value, is. 3. Model: value on the nose, as a wedge. Own words should match this idea: The already-placed nose, treated as a wedge, takes different values on its front plane and its side plane, with a terminator at the turn, according to the same light used on the sockets. Near-miss: Not a new placement of the nose, not a central ridge drawn as a line down the middle with no planes, and not a single flat tone over the whole wedge. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. At the turn between the front plane and the side plane, where the form goes out of the light.
  2. The mouth, already placed as two masses, and then the ear on the side plane, in the same value pass.

Modern language · Spanish: ser, ir, and hacer in the preterite, contrasted

Check. 1. ser/ir: fui, fue, fueron. hacer: hice, hizo, hicieron. 2. A model: Ayer fuimos al parque. Anoche hicimos la tarea. Other short correct sentences accepted.

  1. Fue is the preterite of ser or ir, not of hacer. The form needed is hizo.
  2. Each heard form used in a short sentence, with the verb labeled. Model: Fue (ser) difícil; ella hizo (hacer) un dibujo; fueron (ir) al parque.

Elective · Art history: light and marble in the church, from Dewing I.1.29–30

Check. 1. “The reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble,” or a short faithful paraphrase of that phrase. 2. Last week described the church as a building after it had been fired and rebuilt “so finely shaped.” Today describes the rebuilt church in sunlight and in reflected light. 3. Model: light and marble in the church (Procopius I.1.29–30). Own words should match this idea: Procopius’s description, in Dewing I.1.29–30, of the church as abounding “in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble,” so that the radiance might seem to come into being within it. Near-miss: Not an official luminance, window-count, or plan measurement, and not a reteaching of last week’s fire and rebuilding as a first look. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The plan is a later editorial addition, not a figure from Procopius in the set extract, and official counts are not invented from it.
  2. A model: that the church “soars to a height to match the sky,” or that breadth and length are carefully proportioned. Those claims are in I.1.27–28, used in history, still without numbers.
