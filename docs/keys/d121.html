Latin · The pluperfect of a deponent, a first look

Check. 1. Locūtus eram, I had spoken; locūtus erās, you (s.) had spoken; locūtus erat, he or she had spoken; locūtī erāmus, we had spoken; locūtī erātis, you (pl.) had spoken; locūtī erant, they had spoken. 2. The speaking is already completed before the silence. Loquēbātur would be ongoing speaking, I was speaking or used to speak. Locūtus est is the perfect, he spoke or has spoken, not marked as prior to another past moment. 3. Puerō locūtō is an already-taught absolute, a loosely attached phrase with no sum. It is not a tense of a main-clause verb. 4. Model: pluperfect of a deponent. Own words should match this idea: The pluperfect of a deponent uses the already-taught perfect participle with the imperfect of sum, and it still looks passive and is translated as active: locūtus eram, locūtus erās, locūtus erat, locūtī erāmus, locūtī erātis, locūtī erant. The meaning is I had spoken, you had spoken, and the rest. The action is presented as already completed before another past moment. Near-miss: Not the perfect locūtus sum, I spoke or I have spoken, which uses the same participle with the present of sum. Not the imperfect loquēbar, I was speaking or I used to speak, which has no participle. Not the already-taught absolute puerō locūtō, which has no sum and is not a main-clause tense. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. I had spoken (pluperfect). I was speaking or I used to speak (imperfect). I spoke or I have spoken (perfect). 2. A fair sentence has the pluperfect for the earlier completed action and another past verb after it, for example: Puer locūtus erat, et tum scripsit. Do not copy the model word for word if you can make a new pair. 3. Locūtus eram. Locūtus eram is also the pluperfect that answers loquēbar in the first person; loquēbar itself has no participle to change, so the pluperfect is still locūtus eram.

Mathematics · Geometry LXVII: a line and a plane in space, a first look

Check. 1. The line may lie in the plane, meet the plane at exactly one point, or be parallel to the plane and never meet it. 2. AB lies in the floor; DA meets the floor at A; DC is parallel to the floor. 3. A value plane in the atelier is a flat turn of a form under light. The floor of the box is a geometric plane in space, used to study how a line meets it. 4. Model: a line and a plane in space. Own words should match this idea: In space, a line and a plane have three possible relations: the line may lie in the plane, meet the plane at exactly one point, or be parallel to the plane and never meet it. These are relations in three dimensions, not a new kind of plane drawn as a value plane in a head study. Near-miss: Not a line drawn on a sheet of paper as if the paper were the only plane in the world. Not the already-taught value planes of a form in the atelier. Not a claim about an official room measurement; the box is an invented model. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. EF lies in the floor. CB meets the floor at B. 2. A fair answer is the top back edge, or a top side edge: each stays a constant height above the floor and does not meet it. 3. The box is an invented model. No official room measurement is being used or claimed.

Writing · A fable, a first look: Townsend’s Crow and the Pitcher

Check. 1. A short invented narrative, often with animals that act, and a stated moral after the action. 2. The crow drops stones one by one into the pitcher until the water is within reach, and so saves its life. The moral is “Necessity is the mother of invention.” 3. The moral is the fable’s closing claim, not a measured physical law. The crow in the fable is a character in a short narrative, not a source for an official fact about living birds. 4. Model: fable (progymnasmata). Own words should match this idea: A fable, as the first of the classical progymnasmata, is a short invented narrative, often with animals that act and speak, that ends in a stated moral. The narrative is short and complete; the moral is named after the action, not lectured in the middle of it. Near-miss: Not a long short story, and not a précis of a history chapter. Not a moral announced before any action has happened. Not a new official proverb claimed as a law of nature. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. A crow, perishing with thirst, sees a pitcher, flies to it, finds too little water to reach, tries everything in vain, collects stones, drops them one by one, brings the water within reach, and so saves its life. 2. After the action, not in the middle of it, so the narrative stays a narrative and the moral is named once, at the end. 3. A long short story, or a précis of a history chapter, or a moral with no action. A fable is short, invented, and ends in a stated moral.

Physics · Superposition, and constructive and destructive interference, a first look

Check. 1. When two or more waves arrive at the same point, their disturbances add. The resulting disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. 2. Amplitude 2X, and the same wavelength as either wave. 3. Two identical waves arrive exactly out of phase, crest with trough, and the resulting amplitude is zero. 2X is the pure constructive result, not the destructive one. 4. Model: superposition. Own words should match this idea: When two or more waves arrive at the same point, their disturbances are superimposed: the resulting disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. If the disturbances are along the same line, the amplitudes add, with sign. Near-miss: Not a replacement of one wave by the other, and not a claim that energy is created. Not the reserved first look at beats, and not an official speed of sound or of light invented today. constructive interference. Own words should match this idea: Pure constructive interference, in OpenStax 16.10, is the superposition of two identical waves that arrive exactly in phase, crest aligned with crest and trough with trough. The result is a wave of twice the amplitude and the same wavelength. Near-miss: Not destructive interference, in which crest lines up with trough and the resulting amplitude is zero. Not a change of wavelength. Not an official loudness of a real stereo, which the text uses only as an illustration of mixed interference. destructive interference. Own words should match this idea: Pure destructive interference, in OpenStax 16.10, is the superposition of two identical waves that arrive exactly out of phase, crest aligned with trough. The disturbances are in opposite directions, and the resulting amplitude is zero: the waves cancel. Near-miss: Not constructive interference, which doubles the amplitude. Not a claim that the two waves have ceased to exist elsewhere, or that energy has been destroyed as a new official law. The reserved standing wave and beats are later in the week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Zero. The waves cancel at that point. 2. Pure constructive and pure destructive interference require precisely aligned identical waves. Most waves are not identical, or not exactly in or out of phase, and the mix can vary from place to place. 3. Standing waves and beats, and any official speed of sound or of light.

History · Inter Caetera, May 3, 1493: the recital of the voyage, a first look

Check. 1. The bull Inter Caetera of May 3, 1493, in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 translation, given in Rome at St. Peter’s on that date. 2. It claims “certain very remote islands and even mainlands, that hitherto had not been discovered by others,” and, in the same sentence, that “therein dwell very many peoples.” 3. The first-voyage letter, the May 4 bull, and any line of leagues. Invent no official island count and no official quantity of gold. 4. Model: Inter Caetera (May 3, 1493). Own words should match this idea: Inter Caetera of May 3, 1493, is a papal bull of Alexander VI, used here in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 English translation. It addresses the sovereigns of Castile and León, recounts a westward voyage under Christopher Columbus after the recovery of Granada, and later in the week grants lands “hitherto discovered” and “to be discovered hereafter,” with a proviso about Christian owners. The wording is a claim in a legal and religious document, not a map and not a voyage journal. Near-miss: Not the later May 4 bull, and not a line of demarcation, both of which remain reserved. Not the first-voyage letter, which also remains reserved. Not an official count of islands or an official quantity of gold. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. After “the siege and recovery of the kingdom itself of Granada.” 2. It is the bull’s wording, in a grant. The same sentence already names peoples living there. You are reading a claim, not certifying a map. 3. Term 3 history begins with this grant. Dewing I.3 is taken up in this week’s art-history seminar, as the reserved next section of Buildings.

So that mixing and value are learned with a small set, not hidden by a large store of hues. A portrait is a later study; today is a first layout and two mixes. 3. Modern language · Spanish: the simple future of a regular -ar verb, a first look

Check. 1. The whole infinitive is kept, and the endings -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án are added. Hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablaréis, hablarán. 2. A fair pair: Mañana hablaré con el profesor. Mañana hablaremos en la clase. Do not invent an official appointment. 3. Hablo is present, I speak. Voy a hablar is the already-taught near future. Hablaré is the simple future, I will speak. Irregular futures such as haré and iré remain reserved. 4. Model: simple future of a regular verb. Own words should match this idea: The simple future of a regular Spanish verb keeps the whole infinitive and adds the endings -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án. So hablar becomes hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablaréis, hablarán: I will speak, you will speak, and the rest. The same endings will be used tomorrow with a regular -er or -ir infinitive. Near-miss: Not the already-taught present hablo, I speak, or the near future that uses ir a. Not an irregular future such as haré or iré, which remains reserved. Not a voice code written in the meaning column of a wordlist. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. You (s., informal) will speak; he or she will speak (or you, s., formal); they will speak (or you, pl., formal). 2. A fair sentence: Mañana hablaré. (Hoy hablo is present.) 3. In this tense the endings are added to the infinitive as a whole, not to a shortened stem as in the present.

Seminar · Logic: equally likely outcomes, a first look

Check. 1. Each distinct outcome has the same chance. If there are n equally likely outcomes, and one must happen, the probability of one named outcome is 1/n. 2. 1/6 for the face 4; 3/6 = 1/2 for an even face. 3. No evidence for an official weather probability is being used, and none should be invented. The figures are invented classroom models, not a game of chance to be played for a stake. 4. Model: equally likely outcomes. Own words should match this idea: Outcomes are equally likely when each of the distinct possible results of a chance process has the same chance. If there are n such outcomes, and one of them is named, its probability is 1/n, provided the outcomes really are equally likely and one of them must happen. Near-miss: Not an official probability of a real event, such as weather or a living person’s future, invented without evidence. Not a lesson in gambling. Not a claim that a real cube in the room has been tested as fair. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. 1/8; 2/8 = 1/4. 2. 3/6 = 1/2. 1/3 would be a miss if you treated the three marked faces as if there were only three faces on the cube, or as if the unmarked faces did not count as outcomes. 3. Outcomes that are not equally likely, and the later work on evidence, remain reserved.
