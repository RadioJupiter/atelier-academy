Latin · Capitulum VI, part 2: town names, quō and unde

Check. 1. Puer ad vīllam it. Puer Rōmam it. 2. Mārcus Tusculō venit. 3. Model: town names (motion). Own words should match this idea: With the names of towns, cities, and small islands, Latin usually drops the preposition: the accusative alone means ‘to’ (Rōmam it), and the ablative alone means ‘from’ (Rōmā venit). Common nouns still need ad, ab, or ex (ad vīllam, ab oppidō). Near-miss: Rōmam is not automatically ‘in Rome’, and ad Rōmam is the form this chapter tells you not to use for the city itself. The exception applies to town names, not to vīlla or hortus. unde. Own words should match this idea: The Latin question-word meaning ‘where from?’, asking for a motion-from answer: ab or ex + ablative for common nouns, and the bare ablative for town names. Near-miss: unde is not ubi and not quō. English ‘where’ covers all three, which is why they have to be practiced as three different Latin questions. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Rōmam, Rōmā; ad vīllam, ab vīllā (or ex vīllā).
  2. Mārcus Rōmam it; puer ad vīllam it.
  3. For example, Iūlia Rōmam it.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. Town names use the bare ablative for motion from: Rōmā. (The book’s rule is the exception; keep ab for common nouns.)

Mathematics · Algebra V: the laws of exponents

Check. 1. 2⁵⁺³⁻⁴ = 2⁴ = 16. 2. 5⁰ = 1; 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8. 3. Model: exponent. Own words should match this idea: The small number that says how many times a base is used as a factor: in aⁿ, a is the base and n is the exponent. For a positive integer n, aⁿ = a · a · … · a (n factors). Near-miss: The exponent is not the same as the base, and a² is not ‘a times 2’. Also, (ab)ⁿ is not a·bⁿ unless a = 1 or n = 1. power. Own words should match this idea: The result of raising a base to an exponent, or the expression aⁿ itself. The laws of powers are: aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0), (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ, (ab)ⁿ = aⁿ bⁿ, and a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0), a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0). Near-miss: A power is not addition: 2³ + 2⁴ is not 2⁷. You can add exponents only when you multiply powers of the same base. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 2⁵⁻²⁻¹ = 2² = 4.
  2. 2³ · 2⁵ = 2⁸ = 256; 2³ + 2⁵ = 8 + 32 = 40. The product is larger.
  3. 3⁻³.
  4. a³ b⁶ · a⁻¹ = a² b⁶.
  5. n groups of m factors is mn factors. Then aᵏ · a⁻ⁿ = aᵏ⁻ⁿ must hold; taking k = n gives aⁿ · a⁻ⁿ = a⁰ = 1, so a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.

Writing & Rhetoric · From one paragraph to two: the two-paragraph outline

Check. 1. A model: thesis as given. ¶1: common nouns — ad vīllam, in vīllā, ab vīllā. ¶2: town names — Rōmam, Rōmae, Rōmā. Concluding sentence: the three place-questions stay the same; only the construction changes. 2. A revision might be: ‘The three place-questions are unchanged; only the presence of the preposition changes, and only for town names.’ 3. Model: two-paragraph outline. Own words should match this idea: A short plan for a piece that has two paragraphs, each with its own topic sentence and supporting points, under one overall thesis. It is the single-paragraph outline (Day 6) used twice, plus a sentence that says how the two paragraphs work together. Near-miss: It is not two separate short essays, and it is not one long paragraph cut in half. If the second paragraph could be first without any loss, the outline has not yet said why the order exists. concluding sentence. Own words should match this idea: The last sentence of a paragraph or of a short essay that does not merely repeat the topic sentence, but closes the thought — often by stating a consequence, a limit, or a return to the thesis in new words. Near-miss: A concluding sentence is not ‘In conclusion, as I said…’ and it is not a new claim that would need its own paragraph. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete outline, saved.
  2. Supporting points are phrases that serve the topic sentence.
  3. The sentence names a contrast, a second source, a consequence, or a limit.
  4. The kept sentence closes the thesis.
  5. The thesis is arguable.

Physics · Uniform circular motion and centripetal force

Check. 1. a = 20²/50 = 8 m/s². F_net = 800 × 8 = 6400 N, toward the center, supplied mainly by friction at the tires (on a level road). 2. v = 2π × 2 / 1 = 4π m/s. a = (4π)² / 2 = 16π² / 2 = 8π² m/s², or about 80 m/s² if π² ≈ 10. 3. Model: centripetal acceleration. Own words should match this idea: The inward, center-seeking acceleration of a body that moves at constant speed on a circle, of size a = v²/r (or a = 4π²r/T², where T is the period). The velocity’s direction is changing even when the speed is not, so there is an acceleration. Near-miss: Centripetal acceleration is not ‘a force that appears in circular motion’, and it is not outward. The word names the acceleration; the force that produces it has a separate name and a real source (tension, friction, gravity). centripetal force. Own words should match this idea: The net force toward the center that produces the centripetal acceleration: F_net, inward = m v²/r. It is not a new kind of force; it is the net inward force, supplied by whatever is actually pushing or pulling inward. Near-miss: There is no outward ‘centrifugal force’ acting on the moving body in an inertial frame. The feeling of being ‘thrown out’ is the body’s inertia (Day 6) trying to go in a straight line. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. In an inertial frame the body tends to go straight (inertia). The inward force bends the path; an extra outward force would be invented, not measured.
  2. a = v²/r is halved.
  3. C. With no inward force, Newton’s first law says the body continues in a straight line, along the tangent at the moment the string breaks.
  4. T = m v²/r = 0.5 × 16 / 1 = 8 N.
  5. Car: friction (and, on a banked curve, a component of the normal force, which is a later refinement). Moon: Earth’s gravity.

History · Alexander and the end of the Achaemenid empire

Check. 1. 336; 334; 333; 331; 323 BCE at Babylon. 2. He prefers Ptolemy and Aristobulus, companions of Alexander, and claims to follow the more trustworthy account. (The exact wording depends on the translation; the idea should be right.) 3. Model: Alexander of Macedon. Own words should match this idea: The Macedonian king who, from 336 BCE (the death of his father, Philip II) until his own death at Babylon in 323 BCE, conquered the Achaemenid Persian empire. The main campaign in Asia ran from 334 BCE to his death. These dates, unlike Old Kingdom years, are comparatively secure. Near-miss: Alexander is not ‘the first king of Egypt’ (Egypt had had kings for many centuries), and he is not a Roman. The world he entered was the Late Achaemenid empire, already itself a successor of earlier Near Eastern states you have met. Arrian. Own words should match this idea: A Greek historian (2nd century CE) whose Anabasis of Alexander is a main surviving narrative of the campaign. Arrian says he relied especially on the accounts of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, companions of Alexander. He is therefore a later writer using earlier, now mostly lost, eyewitness narratives. Near-miss: Arrian is not a journalist at Issus, and he is not a Persian source. A complete picture would also need other voices (for example, later the Babylonian astronomical diaries, and Persian evidence), which this lesson only flags. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete five-line note, with the limit that Arrian is late and that speeches may be composed.
  2. The treaty of year 21 is New Kingdom (conventionally mid-13th century BCE); Alexander is four centuries before the Common Era — many centuries later.
  3. They belong to a densely attested Greek and Near Eastern chronology of the 4th century BCE, not to a reconstructed Old Kingdom timeline.
  4. For example, a Babylonian chronicle or astronomical diary, or Persian administrative evidence, or archaeology at a named site.
  5. An arguable thesis, not a topic label.

Atelier · Combining solids: a small still life

Check. 1. Overlap is one form hiding part of another. The hidden part is constructed so the visible fragment belongs to a whole solid, and so the join is true. 2. By the direction and length of the cast shadows, by the placement of highlights and terminators, and by whether the light and shadow families agree. If one object looks lit from the other side, the group has split. 3. Model: overlap. Own words should match this idea: One form partly hiding another, which both creates a depth cue and forces you to measure where a contour stops and another begins. The hidden part of the back form is still constructed, lightly, so that the visible fragment sits on a whole solid. Near-miss: Overlap is not a smear between two objects, and it is not an excuse to leave the back object unconstructed. A broken, guessed join will flatten the group. relative value. Own words should match this idea: How light or dark a passage is compared with its neighbors, not only compared with a scale in the margin. In a group, the light plane of the cube, the light on the sphere, and a white cloth are not automatically the same step. Near-miss: Relative value is not ‘each object gets its own private scale’, and it is not ‘everything in light is the same white’. You compare across objects. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A grouped construction of three solids, with real overlap and shared light, not three separate sketches.
  2. The three marks are present and honest.
  3. Four sentences that include a real cross-object value comparison and a remaining error.
  4. A thumbnail in which the large light and shadow still read.
  5. A specific measurement (a plumb from a contact point, a table-line, a height from the table to a common landmark), or a confirmation that they already sit together.

Modern language · Spanish: reflexive verbs

Check. 1. me despierto, te despiertas, nos despertamos (ie in the first two; e in nosotros). 2. ¿Cómo te llamas? Me llamo… (no real name from this school is required; a blank or an invented given name is fine). 3. Model: reflexive verb. Own words should match this idea: A verb whose object is the same as the subject, marked by a reflexive pronoun: me, te, se, nos, os, se. The infinitive is written with -se: lavarse, levantarse, llamarse. Near-miss: A reflexive is not automatically a daily-routine verb only, and se is not a second subject. Se llama Ana is ‘she is called Ana’, not ‘Ana calls herself’ in a strained English sense — but the Spanish form is still reflexive. the article with body parts. Own words should match this idea: When the possessor is already clear from a reflexive pronoun, Spanish usually uses the definite article with a body part, not a possessive adjective. Near-miss: This is not a ban on mi/tu/su in every sentence, and it is not a new gender rule. The article still agrees with the noun: las manos, el pelo. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Me lavo las manos. / Nos levantamos temprano. (Pronoun before the verb.)
  2. Se van a ir. or Van a irse.
  3. Four grammatical reflexives, with pronouns matching the subjects.
  4. Se llama Ana; Nos llamamos… (invented class name).
  5. Me despierto, me levanto y me lavo la cara.

Logic · Necessary and sufficient conditions

Check. 1. If the angle is right, then the lines are perpendicular: R → P. Being perpendicular is necessary for the angle to be right; being right is sufficient for the lines to be perpendicular. 2. Sufficient but not necessary: being equilateral is sufficient for being isosceles, but not necessary, because a non-equilateral isosceles triangle exists. Necessary but not sufficient: being isosceles is necessary for being equilateral, but not sufficient. Another sound pair: being a quadrilateral is necessary, not sufficient, for being a square; being a square is sufficient, not necessary, for being a rectangle (in this course a square is a rectangle). 3. Model: sufficient condition. Own words should match this idea: A condition that is enough to guarantee the result: if P is sufficient for Q, then P → Q. Whenever P holds, Q holds. Near-miss: Sufficient is not the same as necessary. A condition can be enough without being the only way. A triangle can be isosceles without being equilateral. necessary condition. Own words should match this idea: A condition that must hold if the result holds: if P is necessary for Q, then Q → P (equivalently, ¬P → ¬Q). Without P, you cannot have Q. Near-miss: Necessary is not the same as sufficient. Being a quadrilateral is not enough to make a square. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. If you can construct the perpendicular bisector, then the compass opening is more than half the segment: C → H. (The original ‘only if’ is that construction only if the opening is large enough.)
  2. On the course definition they are necessary and sufficient for each other: a rhombus is a quadrilateral with all sides equal.
  3. ‘P only if Q’ is P → Q. ‘If Q then P’ is Q → P, the converse, which can fail.
  4. You can: Z ⇔ A, where Z is ‘the number is zero’ and A is ‘its absolute value is zero’. Both directions hold.
  5. A real ‘only if’ (one arrow) and a real ‘if and only if’ (both), correctly symbolized.
