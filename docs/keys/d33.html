Latin · Capitulum IX, part 3: numerals — ūnus, duo, trēs, and the rest

Check. 1. Duae puellae in hortō sunt. 2. Quattuor canēs in hortō sunt. 3. Model: declining numerals. Own words should match this idea: In Latin, ūnus, duo, and trēs change endings (they decline). Many other numerals, including quattuor, quinque, and centum, do not change. Near-miss: Not “every number word declines.” centum canēs does not become *centī canēs. And ūnus takes familiar first-and-second-declension-like endings (ūnus, ūna, ūnum). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. centum is invariable in the pattern taught today.
  2. latera takes tria; rēx takes ūnus, not ūna.
  3. duae; trēs canēs.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. A marked original sentence.

Mathematics · Algebra X: graphs of quadratic functions

Check. 1. y(3) = 5, so (3, 5) does lie on the graph (it is just not drawn in the figure’s window). 2. x² − 2x − 3 = (x − 3)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 3 and x = −1. The midpoint of −1 and 3 is 1, the axis already found. 3. Model: parabola. Own words should match this idea: The graph of a quadratic function, y = ax² + bx + c with a ≠ 0. It is a curve with a single turning point, the vertex, not a straight line. Near-miss: Not a linear graph, and not a circle. A parabola is not “any curved graph.” vertex of a parabola. Own words should match this idea: The turning point of the graph of a quadratic. For y = x² + px + q, completing the square (Day 5) shows the x-coordinate of the vertex; the y-coordinate is the value of the function there. For y = ax² + bx + c, a first formula, once the square is completed or the axis of symmetry is named, is x = −b/(2a). Near-miss: Not an x-intercept automatically (the vertex lies on the x-axis only when the root is repeated). And not the slope of a line. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Vertex (0, 0); for example (1, 1) and (−1, 1).
  2. Opens down. Vertex (0, 4).
  3. The graph is a curve, not a polygonal path through those three points.
  4. y = (x − 1)² − 4; vertex (1, −4), matching Watch me.
  5. A clear table and a marked vertex.

Writing & Rhetoric · A parenthetical interruption that does not break the series

Check. 1. A clear series, and a version with a set-off comment that survives removal. 2. A repaired series with matching parts. 3. Model: parenthetical interruption. Own words should match this idea: A word, phrase, or clause inserted into a sentence, often set off by commas or dashes, that adds a comment without taking over the grammatical job of the sentence. Near-miss: Not a new main clause that strands the series, and not a dash that begins a new subject and never returns. If the insertion changes the job, it belongs in a new sentence. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Remove the insertion; the sentence should still work.
  2. If the dash begins a new job, start a new sentence instead.
  3. One promise, one short comment.
  4. The phrase “if the rule is quadratic” (or equivalent) marked.
  5. A copied repair.

Physics · A first look at torque

Check. 1. 3.2 N·m. 2. 0 (the perpendicular distance is zero). 3. Model: torque (first look). Own words should match this idea: A measure of how effectively a force tends to turn an object about a point or an axis: the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the point (or axis) to the line of the force. In this first look, τ = F × (perpendicular distance). Near-miss: Not the same as work (also a force times a distance, but along the displacement), and not a new unit called a joule. The unit here is the newton-meter (N·m), named as a torque, not as an energy. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The torque doubles.
  2. The joule is reserved for work or energy; torque is named in newton-meters.
  3. 10 N × 0.2 m = 2 N·m.
  4. There is no net turning from those two; they balance in this first look.
  5. Work uses distance along the displacement; torque uses the perpendicular distance to the force’s line, about a pivot.

History · How we know: later writers, and a first-person record

Check. 1. About a century (they are early second century CE writers). 2. It is written in the first person as a public account of deeds, so it presents a case, not only a list. 3. Model: Res Gestae as advocacy. Own words should match this idea: The Res Gestae Divi Augusti is a first-person record of deeds attributed to Augustus, known from later copies (notably an inscription at Ankara). It is a primary statement of how he wished to be seen, and therefore advocacy as well as a record. Near-miss: Not a day-by-day chronicle written by a neutral observer, and not a modern constitution. A first-person monumental record is a kind of evidence, not the whole story. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Tacitus (later narrative), Suetonius (later lives), Res Gestae (first-person monumental record).
  2. When was it written, and what kind of text is it?
  3. A fair set: the Res Gestae is useful, because it is a first-person public record; but it is advocacy, so it cannot close the case alone.
  4. A primary source can still be a case made in public; “primary” names its nearness in kind, not its fairness.
  5. A short paragraph that keeps that promise.

Atelier · The neck, and how the head sits on it

Check. 1. A simple neck form attached to the existing head. 2. Axis checked and, if needed, corrected. 3. Model: head on the neck. Own words should match this idea: The head does not float: it sits on the neck, which has width, a slight forward or backward lean, and a connection into the shoulders. All of this is judged from the plate or plaster. Near-miss: Not a stick under a finished mask, and not a neck copied from a front-view diagram onto a turned head. If the plate shows a short, thick neck, or a long one, that sight wins. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A named meeting, not a gap or a pasted haircut.
  2. A full anatomical plate of the neck is not today’s job; a shadow or one muscle may be added if seen.
  3. One honest note.
  4. A simple ending of the neck, if the plate shows it.
  5. One correction from the side-by-side check.

Modern language · Spanish: the present of decir and venir

Check. 1. dice, dicen; viene, vienen. 2. Both have a yo form in -go and an e → ie change in the tú (and other) forms: tengo/tienes, vengo/vienes. 3. Model: present of decir and venir. Own words should match this idea: Two more irregular presents: decir (digo, dices, dice, decimos, decís, dicen) ‘to say / to tell’; venir (vengo, vienes, viene, venimos, venís, vienen) ‘to come.’ Both have a first-person -go, and venir has an e → ie change in several forms, like tener. Near-miss: Not *yo dico, *yo veno, or *yo veno. And still not a past tense (dije, vine are not today’s forms). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ¿Qué dicen? Vengo a la mesa.
  2. Both yo forms end in -go (hago, digo), against a guess such as *haco or *dico.
  3. Two heard forms, each in a correct new sentence.
  4. A short, correct four-sentence string.
  5. vienes, viene, vienen. (venimos and venís do not show ie.)

Art history · Roman art: the Augustus of Prima Porta, and the Ara Pacis

Check. 1. It can show how Augustus was presented in a commander’s portrait type. It cannot, by itself, prove a single day’s event or supply a year we have not given. 2. Voted 13 BCE; dedicated 9 BCE; both conventional. 3. Model: Augustus of Prima Porta (copy type). Own words should match this idea: A marble statue of Augustus, now in the Vatican Museums, generally treated as a copy of a (lost) bronze type rather than as the unique original bronze in our hands. This lesson does not fix a single year for the marble copy. Near-miss: Not a Greek original of the fifth century BCE, and not a work we should date to 27 BCE just because that year was this week’s conventional start of the principate. A portrait type can be later than the political moment it celebrates. Ara Pacis (conventional dates). Own words should match this idea: The Ara Pacis Augustae, an altar in Rome associated with Augustan peace, conventionally voted in 13 BCE and dedicated in 9 BCE. Those years are a conventional scholarly range, not a classroom measurement. Near-miss: Not a Republican work of the Punic Wars, and not a building dated to Actium (31 BCE) or to 27 BCE simply because those dates were taught this week. Use the conventional 13–9 BCE pair, labeled as conventional. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Both are public, first-person or official presentations that make a case, not only a neutral list of facts.
  2. The altar has been moved and reconstructed; the modern display is not the Augustan siting unchanged.
  3. A short paragraph that keeps that promise, using one of the two works.
  4. No unsourced year or unsourced measurement presented as a fact of the lesson.
  5. A complete copied reminder.
