Latin · Secūtus sum and ēgressus sum, mixed with already-taught deponent tenses

Check. 1. Sequor, I follow; sequar, I shall follow; secūtus sum, I followed, or I have followed; secūtus eram, I had followed. 2. Puella ēgressa est: she went out, or has gone out. Puer eam sequētur: he will follow her. 3. Sequuntur is the present, they follow, not the perfect secūtī sunt.

Exercises. 1. Ēgressī sumus; sequētur. 2. A fair pair would keep secūtī sumus, we have followed, in the perfect, and ēgrediar, I shall go out, in the future. 3. The lesson uses Puer and Puella as the Latin words already taught for that work, so an unquoted English “boy” or “girl” is not added as if it were the Latin.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXXV: sine and cosine at the familiar angles, now in radians

Check. 1. sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1; sin(π/6) = 1/2, cos(π/6) = √3/2; sin(π/4) = cos(π/4) = √2/2; sin(π/3) = √3/2, cos(π/3) = 1/2; sin(π/2) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0; sin π = 0, cos π = −1. 2. sin(π − π/3) = sin(2π/3) = sin(π/3) = √3/2, because sin(π − θ) = sin θ. 3. The values were already taught on the unit circle in degrees. Today the same angles are named in radians.

Exercises. 1. cos(π − π/3) = cos(2π/3) = −cos(π/3) = −1/2, because cos(π − θ) = −cos θ. 2. 210° = 210 × π/180 = 7π/6 radians. Sin 210° = sin(180° + 30°) = −sin 30° = −1/2, so sin(7π/6) = −1/2. 3. The ambiguous case remains reserved.

Writing · A short original refutation, on a second invented claim

Check. 1. A fair answer is one clearly invented claim and two short reasons, labeled invented throughout. 2. It examines a claim, with reasons. A maxim is a precept; a chreia needs a person, an occasion, and a saying. 3. The claim is invented classroom work, not a newly discovered proverb, and not a biography.

Exercises. 1. Remove the living person’s name; keep only a general invented claim, labeled invented. 2. A second claim should still be general, short, labeled invented, and answered with a reason. 3. Confirmation remains reserved.

Physics · Official Example 17.4: a 150 Hz train horn

Check. 1. f_s = 150 Hz, v_s = 35.0 m/s, v_w = 340 m/s, all official in Example 17.4. 2. 167 Hz approaching, 136 Hz receding. The shifts are 17.0 Hz toward and 14.0 Hz away. 3. 150 Hz, because the relative velocity between engineer and horn is zero, and f_obs = f_s.

Exercises. 1. The moving-observer formula: f_obs = 150 × (340 + 35.0) / 340 = 150 × 375 / 340 ≈ 165 Hz. The 150 Hz and 340 m/s are official in the example; the 35.0 m/s as an observer speed, and this use of the observer formula, are invented classroom work, labeled invented, not official Example 17.4 part (a). 2. The two official calculations use 340 − 35.0 and 340 + 35.0 in the denominator, which are not reciprocals of each other, so the shifts differ: 17.0 Hz and 14.0 Hz. 3. Sonic booms wait until Friday. Official frequencies of trains not in the example are not invented.

History · The first-voyage letter: May foliage, November birds, harbors, and rivers bearing gold

Check. 1. The trees were “as green and luxuriant as in Spain in the month of May,” and he is told they “never lose their foliage.” Nightingales and “other birds of a thousand different kinds” were singing “in November.” 2. “Española is a wonder,” rich for planting and for “building towns and villages.” The harbors, and the rivers, “most of them bearing gold,” “surpass anything that would be believed by one who had not seen them.” 3. They are the letter’s wording in this translation, not an official weight, census, or species count, and none is invented.

Exercises. 1. “There are palm-trees of six or eight kinds, wonderful in their beautiful variety.” “Six or eight kinds” is the letter’s wording, not an official botanical list. 2. The letter is a voyage report, earlier than the June 7, 1494 treaty that later divided lands. 3. Do not add official modern identifications, official gold quantities, or conversions of the letter’s leagues.

Atelier · The bounce and the core shadow, kept as one turn

Check. 1. In the shadow, darker than the half and darker than the true light; the core shadow stays the darkest of those passages. 2. Keep it as a softer step, checked on the scale, not as a hard band of light. 3. The object is a simple still-life form, not a head from life. The bounce is mixed from paints already in the four.

Exercises. 1. A fair order blocks the light, the half, and the core shadow first, then adds a quieter bounce, so the bounce cannot steal the light’s value. 2. A highlight belongs on the light side. A bounce painted as a highlight pulls a second light into the shadow and flattens or splits the form. 3. A portrait and a fifth tube remain reserved.

Modern language · Spanish: the present subjunctive of estar, a first look

Check. 1. Esté, estés, esté, estemos, estéis, estén. 2. He or she wants us to be ready. Estamos is the present indicative, not the subjunctive estemos. 3. Sea is the subjunctive of ser. For a “be here” frame with estar, the form is esté. 4. Model: present subjunctive of estar. Own words should match this idea: The present subjunctive of estar is irregular: esté, estés, esté, estemos, estéis, estén. It is not the present indicative estoy or está, not the future estará, not the conditional estaría, and not sea or vaya. Near-miss: Not está, the present indicative. Not estará, the future. Not estaría, the conditional. Not sea, the present subjunctive of ser, a different verb. Not vaya. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. That you (s.) be; that they be. Fair invented sentences: “Quiere que tú estés aquí.” “Quiere que ellos estén listos.” 2. She is; she will be; she would be; that she be (present subjunctive of estar). 3. Haya, and other irregular subjunctives not yet taught, remain reserved.

Seminar · Art history: Dewing, Buildings I.4.1–8, Peter and Paul, and Saints Sergius and Bacchus

Check. 1. Alongside the residence “called by the name of Hormisdas.” “When he became Emperor of the Romans he joined it to the Palace proper.” 2. A shrine “to the famous Saints Sergius and Bacchus,” and “another shrine which stood at an angle to this one.” The two “share the same entrances,” and “each of them is found to be neither superior nor inferior to the other either in beauty or in size.” 3. One long axis is “built straight,” the other has columns “for the most part in a semi-circle.” They share propylaea and a court, and belong to the Palace. Do not invent official lengths or official column counts. 4. Model: the churches of Peter and Paul and of Saints Sergius and Bacchus (Dewing, Buildings I.4.1–8). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, Buildings I.4.1–8, Justinian builds a church of Peter and Paul at the residence once called Hormisdas, later joined to the Palace, and also a shrine of Saints Sergius and Bacchus, with another shrine at an angle. The two churches “share the same entrances,” and “each of them is found to be neither superior nor inferior to the other either in beauty or in size.” Official lengths are not invented. Near-miss: Not last week’s rebuilt shrine of Michael, a different extract. Not Buildings I.4.9 onward, including the later church of the Apostles, which remain reserved. Not an official length, official area, or official column count. Not a living building being measured in the classroom. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. “These two churches are so admirable that they manifestly form an adornment of the whole city, and not merely of the Palace.” 2. Last week rebuilt the Michael shrine in I.3.16–18. Today’s extract is a first look at I.4.1–8, a different pair of churches at Hormisdas. 3. Buildings I.4.9 onward, including the later church of the Apostles, and official measurements, remain reserved.
