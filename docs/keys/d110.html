Latin · Friday mix: the deponent perfect, with present and absolute contrasts

Check. 1. Locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt. Cōnātus sum, cōnātus es, cōnātus est, cōnātī sumus, cōnātī estis, cōnātī sunt. I spoke or have spoken, and so on; I tried or have tried, and so on. 2. I spoke, or have spoken: perfect. We speak: present. 3. Puerī is plural, so the participle is plural. 4. Recitante is contemporaneous with the main verb. Captā is already complete when the main verb happens. Both are ablative absolutes, with no sum. The perfect tense is locūtus est, with est.

  1. You (singular) tried, or have tried: perfect. You (plural) try, or are trying: present.
  2. With the boy having spoken, the schoolmaster answers. A perfect-participle ablative absolute.
  3. The participle must be locūtī to agree with nōs.
  4. That present table was taught last week. This week’s first look is the perfect.

Physics · Friday mix: forced oscillations, resonance, and the shape of the peak

Check. 1. A forced oscillation is driven by a periodic driving force. The driving frequency belongs to that force. The natural frequency is the frequency the system would have with no driving and no damping. 2. Driving at the natural frequency. Energy is transferred most efficiently, and the amplitude is greatest for that system. 3. The peak is higher and narrower. Heavy damping lowers the amplitude and makes the system respond at more frequencies. 4. OpenStax 16.8 does not give those official figures for use as classroom measurements.

  1. Those names were taught last week from 16.7. This week used damping only to read the shape of the 16.8 peak.
  2. The amplitude is smaller; energy is transferred less efficiently than at resonance.
  3. So that it can resonate strongly at a very specific frequency.
  4. Section 16.8 does not set out such a formula to be used here.

Modern language · Spanish: Friday mix of iba, era, and veía

Check. 1. Iba, ibas, iba, íbamos, ibais, iban. Era, eras, era, éramos, erais, eran. Veía, veías, veía, veíamos, veíais, veían. 2. Iba: I was going. Era: I was. Veía: I was seeing, or used to see. Ir and ser share the already-taught preterite fui. 3. Sample: Cada mañana yo iba. Ayer yo fui. Or: Cada mañana yo veía. Ayer yo vi. 4. Vía would drop the e that veía keeps. The first-person and third-person singular share era; the subject, or the rest of the sentence, tells them apart.

  1. Every morning we used to go, or we were going: imperfect. Yesterday we went: preterite.
  2. Veía / vi (I was seeing, or used to see / I saw). Éramos / fuimos (we were, a past state / we were, or we went, a completed event).
  3. The imperfect, in this first look, is for ongoing or habitual past action, or for a past state with era. The preterite is for a completed event.
  4. Those regular imperfects were taught last week. Fui and vi were already taught as preterites. This week’s first looks are iba, era, and veía.

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: the curves of the vertebral column, from Gray 1918

Check. 1. Cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and pelvic. 2. The cervical and the lumbar are convex forward, or convex anteriorly. The cervical is the least marked of all the curves. The thoracic is concave forward. 3. The thoracic and pelvic curves are primary, because they alone are present during fetal life. The cervical and lumbar are compensatory or secondary, and are developed after birth. 4. Those lengths have not been taken up with a required attribution in this first lesson on the curves, and would be easy to treat as a required measurement if they were imported now. Last week’s job was the count of 33 vertebræ and the true-or-false division, already taught. 5. Model: curves of the vertebral column, from Gray. Own words should match this idea: Gray 1918, in the public-domain text used here from Theodora, “The Vertebral Column as a Whole,” describes four curves viewed laterally, called cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and pelvic. The cervical curve is convex forward, the least marked of all the curves. The thoracic curve is concave forward; its most prominent point behind corresponds to the spinous process of the seventh thoracic vertebra. The lumbar curve is convex anteriorly. The pelvic curve begins at the sacrovertebral articulation and ends at the point of the coccyx; its concavity is directed downward and forward. The thoracic and pelvic curves are termed primary, because they alone are present during fetal life. The cervical and lumbar curves are compensatory or secondary, and are developed after birth. Official average lengths, including Gray’s 71 cm and 61 cm figures, remain reserved on this first day. A slight lateral curvature, also named by Gray, remains reserved. Keep Gray’s spelling vertebræ when you use that word. Last week’s count of 33 vertebræ is already taught, not a first look today.
