Latin · The perfect of a deponent, a first look

Check. 1. Locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt: I spoke or have spoken, you (sg.) spoke, he or she spoke, we spoke, you (pl.) spoke, they spoke. 2. Cōnātus sum, cōnātus es, cōnātus est, cōnātī sumus, cōnātī estis, cōnātī sunt: I tried or have tried, and so on. 3. The participle agrees in number with the subject. Nōs is plural, so locūtī. 4. Locūtus est is the perfect tense, with sum, “he or she spoke.” Puerō locūtō is an ablative absolute, already taught, a loosely attached phrase with no sum. 5. Model: perfect of a deponent. Own words should match this idea: The perfect of a deponent is formed with the already-seen perfect participle plus sum: locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt. The forms still look passive and are translated as active: I spoke, or I have spoken. Cōnātus sum, cōnātus es, cōnātus est, and the rest, follow the same pattern: I tried, or I have tried. The participle agrees in number: locūtus est (singular), locūtī sunt (plural). Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s present table (loquor, loqueris, loquitur). Not a first look at the perfect-participle ablative absolute (urbe captā, puerō locūtō), already taught: that is a loosely attached phrase, not the perfect tense with sum. Classroom sentences only, not copied from Ørberg. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. I spoke, or I have spoken. You (singular) tried, or have tried.
  2. The boys spoke, or have spoken. We tried, or have tried.
  3. They are deponent: the forms look passive and the meaning is active.
  4. The present table, loquor, loqueris, loquitur, was taught last week. Today is the perfect tense.

Physics · Forced oscillations and resonance, a first look

Check. 1. An oscillation driven by a periodic driving force. The driving frequency is the frequency of that force, which need not equal the system’s natural frequency. 2. The frequency at which the system would oscillate if there were no driving and no damping force. 3. Driving the system at its natural frequency. Energy is transferred most efficiently, and the amplitude is at its greatest for that system. 4. OpenStax uses it to show resonance of strings that share a note, not to supply a number this course should invent. How damping changes the height and width of the peak is Wednesday’s job. 5. Model: forced oscillation and resonance. Own words should match this idea: A forced oscillation, in OpenStax 16.8, is an oscillation driven by a periodic driving force. The driving force puts energy into the system at a certain frequency, not necessarily the system’s natural frequency. The natural frequency is the frequency at which the system would oscillate if there were no driving and no damping force. Resonance is the phenomenon of driving a system with a frequency equal to its natural frequency; a system so driven is said to resonate. Energy is transferred most efficiently at resonance, and the amplitude is then at its greatest for that system. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s damped motion, or at underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped motion, already taught, except as the damping that will be used on Wednesday. Not an official measured wind speed for a named bridge, and not a first look at MRI as a medical procedure beyond OpenStax’s brief mention of resonant nuclei, which waits if it is used at all until a later sourced contrast. Forced oscillations in a later quantitative formula remain reserved if 16.8 does not give one. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Modern language · Spanish: the imperfect of ir, a first look

Check. 1. Iba, ibas, iba, íbamos, ibais, iban: I was going or used to go, you (sg.) were going, he or she was going, we were going, you (pl.) were going, they were going. 2. The regular -ir imperfect uses -ía, as in vivía. Ir uses iba, with no -ía. 3. Sample: Cada mañana yo iba. Ayer yo fui is a completed event in the already-taught preterite. 4. The imperfect of ser is era, which has not been taught and is not the same set as iba. It waits until tomorrow. 5. Model: imperfect of ir. Own words should match this idea: The irregular imperfect of ir is iba, ibas, iba, íbamos, ibais, iban. There is no -ía from the regular -ir imperfect, already taught (vivía). In this course’s first look, the imperfect is still used for an ongoing or habitual past action: Cada mañana yo iba. The already-taught preterite of ir, fui, is a completed event: Ayer yo fui. Ir and ser share the preterite fui, already taught, but they do not share an imperfect: tomorrow’s era is not iba. Instruction remains in English. Classroom sentences are invented and do not identify a living person. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s regular imperfect, or at the preterite fui, already taught. Not tomorrow’s era, or Thursday’s veía. Instruction remains in English. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Seminar · Logic: Ferison, a first look at a third-figure EIO

Check. 1. No M are P; some M are S; therefore some S are not P. Third figure (the middle term is the subject of both premises). 2. Felapton is EAO-3: no M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P, two universal premises. Ferison is EIO-3: the second premise is particular, some M are S. 3. Ferio is first figure, already taught: the middle term is not the subject of both premises. Ferison is third figure, EIO-3. 4. Sample: No island-built temples are wooden; some island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. The terms are invented so the figure can be practiced; they are not a historical claim. 5. Model: Ferison. Own words should match this idea: Ferison is a valid third-figure mood, EIO-3: No M are P; some M are S; therefore some S are not P. The middle term is the subject of both premises, as in last week’s Felapton and in Darapti. The conclusion is particular and negative, like Felapton’s, but the second premise is particular (some M are S), not universal (all M are S). So Ferison does not need two universal premises. Invented terms are classroom terms, not official classifications. Near-miss: Not last week’s Felapton (EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P), which has a universal second premise. Not Darapti (AAI-3), and not first-figure Ferio, already taught, which has a different placement of the middle term. Other third-figure moods remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. M is the middle term, the subject of both premises, which is the third figure already taught.
  2. The second premise only places some M, not every S, among the things that are not P. A universal negative about all S does not follow.
  3. No M are P; some M are S; therefore some S are not P. Then: no island-built temples (M) are wooden (P); some island-built temples (M) are coastal works (S); therefore some coastal works (S) are not wooden (P).
  4. They have not been taught.
