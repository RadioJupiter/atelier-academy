Latin · Purpose clauses with ut and nē, mixed

Check. 1. Ut marks a positive purpose; nē marks a negative purpose. Both take the present subjunctive. 2. He rises in order that he go out. He sits in order that he not go out. 3. A result clause remains reserved. Today is a mix of purpose clauses already taught.

Exercises. 1. Fair sentences use ut or nē with sequātur or ēgrediātur, and give a short English gloss for each. 2. A fair correction: Puella manet nē eum sequātur, she stays in order that she not follow him. The miss used ut and the future. 3. A result clause remains reserved.

Mathematics · Algebra / analysis: the limit of a quotient, a first look

Check. 1. If lim f = L and lim g = M, and M ≠ 0, then lim (f / g) = L / M. If M = 0, the rule does not apply. 2. 6 / 4 = 3/2. The functions are invented. 3. The denominator’s limit is 0, so the rule does not apply. Last week used a factorization instead. 4. Model: the limit of a quotient, a first look. Own words should match this idea: If two quantities have limits, and if the limit of the denominator is not zero, the limit of their quotient is the quotient of those limits. If lim x→a f(x) = L and lim x→a g(x) = M, and M ≠ 0, then lim x→a (f(x) / g(x)) = L / M. If M = 0, this rule does not apply. Last week’s limit lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4 is not an instance of today’s rule, because the denominator’s limit is 0; that limit was found by an already-taught factorization, not by dividing the two limits. The figures used today are invented. Continuity remains reserved. Near-miss: Not yesterday’s product as if it were already this first look. Not last week’s (x² − 4)/(x − 2) treated as if today’s rule applied, because that denominator’s limit is 0. Not continuity, which remains reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. lim u = 5, lim v = 4, and 4 ≠ 0, so lim (u / v) = 5/4. The functions are invented. 2. lim g = 0, so the rule does not apply. 3. Continuity, and a formal epsilon argument, remain reserved.

History · “No harbour,” and formal possession with the royal banner, from the second-voyage letter

Check. 1. They searched more than a league and found “no harbour there.” The admiral decided to go to the other island, “at four or five leagues distance.” 2. He landed “with the royal banner in his hands, and took formal possession on behalf of their Majesties.” 3. They are Major’s spellings and stay inside quotations. Unquoted prose in this course uses US spelling. 4. Model: “no harbour,” and formal possession with the royal banner. Own words should match this idea: In Major’s 1870 wording, confirmed from the page, they “proceeded more than a league in search of a port where we might anchor, but without finding one.” “When we found that there was no harbour there, the admiral decided that we should go to the other island, which appeared on the right, and which was at four or five leagues distance.” One vessel remained “seeking for a harbour.” Later, “having found a good one,” they returned to the fleet, “which had put into harbour at the other island, and there the admiral, accompanied by a great number of men, landed with the royal banner in his hands, and took formal possession on behalf of their Majesties.” “Harbour” is Major’s spelling and stays inside the quotation. The letter also says that “at that season there is scarcely any thing green in our own country.” “Any thing” stays inside the quotation. A footnote on that page, “Dominica has no harbours,” is Major’s note, not the body. The later clove-scented leaf, and the waterfall, wait until Friday. Near-miss: Not Monday’s six islands as if they were new. Not a later official island name written as if it stood in the body. Not Friday’s leaf and waterfall, which wait until then. Not an official modern conversion of “four or five leagues.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The footnote, “Dominica has no harbours,” is Major’s note, not wording that stands in the body of the letter. 2. Monday taught the six islands and the pilots’ leagues. Today continues with the search for a “harbour” and the formal possession. 3. The clove-scented leaf, the two hours on the island, and the waterfall, wait until Friday.

Model: three straits and a bay of more than forty stades (Dewing, I.5.7–13). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, Buildings I.5.7–13, as posted on LacusCurtius, after last week’s account of how the sea adorns Byzantium, the writer describes three straits. One “would imagine that he was looking upon a river moving toward him with gentle current.” “The sea forms a garland about the city.” The bay “is always calm,” “just as if limits were set there for the turbulent waters and all billows were excluded from that area so as to do honour to the city.” “Honour” is Dewing’s spelling and stays inside the quotation. “The circuit of the bay extends to a distance of more than forty stades, and furnishes anchorage throughout its whole extent.” “More than forty stades” is Dewing’s wording, not a modern official conversion of a stade. Last week’s I.5.1–6 remains a different extract. I.4.17–24 remains reserved. Official modern measurements are not invented. Near-miss: Not last week’s I.5.1–6 as if it were already this extract. Not I.4.17–24, which remains reserved. Not an official modern conversion of a stade. Not a later section of I.5 beyond this extract. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. It is Dewing’s spelling. Unquoted prose in this course uses US spelling. 2. They “proceed for the rest of the way without a pilot and are anchored without precautions.” 3. Later sentences of I.5 beyond this extract, official modern conversions, and I.4.17–24 remain reserved.
