Latin · The deponent pluperfect, used in short sentences

Check. 1. Cōnātus eram, I had tried; cōnāta erat, she had tried; cōnātī erāmus, we had tried. 2. The trying is already completed before the laugh. Cōnābātur would be ongoing or habitual trying. 3. The subject puella is feminine, so the participle is cōnāta, as in the already-taught perfect. 4. Model: pluperfect of a deponent. Own words should match this idea: The same pluperfect pattern holds for other already-taught deponents: cōnātus eram, I had tried, uses the participle cōnātus with eram. Cōnāta erat, she had tried, follows the already-taught agreement of the participle. Near-miss: Not cōnābar, I was trying or I used to try, and not cōnātus sum, I tried or I have tried. Not a new verb beyond the already-taught deponents. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. We had spoken. She had tried. I was trying, or I used to try. 2. A fair sentence: Puerī cōnātī erant, et tum sēdērunt. The trying is first, the sitting later. 3. Both become cōnātus eram in the first person.

Mathematics · Geometry LXVIII: a line perpendicular to a plane, a first look

Check. 1. The line meets the plane at a foot P and is perpendicular to two lines in the plane that intersect at P. Then it is perpendicular to the plane. 2. DA meets the floor at A and, because the box is rectangular, is perpendicular to the two floor edges AB and AE that meet at A. 3. A line may meet the plane at one point and still be oblique. Perpendicularity is a right-angle condition, not only a meeting. 4. Model: a line perpendicular to a plane. Own words should match this idea: A line is perpendicular to a plane if it meets the plane at a point P, the foot, and is perpendicular to every line in the plane that passes through P. In practice it is enough, and it is the method used today, to check two intersecting lines in the plane through P: if the given line is perpendicular to both, it is perpendicular to the plane. Near-miss: Not a line that merely meets the plane at one point, which may be oblique. Not perpendicular only to one line in the plane. Not an official claim about a real wall being square. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Yes, on this rectangular box. CB meets the floor at B; check BA and BF, the floor edges through B. CB is perpendicular to both, so it is perpendicular to the floor. 2. It may be perpendicular to AB and still fail to be perpendicular to AE. Then it is not perpendicular to the floor. 3. The box is an invented model. The argument uses right angles, not a measured official height.

Writing · A retelling of Townsend’s Crow and the Pitcher

Check. 1. A fair retelling keeps thirst, the pitcher with too little water, the failed efforts, the stones dropped one by one, the water brought within reach, and the life saved, then states, “Necessity is the mother of invention.” 2. A new animal, a new official fact about crows, a second moral in the middle, or a change in what happens (for example, the crow tips the pitcher over). 3. The set text is in view, and the task is to tell the same fable in new words. Franklin’s method hides the model and reconstructs it from hints.

Exercises. 1. A fair short version: A thirsty crow cannot reach the little water in a pitcher. After failed efforts, it drops stones in one by one until the water rises, and so lives. “Necessity is the mother of invention.” 2. So the narrative stays a narrative, and the moral is named once, after the action. 3. “Necessity is the mother of invention.”

Science · Ochre, from rock to pigment, a first look

Check. 1. An earthy, often clayey form of an iron-oxide mineral used as a pigment after milling and minimal processing. 2. Hematite for red; limonite or goethite, hydrated iron oxides, for yellow, buff, or brown. 3. An official iron percentage for a classroom lump. Lapis lazuli and ultramarine remain reserved. 4. Model: ochre. Own words should match this idea: Ochre, as the term is used in USGS mineral sources, is an earthy, often clayey form of an iron-oxide mineral used as a pigment after milling and only minimal processing. Hematite gives red ochres; limonite or goethite, which are hydrated iron oxides, give yellow, buff, or brown ochres. Impurities also influence the shade. Near-miss: Not a synthetic modern convenience color from a catalog, and not lapis lazuli, which remains reserved. Not an official iron percentage invented for a classroom lump of earth. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Natural iron-oxide pigments are suitable after milling and only minimal processing; the color is already in the mineral, not invented by a heavy chemical change in this first look. 2. Yellow ochre is one of the four paints in this week’s limited palette. A tube labeled yellow ochre is a paint, not automatically a USGS identification of a particular rock. 3. Hematite more closely, including calcining a yellow ocher to make a red, from Bulletin 430-G.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Volume I, Chapter 1: the opening, a first look

Check. 1. “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.” The “truth” is “so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families.” 2. A claim announced as universal is, in the next sentence, a fixed family idea, and even a claim that the man is “rightful property.” 3. Not as a fact about living people. The dialogue that follows in Chapter 1, including Netherfield and the novel’s income figure, waits until Thursday. 4. Model: ironic opening (Pride and Prejudice). Own words should match this idea: The novel opens with a sentence that presents a “truth universally acknowledged” as if it were settled, and the next sentence shows that the “truth” is what surrounding families have already decided. The irony is that a claim offered as universal is, in the book’s own wording, a fixed idea about a “rightful property.” The set text is the 1813 wording. Near-miss: Not a statement the course is asking you to adopt as a fact about living people. Not an official income, and not an identification of a character with a historical person. British spelling inside the quotation stays inside the quotation. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. “The rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.” The book’s own wording already treats the “truth” as a claim of possession, not as a neutral report of the man’s views, which the second sentence says are “little known.” 2. The quotation keeps the 1813 spelling. The teaching prose around it stays in US spelling. 3. Do not identify a character with a living person, or with a historical person the book does not name here.

Atelier · A color-value scale in yellow ochre

Check. 1. Yellow ochre, white, and black only. Four or five even steps are enough. 2. A jump that bunches the values at one end, or a missing middle. The earth red stays out of this scale. 3. Comparing neighboring steps as values, or half-closing the eyes, as already taught in monochrome.

Exercises. 1. A light ochre-and-white, a less light mix, unmixed ochre if it sits in the run, then two darker ochre-and-black steps, even in value. 2. The job is a scale, not a likeness. A portrait remains later work. 3. The scale trains mixing and value in a paint. Science may name ochre as a mineral; a label is not an assay.

Modern language · Spanish: the simple future of regular -er and -ir verbs

Check. 1. Comeré, comerás, comerá, comeremos, comeréis, comerán. Comeré, I will eat; comeremos, we will eat. 2. Viviré, I will live or reside; vivirá, he or she will; vivirán, they will. A fair sentence: Un día viviremos cerca del río, with the place invented. 3. The simple future adds the same six endings to the whole infinitive, for regular -ar, -er, and -ir verbs. Irregular futures such as haré and iré remain reserved.

Exercises. 1. Tomorrow I will eat. They will eat. We will live, or we will reside. 2. A fair pair: Mañana comeremos a las dos, an invented time, not an official timetable. Ella vivirá cerca del parque, an invented place. 3. Comeré.

Seminar · Music: binary form, a first look

Check. 1. Two parts, A then B, each a complete strain. A period is an antecedent and a consequent, already taught, and may sit inside a strain; it is not by itself the two-part form. 2. A ends on the dominant, degree 5, at the end of bar 4. B ends on C, degree 1, at the end of bar 8. 3. A then A is a repeat, not a second strain. Ternary form, ABA, remains reserved. 4. Model: binary form. Own words should match this idea: Binary form is a two-part musical form, A then B. Each part is a complete strain; B is a second strain, not only a repeat of A, and not a return of A as a third large part. Today’s examples are short invented classroom figures, not a copyrighted score. Near-miss: Not the already-taught musical period (antecedent and consequent), which may sit inside a strain. Not ternary form, ABA, which remains reserved. Not a copyrighted tune named as a classroom example. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. A fair answer has two labeled strains, invented, with B not a mere copy of A, and with no copyrighted melody. 2. The lesson uses invented classroom figures. A copyrighted tune is not a set example today. 3. A strain may itself be a period, or contain one, while the large form is still A then B.
