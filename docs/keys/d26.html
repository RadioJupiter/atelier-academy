Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 1: ille, and hic against is

Check. 1. For example, Hic liber est meus; ille liber est tuus. (or two boys in two places, as in Learn). 2. eum simply refers back to the book just mentioned. hunc or illum would add a near or far pointing that the sentence does not need. 3. Model: ille, illa, illud. Own words should match this idea: The far demonstrative: ‘that’ (farther from the speaker, or already distinguished from ‘this’). A working set today: ille, illa, illud (nominative), illum, illam, illud (accusative), and the idea that illīus / illī (genitive and dative singular, all genders) will appear as you read. You need the nominative and accusative fluently today; the other cases will be practiced as they occur in original sentences and in the book. Near-miss: Not hic (this, near). And not the adverb illīc (‘there’), which is a different word. is, ea, id remains the weaker ‘he/she/it / the one just mentioned,’ not a strong pointing word. is, ea, id (reminder). Own words should match this idea: The pronoun of reference: ‘he, she, it / that one just mentioned,’ without the strong near/far pointing of hic and ille. Near-miss: Not ‘this here’ or ‘that over there.’ If you mean to point, use hic or ille. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ille, illa, illud.
  2. Iūlia illam rosam Mārcō dat.
  3. The adverb means ‘there’; it is not declined as a pronoun in this lesson.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. They are pronouns (demonstratives); the neuter singular nominative and accusative take -d, not the adjectival -um.

Mathematics · Algebra VIII: the sum of a finite geometric progression

Check. 1. S = 3((-2)^4 − 1)/(-2 − 1) = 3(16 − 1)/(−3) = −15. Check: 3 − 6 + 12 − 24 = −15. 2. S = 6 × 4 = 24. Use S = n a because r = 1. The other formula would divide by r − 1 = 0. 3. Model: sum of a finite geometric progression. Own words should match this idea: The sum of the first n terms of a geometric progression with first term a and common ratio r is Sn = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1. When r = 1, every term is a, and Sn = n a. Day 20 defined the GP and the nth term; today adds the finite sum. Near-miss: Not the AP pairing formula n/2 · (a + ℓ), which belongs to a constant difference. And not an infinite-sum formula, which this course does not use today (and which would need |r| < 1 and a later argument). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. S = 3(2^6 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 3(64 − 1) = 189.
  2. The terms do not have a constant difference, so first + last is not the sum of each pair (2+54=56, 6+18=24). Pairing is an AP idea.
  3. S = 2+6+18+54; 3S = 6+18+54+162; 3S − S = 162 − 2 = 160, so 2S = 160, S = 80.
  4. S = 1((1/2)^4 − 1)/(1/2 − 1) = (1/16 − 1)/(−1/2) = (−15/16) / (−1/2) = (15/16)×2 = 15/8.
  5. AP: last = 3+3×4=15, S=4/2×(3+15)=36. GP: S=3(16−1)/(2−1)=45. The GP sum is larger in this example.

Writing & Rhetoric · The three-paragraph outline

Check. 1. A three-job outline on a two-week thesis, in phrases, with a clear thesis line. 2. A theorem, quotation, or précis marked in the middle; a real limit marked in the close. 3. Model: three-paragraph outline. Own words should match this idea: A plan for a short essay of three paragraphs: an opening paragraph that states a thesis, a middle paragraph that develops one line of support (with evidence or a précis of a source), and a closing paragraph that includes a concession or a limit and then returns to the thesis in new words. Near-miss: Not three unconnected paragraphs, and not one long paragraph chopped at two indentations. The jobs are different. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. For example: A long 1776 sentence can be studied as a structure — a condition, a parallel pair of acts, and a requirement to state reasons — without asking the précis to become a political argument.
  2. Because the support still needs a head: the reader should know the paragraph’s point before the evidence.
  3. Real: a genuine limit (for example, a late source can still be the best narrative we have). Fake: “Some might say Livy is late, but they are wrong,” with no limit actually granted.
  4. It adds a closing paragraph whose jobs are concession (or limit) and a return, rather than asking the second paragraph to do all of that as well as the support.
  5. A thesis plus a three-line outline, saved for Friday, on a school subject from the course.

Physics · Gravitational potential energy near the Earth

Check. 1. Δh = 2 m, ΔPE = 4 × 9.8 × 2 = 78.4 J (an increase). 2. Because 49 J is the PE relative to a chosen zero (the floor). With another zero, the number changes; the usable fact is a difference. 3. Model: gravitational potential energy (near Earth). Own words should match this idea: In this course’s first model, the gravitational potential energy of an object near the Earth, treated as a particle, is PE = m g h, where h is the height above a chosen reference level, g is 9.8 N/kg (or 9.8 m/s²), and PE is in joules. Only differences in PE are physically usable; the zero of h is a choice. Near-miss: Not a claim about gravity far from the Earth (no 1/r formula today), and not a third kind of mass. The m is the same mass as in F = m a and in KE = ½ m v². reference level. Own words should match this idea: The height you choose as h = 0 when you write PE = m g h. Changing the reference changes the number you write for PE, but it does not change a difference PE₂ − PE₁ between two heights. Near-miss: Not a “true” zero required by nature in this model, and not a reason to mix two zeros in one calculation without converting. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. PE = 3 × 9.8 × 4 = 117.6 J.
  2. On the balcony, PE = 0. On the floor, h = −4 m, PE = 3 × 9.8 × (−4) = −117.6 J. The difference from balcony to floor is still −117.6 J.
  3. Gravity does +49 J of work (force downward, displacement downward). PE changes by −49 J. (You do −49 J if you lower it slowly, opposite the displacement? Careful: if you lower slowly, your upward force is opposite the downward displacement, so your work is negative, and gravity’s work is positive, and ΔKE ≈ 0, so the two works cancel. Yes: W_gravity = +49 J, ΔPE = −49 J.)
  4. The 1/r form is a different model, for a wider range of distances; this course is teaching a first, constant-g model near the Earth and will not pretend to have derived the general formula today.
  5. With 9.8: 49 J. With 10: 50 J. This course prefers 9.8 unless a problem tells you to approximate; if you use 10, you must label it as an approximation.

History · The Punic Wars, with Polybius as a source

Check. 1. They belong to a better-documented period, with a standard scholarly chronology tied to events that later and nearer sources treat in a tighter frame — unlike a traditional founding year for a thinly documented early city. 2. A tag such as “in the Greco-Roman sources” or a named author (Polybius, or a later Roman narrator), rather than an untagged story. 3. Model: Punic Wars. Own words should match this idea: Three wars between Rome and Carthage: the First (264–241 BCE), the Second (218–201 BCE, including Hannibal’s invasion of Italy, Cannae in 216, and Zama in 202), and the Third (149–146 BCE), which ended with the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE. These dates are the standard scholarly chronology and are much better fixed than the traditional years of early Rome. Near-miss: Not a fourth war, and not a story that has to be told only from a Roman point of view. Carthage was a major power of the western Mediterranean; the sources we have are still largely Greek and Roman. Polybius (on the wars). Own words should match this idea: A Greek historian of the second century BCE who wrote a history of Rome’s rise, including the Punic Wars. He had Roman patrons and access, and he is an analyst as well as a narrator. Use him as a second-century source, not as a modern staff officer’s report. Near-miss: Not an eyewitness of every campaign he describes, and not a Carthaginian voice. For early Rome’s constitution, Day 23 already used him as a mid-Republican analyst. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Cannae 216 BCE, Zama 202 BCE, both in the Second Punic War.
  2. Likeness: a second-century analysis with aims. Difference: here he is narrating wars closer to (and partly in) his world’s recent past; on the early Republic’s beginning he was already being used with a stronger caution about 509 BCE.
  3. Because a narrative drawn only from Roman and Greek writers can hide that one side’s archive is mostly missing; the caution belongs in the wording, even in a short lesson.
  4. What each author could have known, when each wrote, and whether the difference is fact, emphasis, or design — not “which one feels more vivid.”
  5. A plan that names the chronology, names Polybius (with a date), and notes later Roman retelling and the scarcity of Carthaginian literary voices.

Modern language · Spanish: tener que, hay, and hay que

Check. 1. Tienes que leer. Tenemos que leer. 2. For example: Hay un lápiz. Hay que estudiar. 3. Model: tener que. Own words should match this idea: A present-tense pattern that expresses obligation: tener que + infinitive, ‘to have to’ do something. The form of tener agrees with the person who is obliged. Near-miss: Not hay que, which is impersonal (‘one has to / it is necessary to’) and does not change for yo or tú. And still not a past tense. hay / hay que. Own words should match this idea: hay means ‘there is / there are’ (present). hay que + infinitive means ‘one has to / it is necessary to,’ without naming a particular subject. Hay does not change for plural in this use: Hay un libro; Hay dos libros. Near-miss: Not *tienen que hay, and not *hayn. And not estoy for mere existence in the ‘there is’ sense taught today: the book’s location can use está, but ‘there is a book’ is hay un libro. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Tiene que: a particular person (he, she, or usted). Hay que: no particular subject, ‘one has to.’
  2. No hay agua. Tenemos que servir la comida.
  3. está locates something already identified; hay introduces existence (‘there is’). Today’s ‘there is a book’ is Hay un libro en la mesa. (Está el libro en la mesa is a different, locating sentence, if the book is already known.)
  4. Two heard patterns, each in a correct new sentence.
  5. A short, correct three-sentence string using tener que, hay, and hay que.

Logic · All, some, and none: a first look at A, E, I, and O

Check. 1. O: Some cyclic quadrilaterals do not have opposite angles supplementary. Given III.22 as taught, O is false, and the A-sentence is true. They are contradictories. 2. In this lesson, “some” means at least one. If all squares are rectangles, the I-sentence is still true. It does not mean “only some.” 3. Model: categorical sentences (A, E, I, O). Own words should match this idea: Four standard forms of sentence about classes: A, “All S are P”; E, “No S are P”; I, “Some S are P”; O, “Some S are not P.” The letters are traditional names. “Some” here means “at least one,” not “some but not all.” Near-miss: Not a full system of syllogisms, which this lesson does not teach. And not “some” in the everyday hint of “only a few.” If even one S is P, the I-sentence is true. square of opposition (first look). Own words should match this idea: A traditional diagram of how A, E, I, and O relate: A and O are contradictories (one true, the other false); E and I are contradictories; A and E are contraries (they cannot both be true, but both can be false); I and O are subcontraries (they cannot both be false, but both can be true), on the standard reading taught here, which assumes there is at least one S (an existential assumption this course will name rather than hide). Near-miss: Not a claim that we have proved every medieval or modern subtlety of the square, and not a license to ignore empty classes in a later course. If there were no S at all, some of these relations would need a more careful treatment, which we only flag today. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. For example: All squares are rhombuses (A, true in this course); Some squares are not rhombuses (O, false).
  2. Yes: Some rectangles are squares (I, true) and Some rectangles are not squares (O, true). Subcontraries can both be true.
  3. That there is at least one S, when we let A imply I. It was named so that a later treatment of empty classes is not silently skipped, and so that we do not pretend to have settled it today.
  4. They are closely related: the conditional talks in if–then form about a figure; the A-sentence talks about the class of squares. This lesson treats them as companions, not as a finished translation theory.
  5. Four sentences on one everyday pair, marked only as far as the student honestly knows, with no invented census.
