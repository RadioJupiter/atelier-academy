Physics · Damped harmonic motion: underdamped, critical, and overdamped

Check. 1. It removes mechanical energy, usually as thermal energy. Wnc = Δ(KE + PE), and for damping Wnc is negative. 2. The period and frequency are nearly the same as in undamped SHM. The amplitude gradually decreases. 3. Underdamped: it overshoots and crosses one or more times. Critically damped: it returns as quickly as possible, overshooting at most once. Overdamped: it moves toward equilibrium more slowly than critical damping, without a train of oscillations. 4. Critical damping. 5. Model: damped harmonic motion. Own words should match this idea: OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.7, treats damped harmonic motion: a non-conservative damping force removes mechanical energy, usually as thermal energy. The work of that force is Wnc = Δ(KE + PE), and for damping Wnc is negative. Completely undamped motion is rare. Last week’s undamped SHM, in which ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX², is the contrast in which those losses were neglected. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s undamped energy equation or at T = 2π√(m/k), except as the contrast when damping is neglected. Not today’s later names, underdamped, critical, and overdamped, except as they will be taught next in this same lesson. Forced oscillations and resonance, OpenStax 16.8, are reserved. underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped. Own words should match this idea: OpenStax 16.7 distinguishes three amounts of damping. An underdamped system returns toward equilibrium but overshoots and crosses one or more times; with small damping, the period is nearly unchanged while the amplitude falls. Critical damping is the condition in which the oscillator returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible; it may overshoot once, but if it does, it will do so only once. An overdamped system also moves toward equilibrium without a train of oscillations, but it takes longer than critical damping. OpenStax’s check: an overdamped system moves slowly toward equilibrium; an underdamped system moves quickly but oscillates about equilibrium as it does so; a critically damped system moves as quickly as possible toward equilibrium without oscillating about it. Seminar · Logic: Felapton, a first look at a third-figure particular negative

Check. 1. No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. Third figure (the middle term is the subject of both premises). 2. The premises show that the M, which are not P, are among the S, so some S are not P. They do not show that every S is an M, so they do not show that no S are P. 3. Darapti is AAI-3: all M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P, a particular affirmative. Felapton is EAO-3, a particular negative. 4. Sample: No island-built temples are wooden; all island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. The terms are invented so the figure can be practiced; they are not a historical claim. 5. Model: Felapton. Own words should match this idea: Felapton is a valid third-figure mood, EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. The middle term is the subject of both premises, as in last week’s Darapti. The conclusion is particular and negative: some S are not P, not “no S are P,” and not “all S are P.” The premises together require that some S exist, because all M are S and there is at least one M in the universal negative, in the traditional reading used here. Near-miss: Not last week’s Darapti (AAI-3: all M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P), which has a particular affirmative conclusion. Not last week’s second-figure Baroco. Other third-figure moods remain reserved. Invented terms are classroom terms, not official classifications. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. M is the middle term, the subject of both premises, which is the third figure already taught.
  2. That would be a universal negative about all S. The premises only place the M, not every S, among the things that are not P.
  3. No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. Then: no island-built temples (M) are wooden (P); all island-built temples (M) are coastal works (S); therefore some coastal works (S) are not wooden (P).
  4. They have not been taught.
