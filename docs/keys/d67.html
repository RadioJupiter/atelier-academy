Latin · Capitulum XII, part 2: the dative of possession

Check. 1. Mārcō scūtum nōn est. 2. In this pattern the possessor is dative (Mārcō), and the thing possessed is nominative (gladius). Mārcus gladium est puts the wrong cases on both nouns. 3. Model: dative of possession. Own words should match this idea: A use of the dative with a form of esse to say that someone has something: the possessor is in the dative, and the thing possessed is in the nominative. Mārcō gladius est means “Marcus has a sword” (a sword is to Marcus). Near-miss: Not a nominative possessor with a made-up verb for “has” in this pattern, and not an accusative of the person who has the thing. Mārcus gladium est would not be this construction. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Iūliae gladius nōn est.
  2. Iūliae gladius est.
  3. Mārcō is dative; it names the possessor.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Geometry XXX: a similar-triangle proof of the Pythagorean theorem

Check. 1. BD = 9/5 = 1.8. Then AD + BD = 3.2 + 1.8 = 5, which is AB. 2. A 3-4-5 check shows the theorem in one triangle. The similarity argument shows why a² + b² = c² in every right triangle, from AA. 3. Model: altitude to the hypotenuse. Own words should match this idea: The perpendicular from the right angle to the hypotenuse in a right triangle. It meets the hypotenuse at a point that splits the original triangle into two smaller right triangles, each similar to the original triangle and to the other. Near-miss: Not a median (which would only bisect the hypotenuse, and need not be perpendicular), and not an altitude to a leg. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A median only bisects the hypotenuse; it need not be perpendicular, so it need not make the right angles this proof uses, except in a special case such as an isosceles right triangle.
  2. In ABC the hypotenuse is AB. In ACD the hypotenuse is AC (opposite the right angle at D). In CBD the hypotenuse is BC.

Science · Water: surface tension

Check. 1. 75.64 mN/m at 0.01 °C, and 71.98 mN/m at 25 °C, each with the table’s own uncertainty (0.38 mN/m and 0.36 mN/m respectively). 2. The table does not list 22 °C in the lines we are using, and an average of two official rows would be a new figure this course has not been given. 3. Model: surface tension. Own words should match this idea: A tension in a liquid surface, arising because the surface behaves as if it were a stretched skin: it tends to contract. It is reported as a force per length, or equivalently as an energy per area, in units such as mN/m (which are the same as mJ/m²). Near-miss: Not the same quantity as last week’s boiling temperature, and not a claim that the surface is a separate solid film. The surface is still water, with a tension. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Surface tension is a property of the liquid surface, not a skin of ice. Ice is a different state, already distinguished last week from liquid water at the ice point and at the triple point.
  2. The official number comes from the IAPWS table. The needle is a picture of the tension, not a substitute measurement of that official value.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -ir verbs, with the same endings as -er

Check. 1. El lunes pasado vivimos aquí. 2. They share the -í ending of the regular -er / -ir preterite. hablé is the -ar yo form, a different set. 3. Model: the preterite of regular -ir verbs. Own words should match this idea: The past-tense forms of a regular -ir verb, such as vivir: viví, viviste, vivió, vivimos, vivisteis, vivieron. In the preterite, regular -ir verbs use the same endings as regular -er verbs. Near-miss: Not a new, different ending-set from comí, comiste, comió…, and not the present (vivo, vives, vive). The shared endings are the point of today’s sitting. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Ayer no viví allí.
  2. Three heard forms, each used.
  3. The yo form of the shared -er / -ir preterite is -í, not the -ar -é.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Music theory · A short I–IV–V–I progression, with inversion

Check. 1. G major, C major, D major, G major. 2. The authentic cadence is the V–I at the end. I–IV–V–I is a short progression that includes that cadence; it does not rename it. 3. Model: a I–IV–V–I progression. Own words should match this idea: A short harmonic progression using the tonic, subdominant, and dominant triads of a key, returning to the tonic: I–IV–V–I. Each triad may be in root position or in an inversion already taught. Near-miss: Not a new kind of cadence name for the whole four-chord pattern, and not a claim that every piece of music uses only these three triads. The authentic cadence already taught is V–I, which this progression contains at the end. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Inversion changes the bass, not the triad’s name. A first-inversion V is still V.
  2. Seventh chords are not this week’s work.
