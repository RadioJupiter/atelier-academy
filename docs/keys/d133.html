Latin · Secūtus eram and ēgressus eram, mixed with already-taught deponent tenses

Check. 1. Sequor, secūtus sum, secūtus eram; ēgredior, ēgressus sum, ēgressus eram. 2. Ēgreditur: nunc asks for the present, he goes out. Locūtus eram: the speaking was already completed before I was silent. 3. You (pl.) had gone out. I had followed her.

Exercises. 1. Secūtī erant; ēgressī erāmus. 2. Locūtus sum is the perfect, I spoke or have spoken, and does not mark the action as already completed before another past moment. 3. A fair pair: Puerī mē secūtī erant. Puella ēgressa erat, et tum tacēbat.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXVII: the law of sines, a first look

Check. 1. In any triangle, a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. From (1/2)bc sin A = (1/2)ac sin B, cancel (1/2) and c, so b sin A = a sin B, and a/sin A = b/sin B. 2. Each ratio equals 2. 3. The law compares each side with the sine of the opposite angle. The sides are equal only when the opposite angles are equal. A non-right triangle, the ambiguous case, the law of cosines, and radians remain reserved.

Exercises. 1. 5 / sin 30° = c / sin 90°, so 5 / (1/2) = c / 1, and c = 10. 2. The law holds in any triangle, including an obtuse one, where an angle such as 150° may appear. Tomorrow’s non-right example uses 120°. 3. It is a classroom length, not an official measurement of a real object.

Writing · A short original chreia, on an invented classroom occasion

Check. 1. A fair invented chreia names a clearly invented figure, gives a short occasion, and ends in a saying or deed that fits, without a moral tag. 2. It attributes a saying or deed to a named person on a brief occasion. It has no stated moral, and it is not a long who-what-when-where narrative. 3. Do not invent official facts about a historical person, or name a living person. A maxim, and a full later elaboration, remain reserved.

Exercises. 1. Person: Diogenes. Occasion: Alexander finds him in the sun and asks whether he wanted anything. Saying: “I would have you stand from between me and the sun.” 2. The exercise is not a biography of a living person, and no official claim about a living person is being made. 3. A stated moral would turn the piece into a fable, already taught as a different exercise.

Physics · The speed of sound, frequency, and wavelength, from OpenStax 17.2

Check. 1. Vw = fλ. In air at sea level, vw = 331 m/s × √(T/273 K). In Example 17.1, vw = 348.7 m/s, λmax = 17 m, and λmin = 1.7 cm. 2. 331 m/s at 0°C; 343 m/s at 20.0°C; 1480 m/s in fresh water at 20°C. 3. λ = 343 / 686 = 0.500 m, with 686 Hz invented and 343 m/s official. Decibels remain reserved. Invent no official speed of light. 4. Model: speed of sound. Own words should match this idea: In OpenStax 17.2, the speed of sound, frequency, and wavelength are related by the already-taught wave equation vw = fλ. Perception of frequency is called pitch. For air at sea level, the book gives vw = 331 m/s × √(T/273 K), with T in kelvin. At 0°C the official table value is 331 m/s; at 20.0°C the book gives 343 m/s. Official Example 17.1 uses 30.0°C air, taken as 303 K, and finds vw = 348.7 m/s, then λ = 17 m at 20 Hz and λ = 0.017 m, or 1.7 cm, at 20,000 Hz, the extremes of the audible range, with those frequencies accurate to two significant figures. Near-miss: Not an official speed of light, which is not being invented. Not last week’s intensity. Not a claim that the speed of sound is the same in every medium; Table 17.1 gives official values that differ, such as 1480 m/s in fresh water at 20°C and 5960 m/s in steel. Decibels remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Sound travels at a finite speed, much slower than light. The book does not give a speed of light in this example, and none is being invented. 2. λ = 348.7 / 100 = 3.487 m, which may be left as 3.49 m to three figures, with 100 Hz invented. 3. In open air, in the audible range, the speed of sound is nearly independent of frequency, so high and low pitches travel at nearly the same speed.

History · The Treaty of Tordesillas: lands east and west of the line

Check. 1. Lands east of the 370-league line, found or to be found by Portugal and its vessels, pertain forever to the King of Portugal and his successors. Lands west of the line, found or to be found by Castile and its vessels, pertain forever to the King and Queen of Castile, Leon, etc., and their successors. 2. Those lands shall pertain forever to the King of Portugal and his heirs. 3. The May 4 bull granted lands west and south of a hundred-league line to Castile and León, by papal grant. This is a later treaty between the two crowns, at 370 leagues from the Cape Verde Islands. Do not convert the 370 leagues into an official modern length or longitude.

Exercises. 1. Portugal: lands east of the bound “shall belong to, and remain in the possession of, and pertain forever to, the said King of Portugal and his successors.” Castile: lands west of the bound “shall belong to, and remain in the possession of, and pertain forever to, the said King and Queen of Castile, Leon, etc., and to their successors.” 2. It is Davenport’s spelling in the translation. Teaching prose may say León when not quoting. 3. The first-voyage letter, and any official modern longitude of the line.

Atelier · A value match on the cloth, between the mixed hue and ochre

Check. 1. An ochre fold or plane and a mixed-earth-hue fold of the same lightness, checked by half-closing the eyes. 2. Changing the hue in order to make the colors look the same, rather than matching lightness. Last week the same value match was taught on scales and on two objects. 3. A fifth tube, azurite, malachite, or ultramarine as a palette color, and a portrait.

Exercises. 1. That would be a hue match, and it would undo the mixed hue. Today’s job is a value match across different hues. 2. Value is lightness, already taught in monochrome, and the folds are still planes of light and dark. 3. The job is a match on a cloth study, not a likeness of a face.

Seminar · Art history: Dewing, Buildings I.3.10–15, the Heraeum and the next shrines

Check. 1. A church to the Mother of God in the Heraeum, “which they now call the Hieron.” 2. A church to St. Anna in Deuteron, “whom some consider to have been the mother of the Virgin and the grandmother of Christ.” A shrine to the martyr Zoê, near the last street within the city. A small, badly lighted shrine of Michael, torn down to the foundations. 3. Invent no official length, and no official shrine count. Later sections of the Buildings remain reserved. 4. Model: the church at the Heraeum. Own words should match this idea: In Dewing, Buildings I.3.10–15, the 1940 Loeb translation as posted on LacusCurtius, Justinian, “Also in the Heraeum, which they now call the Hieron, he built a church to the Mother of God which it is not easy to describe.” The same passage then names a church to St. Anna in the section of the city called Deuteron, a shrine to the martyr Zoê, and a small, badly lighted shrine of the Archangel Michael, which he tore down “even to the lowest foundations.” Invent no official length, and no official shrine count. Near-miss: Not last week’s shrine at Pegê, or the church at Blachernae, as if either were this third shrine of the Virgin. Not an official modern measurement. Later sections of the Buildings remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The church at the Heraeum is “a church to the Mother of God which it is not easy to describe.” The earlier Michael shrine is torn down “even to the lowest foundations, so that no trace of its earlier unseemliness might remain.” 2. Blachernae is the church by the sea, already taught; Pegê is the one close to the Golden Gate, last week; the Heraeum is this third shrine of the Virgin, now called the Hieron. 3. Dewing reports what “some consider,” and the wording stays inside the quotation. The lesson is a close reading, not an official theological claim added by the classroom.
