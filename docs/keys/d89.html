Latin · Capitulum XVI: fiō, and more deponents in the third person

Check. 1. The pupils go out from the school. A storm happens, or a storm arises. 2. Loquitur is a deponent: it looks passive and means “he speaks.” Fit is the irregular verb fiō, “it becomes / it happens,” not a deponent of speaking. 3. Canis magistrum sequitur. 4. Model: fiō, fierī. Own words should match this idea: Fiō, fierī means to become, to be made, or to happen. In this first look the course uses the third person and the infinitive: fit, it becomes, it is made, it happens; fiunt, they become; fierī, to become. Near-miss: Not a first look at sum, es, est, and not a first look at a deponent. Fiō is an irregular verb, not a deponent, though fierī can sit near deponents in this chapter. Do not invent first- and second-person forms of fiō today; they are reserved. sequitur and ēgreditur. Own words should match this idea: Sequitur means he or she follows; ēgreditur means he or she goes out. Both are deponents, used today in the third person and as infinitives (sequī, ēgredī), like yesterday’s loquitur and cōnātur. Near-miss: Not a first look at deponent as a kind, taught yesterday. Not an ordinary active sequit or ēgredit, which this course does not use. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Sequitur, he or she follows; sequuntur, they follow; sequī, to follow.
  2. A sentence such as Discipulī tacitī fiunt.
  3. The boy tries to speak and goes out from the school.
  4. First-person forms of fiō have not been taught and are reserved, like first-person deponents.

Science · The specific heat of ice, and a comparison with melting

Check. 1. 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, from Harvey/NIST at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa: energy per gram per kelvin to change the temperature of ice Ih at that table row. 2. Warming: 40 × 2.10 = 84 J. Melting: 40 × 333.4 = 13,336 J. Invented mass. 3. An official liquid-water cp was not opened as a definite fetched table row for this week, and is not invented or remembered into the calculation. 4. Model: specific heat of ice. Own words should match this idea: The specific heat of ice in this lesson is the Harvey/NIST figure for ice Ih at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa: cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. It is the energy, per gram per kelvin, to change the temperature of the ice at that point in the table, not the energy to melt it. Near-miss: Not a first look at ΔfusH, taught Tuesday, and not last week’s densities as a first look. Not an official liquid-water cp, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2.10 holds at every temperature; the same table gives other cp values at other temperatures, which are not today’s job. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 333.4 / 2.10 ≈ 158.8, about 159. It compares melting 1 g of ice at 0 °C with warming that gram of ice by 1 °C, using the two 0 °C Harvey figures.
  2. Warming: 20 × 2.10 = 42 J. Melting: 6,668 J. Total of those two steps: 6,710 J. Invented mass. The warming step uses the 0 °C cp as a school one-degree figure, not a claim about a long interval.
  3. The table’s cp changes with temperature. Today’s working comparison is the 0 °C pair, 2.10 and 333.4, kept labeled. 2.02 at −10 °C is a different row.
  4. 0.9167 g/cm³ is a density. cp is an energy per gram per kelvin. They are different properties in the same table.

The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
