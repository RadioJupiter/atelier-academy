Latin · Capitulum XII, part 3: the comparative (-ior, -ius)

Check. 1. Mīles nōn fortior est. 2. The Latin comparative is a Latin ending (-ior / -ius), not the English letters -er added to fortis. 3. Model: comparative adjective. Own words should match this idea: The degree of an adjective that compares two things (in English, often the -er form, or “more”). In Latin the first look is the ending -ior (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and -ius (neuter nominative or accusative singular): fortior, fortius, “braver” or “stronger.” Near-miss: Not the positive adjective fortis, -e already taught, and not an English -er added onto a Latin stem (*fortiser). The comparative is a Latin ending, not a borrowed English one. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Scūtum nōn fortius est.
  2. fortius, because scūtum is neuter.
  3. Two heard forms, each used with agreement.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXI: a missing side, from a² + b² = c²

Check. 1. 25, because 49 + 576 = 625 = 25². 2. 20, because 625 − 225 = 400 = 20². 3. Model: a missing side in a right triangle. Own words should match this idea: A leg or the hypotenuse of a right triangle, found from the Pythagorean theorem when the other two sides are known. To find a hypotenuse, add the squares of the legs and take the positive square root. To find a leg, subtract the known leg’s square from the hypotenuse’s square, then take the positive square root. Near-miss: Not adding the legs to get the hypotenuse (6 + 8 is not 10’s reason), and not using the theorem on a triangle that has not been shown to be right. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The theorem adds squares, not the sides themselves. 81 + 144 = 225, so the hypotenuse is 15, not 21.
  2. The theorem applies to a right triangle. Without a right angle, 4² + 5² is not the square of a hypotenuse, and 16 + 25 = 41 is not 6² in any case.

Physics · Stability, and the base of support

Check. 1. The vertical line has a longer horizontal distance to travel before it reaches the edge. 2. The test is whether the vertical line from the center of mass stays inside the base, not a claim that mass by itself forbids tipping. 3. Model: base of support. Own words should match this idea: The area on the ground (or other supporting surface) within which a vertical line from the center of mass must fall, if the object is not to tip. Near-miss: Not the center of mass itself (which is a point in the object), and not a claim that a wider base always means a lower center of mass. A wider base makes it harder for the line to fall outside, which is a different statement. stability (against tipping). Own words should match this idea: An object is stable against tipping while a vertical line from its center of mass falls inside its base of support. It begins to tip when that line reaches the edge of the base. Near-miss: Not “heavy things never tip,” and not a confusion with last week’s rotational equilibrium of a lever as a first look at a new law. The test is the line and the base. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The sketch should show the dashed line meeting the ground outside the base; the block tips.
  2. The lever and Στ = 0 were last week. Today uses the center of mass with a base of support, as a test for tipping.
