Latin · Capitulum XV: sī, nisi, at, and antequam

Check. 1. If you (plural) recite, the schoolmaster is not angry (or is glad, if you used gaudet). 2. Nisi tacēs, audīre nōn possum. 3. At opposes two sides or two people and stands first in its clause. Sed, already taught, corrects or limits what has just been said. 4. Model: sī and nisi. Own words should match this idea: Sī means if. Nisi means if not, unless, or except. Nisi is ne + sī in origin as a compound; in this course it is learned as a conjunction that sets the negative case of an if-clause. Near-miss: Not a first look at nōn, and not a relative clause (quī, quae, quod were taught in Capitulum VIII). at and antequam. Own words should match this idea: At means but on the other hand; it stands first in its clause and sets two sides or two people against each other. Antequam means before, and introduces a clause. Near-miss: Not a first look at sed, which corrects or adds a limitation to what has just been said. At opposes two parties or two sides. Antequam is not the preposition ante used alone (already met as ‘before’ a noun). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Before the schoolmaster comes, the boys recite.
  2. A sentence such as Ego recitō, at tū tacēs, or Vōs recitātis, at nōs tacēmus.
  3. A matching pair, such as Sī tacēs, recitō. Nisi tacēs, nōn recitō.
  4. Antequam introduces a clause with a verb (before the schoolmaster comes). Ante plus a noun is a prepositional phrase (before the door), not a clause.

Mathematics · Geometry XLI: mixed circle measure, and a ring

Check. 1. π(36 − 4) = 32π. 2. C = 10π (length). A = 25π (area). 3. π(R − r)² is the area of a single circle of radius R − r, not the region between the two circles. 4. Model: annulus. Own words should match this idea: An annulus, or ring, is the region between two circles that share a center. Its area is the difference of the two disk areas: A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²), where R is the outer radius and r is the inner radius. Near-miss: Not the area of the outer circle alone, and not π(R − r)², which would be a much smaller circle of radius R − r, not the ring. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Whole area 64π; the sector is (1/4) × 64π = 16π; the remainder is 48π.
  2. 24π = π(R² − 9), so R² = 33, R = √33. Leave it as √33; do not invent a neater official radius.
  3. C = 2πr = 14π, so r = 7. A = 49π.
  4. π(400 − 256) = 144π. Invented path, not a measured track.

Writing & Rhetoric · The short complete essay, assembled

Check. 1. Opening; body paragraphs in outline order; other-side paragraph; close. 2. Correct it from the course text, or rewrite it as a paraphrase and remove the quotation marks. Do not leave a nearly-right quotation standing. 3. The close is not a new body paragraph. A new point belongs earlier, or it belongs to a later essay. 4. Model: assembled short essay. Own words should match this idea: An assembled short essay is the opening, the body paragraphs in outline order, the other-side paragraph, and the close, copied and then repaired so that they read as one piece: pronouns and tense agree, quotations stay exact, and no heading is left out or repeated. Near-miss: Not a first look at the analytical essay (Day 9), the outline (Days 6 and 78), or this week’s paragraph types. Not a demand for a long paper. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete short essay in the taught order. Length may vary, but every heading should be present once.
  2. A list of real repairs, or an honest note that a careful reading found none, with enough detail to be checked.
  3. One marked joint: a transition, a repeated cutoff, or a thesis echo that now matches.
  4. A single claim, matching the opening and the close.

Science · Water: the liquid is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C

Check. 1. 0.99987 g/cm³ at 0 °C, and 1.00000 g/cm³ at 4.0 °C. 2. They come from a different table. Mixing them would invent a single official school value that neither source alone is being used to give. 3. Water near 4 °C is denser than water near 0 °C in this table, so the colder liquid can remain nearer the surface; ice is less dense than the liquid and floats there. 4. Model: maximum density of liquid water near 4 °C. Own words should match this idea: Liquid water is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C. The U.S. Geological Survey, using a Bureau of Reclamation table (1977), lists 0.99987 g/cm³ at 0 °C and 1.00000 g/cm³ at 4.0 °C for the figures used in that table. Those values are not mixed with yesterday’s NIST liquid-water figure. Near-miss: Not a first look at density or at why ice floats, which were taught yesterday. Not a claim that every body of water on Earth is a still lake, and not a blended official number taken from two tables at once. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Ice at 0 °C (NIST 0.9167) is the least dense of the three. Liquid at 4.0 °C in the USGS table is listed as 1.00000, denser than the USGS 0 °C liquid. You should refuse a single ranked list that pretends the NIST 0 °C liquid figure and the USGS 0 °C liquid figure are one number.
  2. No. Ice still floats because ice is less dense than the liquid at 0 °C, which was yesterday’s fact. The 4 °C maximum explains a further point about how a lake cools, not the floating itself.
  3. Currents, wind, depth, and dissolved substances also matter, and this lesson is a density argument for a still body of water, not a survey of every lake.
  4. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so ice cubes float (USGS). Today: the liquid itself is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C, in the USGS / Bureau of Reclamation table.

Literature · Inferno II: three Ladies, recovered courage, and the way (II.94–142)

Check. 1. A gentle Lady in Heaven beseeches Lucìa; Lucìa comes to Beatrice; Beatrice has already come to Virgil, who now tells the speaker. 2. “Even as the flowerets, by nocturnal chill, / Bowed down and closed, when the sun whitens them, / Uplift themselves all open on their stems; / Such I became…” 3. “I entered on the deep and savage way.” 4. Model: three Ladies benedight. Own words should match this idea: In Longfellow, Inferno II.94–126, a “gentle Lady… in Heaven” asks Lucìa to help “thy faithful one”; Lucìa comes to Beatrice, “sitting with the ancient Rachel,” and Beatrice then comes to Virgil. Virgil asks why the speaker delays, “Seeing that three such Ladies benedight / Are caring for thee in the court of Heaven.” Near-miss: Not a first look at Beatrice, named on Tuesday. Not a first look at the Greyhound. Not Canto III. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Beatrice is sitting with the ancient Rachel; she is the one telling this part of the story to Virgil, in the set wording.
  2. The Ladies are caring for him in Heaven’s court, and Virgil’s speech promises good, so the delay is asked “why” — it is treated as baseness in the heart, not as a lack of help.
  3. The line before is “Now go, for one sole will is in us both”: the speaker and Virgil now will the same adventure. The wording does not need a later theory added to it.
  4. The set extract ends at the close of Canto II. Canto III is reserved.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of querer, and a four-stem contrast

Check. 1. Quise, quisiste, quiso, quisimos, quisisteis, quisieron. 2. Yesterday I tried / wanted (on that occasion) to open the door. 3. It can mean “He or she refused to leave,” as a usual school reading of the negative preterite. It is taught carefully because not every later negative will have that English, and this is a pattern, not a complete map. 4. Model: quise. Own words should match this idea: Quise is the first-person singular preterite of querer. The stem is quis-: quise, quisiste, quiso, quisimos, quisisteis, quisieron. In the preterite, the usual school sense is a completed act of wanting, often “I tried to” or, with a negation, “I refused to,” on that occasion — not a long-standing wish. Near-miss: Not a first look at pude, puse, or supe, and not last week’s uv-stems. Not the present quiero. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Pude — poder; puse — poner; supe — saber; quise — querer.
  2. A complete invented sentence, such as Ayer quisimos abrir la puerta. — Yesterday we tried / wanted to open the door.
  3. Pudieron, pusieron, supieron, quisieron.
  4. The preterite, as taught here, names a completed occasion (often a trying). The present quiero names a current want, and a long-standing wish is a different kind of meaning.

Elective · Economics: simple and compound interest, compared (already taught)

Check. 1. Simple 1,300; compound 1,331; difference 31. Invented figures. 2. Interest in later years is paid on interest already earned and added to the principal, as Day 9 already taught. 3. That formula, and the idea of interest on interest, were taught on Day 9. Today only compares them with simple interest on one invented case. 4. Model: simple and compound compared. Own words should match this idea: A comparison of simple and compound interest, in this lesson, uses the already-taught formulas on the same invented principal, rate, and time, and names the difference honestly. Simple: A = P(1 + r t), or A = P + I with I = P r t. Compound, as taught on Day 9: A = P(1 + r)^n, when interest is added to the principal at the end of each of n equal periods. Near-miss: Not a first look at compound interest, at the principal P, or at the rule of 72, all taught on Day 9. Not a first look at A = P(1 + r t), used last week to find P. Not an official bank rate. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Simple: 500 × (1 + 0.20 × 2) = 700. Compound: 500 × (1.20)^2 = 500 × 1.44 = 720. Invented figures.
  2. They are the same: A = P(1 + r) either way, because no earlier interest has yet been added. The formulas diverge after more than one compounding period.
  3. P = 1,300 / (1 + 0.30) = 1,300 / 1.30 = 1,000, which matches today’s invented principal.
  4. 31 is the difference in one invented example (1,000 at 10% for three years, yearly compounding). A different P, r, or n gives a different difference.
