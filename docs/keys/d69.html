Latin · Capitulum XII, part 4: the fourth declension (exercitus, metus)

Check. 1. Exercitus nōn parvus est. 2. The fourth-declension genitive singular is exercitūs, with a long ū, not the second-declension -ī. 3. Model: fourth declension. Own words should match this idea: A declension whose nominative singular often ends in -us and whose genitive singular ends in -ūs (with a long ū), not the second-declension genitive -ī. A first look here is exercitus, -ūs, masculine, “army,” and metus, -ūs, masculine, “fear.” Near-miss: Not a second-declension noun such as gladius, gladiī. The nominative exercitus can look like gladius, but the genitive is exercitūs, not *exercitī. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. metūs.
  2. Sentences such as Exercitus magnus est and Metus nōn magnus est.
  3. Both gladius and exercitus end in -us in the nominative; the genitive (gladiī versus exercitūs) is what distinguishes them.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXII: the converse, and more than one integer triple

Check. 1. Yes: 81 + 144 = 225 = 15², so it is right-angled opposite 15. It is a 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 3. 2. 5-12-13 is already another integer right triangle, and a triangle with legs 1 and 1 has hypotenuse √2, which is not an integer and is not a 3-4-5 side. 3. Model: converse of the Pythagorean theorem. Own words should match this idea: If the sides a, b, and c of a triangle satisfy a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right-angled at the angle opposite c. This is the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, not the same sentence as the theorem itself. Near-miss: Not “every right triangle is 3-4-5.” Many right triangles are not integer-sided, and other integer triples exist (5-12-13, and scaled copies such as 6-8-10). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Theorem: if a triangle is right, with hypotenuse c, then a² + b² = c². Converse: if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right-angled opposite c.
  2. c² = 1 + 4 = 5, so c = √5, which is not an integer. The converse still says the triangle is right; it does not require an integer hypotenuse.

Science · Water: capillary rise

Check. 1. The IAPWS figure is a surface tension at 20 °C, with its own uncertainty. A height would be a new official measurement this course has not opened, so one is not invented from that figure. 2. It is the liquid surface, tensing and wetting the wall, supporting a short column. There is no separate pump. 3. Model: capillary rise. Own words should match this idea: The rise (or, in some liquids, the fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube, caused by the liquid’s surface tension together with the way the liquid meets the wall. For water in a clean glass tube, the surface curves up at the wall and the water rises. Near-miss: Not a pump, and not a new official IAPWS surface-tension figure. The rise is a consequence of surface tension and wetting; a classroom tube’s height is invented unless a sourced height is named. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The meniscus curves down at the wall, and the column may fall rather than rise.
  2. It shows the shape of the meniscus and the qualitative fact of a rise, while the official tension remains the IAPWS table’s.
