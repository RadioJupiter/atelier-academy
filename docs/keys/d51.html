Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: et, sed, and an accusative object

Check. 1. Puer rosam habet. 2. Puella rosam nōn habet. 3. Model: et and sed. Own words should match this idea: Two linking words already used in Capitula I–X: et joins, sed contrasts. They appear today in short original sentences, not in a new chapter. Near-miss: This is not the start of Capitulum XI, and not a claim that et and sed are the only links in Latin. the accusative as object. Own words should match this idea: In these I–X sentences, the thing had or seen takes the accusative (rosam, not rosa) when it is the object of the verb. Near-miss: Not every noun after a verb is accusative, and this is not a new case beyond I–X. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Puella rosam habet (rosam, not rosa).
  2. Three heard items, each used.
  3. The words and the accusative object are a review of I–X, in original sentences, not a new Ørberg chapter.
  4. A copied model sentence.

Mathematics · Algebra XVII: a quadratic inequality, from the graph

Check. 1. 3 < x < 7. 2. x ≤ −1 or x ≥ 4. 3. Model: quadratic inequality. Own words should match this idea: An inequality whose sides, once arranged, give a quadratic on one side, such as (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0. We read it from the already-taught graph: the parabola is below the x-axis, or above it, in intervals marked by the roots. Near-miss: Not a linear inequality, and not a request to invent a new kind of number. The discriminant still only tells how many roots; it does not, by itself, write the inequality’s interval. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Both factors less than 0 would be x < 2 and x < 6, which is just x < 2, the left outside region — and there, if the parabola opens upward, y is positive, not negative. The product is negative when the factors have opposite signs, which is between the roots.
  2. Yes. (4 − 2)(4 − 6) = 2 × (−2) = −4, which is less than 0. x = 4 sits between 2 and 6.
  3. 2 ≤ x ≤ 6 (the same interval, now including the roots, where y = 0).
  4. Treating the product as if it were linear, and combining two “less than” statements, dropped the between-the-roots case, which is the actual solution.

About 3.7 m/s² is the honest report, and it matches the sheet’s listed value. 3. Model: g on Mars, from the same formula. Own words should match this idea: The already-taught formula g = G M / R², applied to NASA fact-sheet figures for Mars, giving about 3.7 m/s², which matches the fact sheet’s own listed gravity 3.7 m/s². G still has three significant figures (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹). Near-miss: Not a new formula, not an invented orbital speed, and not a claim of more significant figures than G and M support. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Stronger by about 2.1 m/s² (3.7 − 1.6). Both are fact-sheet-family figures, already computed in this course’s way.
  2. Today’s formula gives g at the surface, not an orbital speed, and we will not invent one.
  3. The mass 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg (three significant figures).
  4. 9.8 is the already-taught working value beside the 9.79 computation. We will not blend 9.79 and 9.8 into a new official Earth g in order to make the Mars ratio neater.

Atelier · Two forms under one lamp: a sphere and a cube

Check. 1. A sphere and a cube are still geometric forms, not a portrait or a Bargue head. 2. The sphere turns through half-tones; the cube changes at planes. 3. Model: two forms, under one lamp. Own words should match this idea: A sphere and a cube, already studied separately, now set together under one lamp, so the light plane, the dark plane, and a short cast shadow can be compared on both. Still not a head. Near-miss: Not a portrait, not a second lamp invented to “fix” a plane, and not a claim that two forms make a head. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A chosen place, usually upper left or upper right, kept still so the planes and shadows can agree.
  2. A shadow thrown on another surface by the form blocking the lamp. It is not the form’s own dark plane.
  3. A small labeled thumb-nail.
  4. A note that the sitting is a study, not a reward.

Logic · Disjunctive syllogism, and an inclusive “or”

Check. 1. Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q (or the symmetric form). Modus ponens uses a conditional (If P then Q; P; therefore Q), already taught last week. They are different forms. 2. The default “or” allows both sides to be true. P does not, by itself, rule out Q. 3. Model: disjunctive syllogism. Own words should match this idea: A valid form: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. From an “or” and the denial of one side, you may infer the other side. In this course’s default “or,” which is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one, you may not, from P, infer not-Q. Near-miss: Do not infer not-Q from P, unless the problem has said the “or” is exclusive (exactly one). Inclusive “or” allows both. Not modus ponens or modus tollens, which use a conditional, already taught last week. inclusive or, in a first look. Own words should match this idea: An “or” that is true when at least one side is true, including the case where both are true. This is the default in this course unless a problem says exactly one (an exclusive “or”). Near-miss: Not an exclusive “or” (exactly one), and not a claim that English everyday “or” is always exclusive. The school default here is inclusive unless a problem says otherwise. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A sound invented or already-taught pair, with not-P therefore Q (or not-Q therefore P).
  2. Only when the problem has said the “or” is exclusive (exactly one). That is not the default.
  3. A sound sentence: not-P gives Q because of disjunctive syllogism, but the default “or” is inclusive, so P does not give not-Q.
  4. A note that the seminar is not a reward.
