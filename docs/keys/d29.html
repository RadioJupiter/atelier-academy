Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 4: the ablative of price, and tantus

Check. 1. Iūlia illum ānulum decem sēstertiīs emit. 2. Hic ānulus decem sēstertiīs cōnstat. 3. Model: ablative of price. Own words should match this idea: With verbs of buying, selling, and costing (emere, vēndere, cōnstāre), the price is put in the ablative, with no preposition: you pay with that money. Near-miss: When the price is the predicate of pretium est, it is nominative: Pretium est decem sēstertiī. Do not mix the two frames. And this is not the dative (‘to/for someone’). tantus, -a, -um. Own words should match this idea: An adjective meaning ‘so great, of such a size (or price).’ It often pairs with quantus (‘how great, as great as’). Near-miss: Not a demonstrative like hic or ille, though it points to a degree. It agrees like a first-and-second-declension adjective. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. sēstertiīs (ablative); sēstertiī (nominative).
  2. For example, Tantum pretium Iūlia nōn dat.
  3. The dative would mark a person to or for whom, not the money with which the ring costs or is paid. Price, in this frame, is ablative.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. Three original sentences: a pointing word, a price in the right case, and a decision to buy or not.

Mathematics · Geometry XV: the circumcircle, revisited with this fortnight’s theorems

Check. 1. Construct the perpendicular bisectors of at least two sides (AB and BC, or any two); their intersection is the circumcenter; the radius is the distance to a vertex. 2. The diagonals are both 10 (6-8-10), and each is a diameter, so the circumradius is 5. A rectangle is cyclic because each angle is 90°, so opposite angles sum to 180° (and each is an angle in a semicircle on a diagonal). 3. Model: circle through three points (review). Own words should match this idea: A unique circle passes through three points that are not on a single line (the circumcircle of the triangle they form). Its center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides — a construction you already have from week 4. Near-miss: Not a circle through four points in general: four points have to satisfy an extra condition (for example, a cyclic quadrilateral) to lie on one circle. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. None: a circle cannot pass through three distinct collinear points (a line meets a circle in at most two points).
  2. The center must also lie on the perpendicular bisector of another side (AC or BC). One bisector is only the locus of points equidistant from A and B, not yet from C.
  3. MA, MB, and MC must all equal the radius; MA = MB already, because M is the midpoint of the diameter, and MC should equal them because C is on the circle with diameter AB.
  4. No: opposite angles are equal but, in a non-square rhombus, not right, and two equal acute angles do not sum to 180°.
  5. Three points (not collinear) always lie on a unique circle; a fourth lies on that same circle only in a special case, which for a quadrilateral is the cyclic case.

Writing & Rhetoric · Concession and return in a three-paragraph close

Check. 1. A real limit plus a return in new words, on the student’s Friday thesis. 2. A specific qualification named (a hypothesis, a date, a class of source, a net-summary label). 3. Model: concession (three-paragraph close). Own words should match this idea: A real limit or opposing point, granted in the closing paragraph, after which you return to the thesis in new words. The concession must change what a careful reader would have to admit; it is not a decoration. Near-miss: Not “some people might disagree, but they are wrong,” and not a second support paragraph that never grants a limit. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A fake concession identified as fake (personal attack, empty “some say,” or a point that does not touch the claim).
  2. Yes, if a piece of evidence needs a limit on the spot. The close should still gather the limit and return; it should not be an empty bow.
  3. A rewritten return that does not paste the opening sentence.
  4. A confirmed slot in the outline, or an added one.
  5. A revision if needed; otherwise a brief note that both jobs can be heard.

Science · Autumn color: unmasking, and a caution about new reds

Check. 1. Chlorophyll breaks down; carotenoids already present can be seen as yellow or orange. 2. Some reds and purples come from anthocyanins made in autumn, not only from pigments that were waiting, unmasked, in the midsummer leaf. 3. Model: autumn color (careful account). Own words should match this idea: A first account of why many deciduous leaves change color: chlorophyll often breaks down in autumn, so yellow and orange carotenoids already present can become visible. In some species, red or purple anthocyanins are made in the leaf in autumn; those colors are not always a mere unmasking of a pigment that was waiting in midsummer. Near-miss: Not “all red leaves were secretly red all summer,” and not invented percentages of trees, pigments, or days of frost. Weather, species, and the leaf’s own chemistry all matter, and this course will not reduce them to a single number. anthocyanin. Own words should match this idea: A class of red and purple pigments that, in many autumn leaves, are produced in the season itself, rather than merely unmasked. This course names them and keeps the caution; it does not invent a biosynthetic pathway or a percentage of species. Near-miss: Not another name for carotenoids, and not a required ingredient in every autumn leaf (many yellow leaves can be told without them). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Because such a percentage would be invented here: it depends on species and place, and this course has not measured it.
  2. Week 5’s Tuesday was leftover green light from chlorophyll’s selective absorption. Today is about pigments becoming visible, or being newly made, as the leaf’s pigment mix changes in autumn.
  3. Yellow: often unmasking of carotenoids, with ordinary caution. Red: may involve new anthocyanin; do not assume a summer-red waiting under the green.
  4. Identification of the species, observation of this year’s leaves, and, if one wanted chemical support, an actual test — not a slogan from a general lesson.
  5. A short, accurate four-part summary in two sentences, with the net-equation limit and the anthocyanin caution still visible.

Atelier · Accents, and a likeness check against the plate or plaster

Check. 1. Three real planned comparisons (height to width, brow to chin, mouth to nose, ear alignment, and so on). 2. At most three seen accents, with any imagined ones crossed out. 3. Model: accent (head). Own words should match this idea: A small, dark or sharp mark — or a small true highlight — that is placed late, on a found edge or at a tight plane, after the large values of the head are true. Accents are few. Near-miss: Not a sprinkle of highlights on every feature, and not an accent used to “fix” a wrong terminator. likeness to the plate. Own words should match this idea: The match of the drawing to this Bargue head or plaster: tilt, silhouette, feature triangle, and light. It is likeness to the object in front of you, not to a general idea of a person, and not to a living, named sitter. Near-miss: Not flattery, and not a “more beautiful” correction of the plate. The plate or plaster wins, as in week 5. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It forbids shortening the nose toward an ideal. Measure and follow the plate.
  2. The work is a match to a steady object (or plate), not a portrait sitting of an identified person, and the site still holds no identifying personal information.
  3. Remove or quiet the accent and repair the terminator; do not add a second accent to hide the first.
  4. A specific edge that still nearly matches its neighbor, kept lost so the accents do not outline the whole head.
  5. One named strength and one named remaining error, ready for Friday’s critique.

Modern language · Spanish: por and para, a core contrast

Check. 1. Gracias por el agua. Caminamos por el parque. 2. Tengo que estudiar para aprender. 3. Model: para (core uses). Own words should match this idea: A preposition whose core uses in this lesson are purpose (in order to, for a goal), a deadline or a point in time toward which one works, a destination, and a recipient: para estudiar, para el viernes, para Madrid, para Ana. Near-miss: Not a complete map of every use of para, and not interchangeable with por. If you mean duration, exchange, or a general cause, you often need por — tomorrow’s contrast is already today’s caution, and both are practiced now. por (core uses). Own words should match this idea: A preposition whose core uses in this lesson are duration (por dos horas), exchange or a price (por diez euros — a classroom phrase, not a Latin sesterce), motion through or along (por el parque), and a general reason or cause (por eso, por la lluvia, at this lesson’s level). Near-miss: Not purpose-as-a-goal (that is typically para), and not a deadline (para el viernes). Do not treat por and para as “both mean for” and guess. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Four short original sentences, one for each listed job.
  2. Because English ‘for’ covers several jobs that Spanish splits. Duration and thanks typically take por; purpose and deadlines typically take para.
  3. Two heard phrases, each in a correct new sentence.
  4. For example: Tengo que leer a las siete, por media hora; la lectura es para el viernes.
  5. A short planned list, ready for the two-week composition.

Economics & personal finance · Money: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value

Check. 1. Paper and a numeral are not enough: the thing must actually do the jobs (be accepted, be used to write prices, store value reasonably) in a setting. A doodled bill does not. 2. It would be a model of a medium of exchange (and, if they priced in it, a unit of account) — still invented, still labeled, still not a national currency. 3. Model: money (three jobs). Own words should match this idea: In this course’s working definition, money is whatever, in a given setting, does three jobs: a medium of exchange (you can pay with it), a unit of account (prices and debts can be written in it), and a store of value (it can be kept and later spent, with the usual cautions about inflation and risk, which are named, not computed today). Near-miss: Not “a piece of paper with a number on it” as a complete definition (a page of doodles is paper with marks). And not a history of the first coin with a fixed year; early coinage in Asia Minor is often discussed by scholars for the first millennium BCE, and this course will not assign an exact year. medium of exchange. Own words should match this idea: The job of money as a thing commonly accepted in payment, so that trade does not have to be a direct swap of two particular goods. Near-miss: Not a unit of account by itself (you can write prices in a unit you rarely hand over), and not automatically a good store of value in a time of rapid inflation. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Three jobs in the student’s words, plus a well-chosen near-miss that fails at least one job.
  2. Because the early history of coinage is a scholarly discussion this lesson has not settled, and an exact year would be a fake precision. A cautious mention of early coinage in Asia Minor in the first millennium BCE is enough, if any history is offered at all.
  3. If prices in general rise quickly, a stored sum buys less later, so the store-of-value job is weakened. No rate is invented here.
  4. In the Roman setting of the lesson, a sesterce is used as a coin of price (medium of exchange and unit of account, at least in the sentences you wrote). A full history of Roman money, and of how well it stored value in a given year, is beyond this sitting; say that.
  5. A two-sentence definition plus a near-miss, accurate to the three jobs.
