Latin · Capitulum XI, part 1: third-declension neuters (corpus, caput)

Check. 1. corpor-, from the genitive corporis. 2. Puella caput videt. Accusative singular (same form as the nominative). 3. corpora; capita. 4. Caput magnum est. Caput is the subject (nominative). There is no form *capitem. 5. Model: third-declension neuter. Own words should match this idea: A neuter noun of the third family (genitive singular in -is). In the singular, the nominative and the accusative have the same form; in the plural, both of those cases end in -a. The stem is still read from the genitive: corpus, corporis (stem corpor-); caput, capitis (stem capit-). Near-miss: Not a masculine or feminine third-declension noun such as rēx, rēgem, whose accusative singular ends in -em. Do not invent *corporem or *capitem. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Corpus sānum est.
  2. Puer corpora videt. (Accusative plural of a neuter is also in -a.)
  3. Because corpus is neuter: the accusative singular is corpus, not *corporem. corporum would be a genitive plural, which this lesson has not yet asked you to use as a new job.

Mathematics · Geometry XXV: similar triangles

Check. 1. Congruence means the triangles can be placed exactly on one another (corresponding sides equal, corresponding angles equal). Similarity means the same shape: corresponding angles equal and corresponding sides proportional; the sizes may differ. 2. Yes. 10/5 = 14/7 = 16/8 = 2. The scale factor from the first to the second is 2. 3. Corresponding angles are equal, so ∠B = ∠E = 80°. Then ∠C = 180° − 50° − 80° = 50°. (Or, ∠C corresponds to ∠F, but you can also use the angle sum once two angles of △ABC are known.) 4. Those sides do not correspond. In the figure, 3 corresponds to 6 and 5 corresponds to 10; 5 does not correspond to 12. The ratios must use matching sides. 5. Model: similar triangles. Own words should match this idea: Two triangles are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are proportional — they are the same shape, not necessarily the same size. We write △ABC ∼ △DEF when angle A = angle D, angle B = angle E, angle C = angle F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD. Near-miss: Not congruence. Congruent triangles (△ABC ≅ △DEF) can be placed exactly on one another; similar triangles may differ in size. Equal angles alone, without a check of which angles correspond, is not yet a similarity statement. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Yes: for example △ABC ∼ △DEF if 2, 3, 4 match 4, 6, 8 in that order. Scale factor 2.
  2. No. 4/2 = 2, 6/3 = 2, but 10/4 = 2.5. The three ratios are not equal, so the sides are not proportional.
  3. PQ/ST = QR/TU, so 6/9 = 10/TU. Then TU = 15.

Writing & Rhetoric · The Toulmin model: claim, grounds, and warrant

Check. 1. Grounds are the supporting material under a claim: a fact, a detail, or a short accurate quotation. In this course that is the same job Day 8 called evidence. Toulmin’s name is data or grounds. 2. A warrant is the general rule that makes those grounds count as support for that claim. Grounds alone are details; without a warrant it is not yet clear why they support this claim. 3. Claim: the bread is stale. Grounds: it is hard. Warrant: stale bread goes hard (so hardness, in this case, counts as support for staleness). 4. It only repeats the claim. A warrant has to be the rule that joins the grounds to the claim, not a second copy of the claim. 5. Model: Toulmin model. Own words should match this idea: A named way of analyzing an argument into a claim, the data or grounds that support it, and a warrant — the general rule that makes those grounds count as support for that claim. In this course the three-part model is taken from Stephen Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958). Near-miss: Not a new replacement for the claim, evidence, and warrant already taught on Day 8. Toulmin’s word for the supporting material is data or grounds; this course already called that material evidence. The model does not, by itself, make a claim true. grounds (Toulmin). Own words should match this idea: Toulmin’s name for the supporting material under a claim — a fact, a detail, or a short quotation given accurately. In this course it is the same job as evidence, taught on Day 8. Near-miss: Not the claim itself, and not the general rule (the warrant) that joins the grounds to the claim. A restated claim is not grounds. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: claim, the door is closed; grounds, no daylight shows at the edge, and the handle will not turn inward; warrant, a closed door in this room hides the daylight at the edge and will not turn inward. Other accurate three-part invented examples are accepted.
  2. Grounds, and a warrant that is not just the claim again. A restated claim does not support a claim.
  3. Yes. The definition allows a short quotation, given accurately, as grounds (the same job as evidence on Day 8).

Physics · Rotational equilibrium of a rigid body

Check. 1. Στ = 0 about the chosen point, and ΣF = 0. The unit of torque is the newton-meter (N·m). 2. Each end is 1.50 m from the center, so the right-hand force is also 40 N downward. The support supplies 80 N upward. 3. Zero. The perpendicular distance from the support to the line of the force is zero, so τ = F × 0 = 0. 4. A joule is the unit of work or energy (a force along a displacement). Torque is a turning effect, still written in N·m and named as a torque, as Day 33 already taught. 5. Model: rotational equilibrium. Own words should match this idea: A rigid body is in rotational equilibrium about a point or an axis when the sum of the torques about that point is zero (Στ = 0): the clockwise torques and the counterclockwise torques balance, so there is no net tendency to start turning. A body that remains at rest also has no net force (ΣF = 0). Near-miss: Not the already-taught market equilibrium of supply and demand. Not a claim that one force of zero is enough, or that torques may be added without naming the point they are taken about. The unit of each torque is still the newton-meter (N·m), not the joule. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Yes: 15 N × 1.00 m and 15 N × 1.00 m in opposite senses, so Στ = 0. The support supplies 30 N upward.
  2. No. The left torque is 15 N·m and the right torque is 10 N·m; they do not cancel. Στ ≠ 0 about the center.
  3. Rotational equilibrium is a balance of torques (and, for rest, of forces) on a rigid body. Market equilibrium is a price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The word is the same; the jobs are different.

History · 410 CE: Augustine on the sack of Rome

Check. 1. The Goths, under Alaric their king. 2. They attributed the calamity to the Christian religion (and, in Dods’s wording, began to blaspheme the true God). This, Augustine says, prompted him to undertake the defense of the city of God. 3. A footnote in the Dods edition dates the sack A.D. 410. That year is not a number inside the set sentence itself. 4. Augustine is looking back on why he wrote The City of God. He tells us that the sack had happened and how some people explained it, not what he saw street by street during the fighting. 5. Model: late antiquity. Own words should match this idea: The name later writers give to the long transition from the later Roman world to the early medieval world. It is a period name, not a single year, and it is not a claim that the empire vanished on one date. Near-miss: Not a single day on a calendar, and not a synonym for “the fall of Rome” as if everything ended at once. This week’s work starts from a named source, not from a slogan. sack of Rome (410). Own words should match this idea: The taking and plundering of Rome in 410 CE by the Goths under Alaric, as later dated in the Dods edition of Augustine. This course uses Augustine’s own later account of why he wrote The City of God, not a battlefield report, and it does not invent a death toll. Near-miss: Not a first-person description of the sack written from the walls, and not a source for a death toll or for a three-day timetable this course has not set. 410 is the year given in the edition’s footnote, not a figure invented here. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A period name for the long transition from the later Roman world to the early medieval world, not a single year in which the empire vanished.
  2. “stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king.”
  3. The set extract does not give a death toll. The figure would be invented, not sourced.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -ar verbs

Check. 1. hablé, I spoke; hablaste, you spoke (familiar singular); habló, he / she / you (formal) spoke; hablamos, we spoke; hablasteis, you spoke (familiar plural); hablaron, they / you (plural) spoke. 2. Yo compré. (or: compré.) Tú cantaste. (or: cantaste.) 3. The accent marks the él / ella / usted past form (habló). Without it the form is not correctly written. hablamos has the same spelling in the present and the preterite, so a time word or the surrounding past forms tell you it is past. 4. Nosotros compramos pan. Ellos compraron pan. The nosotros ending is -amos; the ellos ending is -aron. 5. Model: preterite of regular -ar verbs. Own words should match this idea: A simple-past tense of Spanish regular -ar verbs, used for a completed action in the past. The endings, shown on hablar, are -é, -aste, -ó, -amos, -asteis, -aron: hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablasteis, hablaron. Instruction in this lesson is in English; the Spanish words are the forms being taught. Near-miss: Not the already-taught present (hablo, hablas, habla…). Not an irregular past such as fui or tuve, which this week does not teach. Note that nosotros hablamos looks like the present; the time word or the context tells you it is past. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. hablamos; hablaron; habló.
  2. canté; cantó; cantaron.
  3. hablo is the already-taught present, “I speak” or “I am speaking,” not a completed past. Today’s form is hablé.

Logic · Celarent, and the major and minor terms

Check. 1. No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. E is a universal negative (No…), A a universal affirmative (All…), so the mood is EAE. 2. Major term P: gases. Minor term S: irons. Middle term M: metals. 3. Celarent’s first premise and conclusion are negative (No M are P; No S are P); Barbara’s are affirmative (All M are P; All S are P). Both are valid first-figure moods, with M as subject of the first premise and predicate of the second. 4. If the middle term never refers to the whole of its class in either premise, it does not actually link S and P, and the argument is invalid, as Day 36 already taught. The name Celarent is only for the valid EAE pattern. 5. Model: Celarent. Own words should match this idea: The traditional name for a valid syllogism in the first figure whose vowels are E, A, and E: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. The middle term is the subject of the first premise and the predicate of the second, as it was in Barbara. Near-miss: Not Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), and not an invalid form with an undistributed middle. Celarent’s conclusion is negative and universal (No S are P), not an affirmative All S are P. major term and minor term. Own words should match this idea: In a syllogism, the major term (P) is the predicate of the conclusion, and the minor term (S) is the subject of the conclusion. The middle term (M), already taught, links them in the premises and does not appear in the conclusion. Near-miss: Not a claim that “major” means more important in ordinary speech. The names mark jobs in the syllogism: S and P in the conclusion, M in both premises. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: No owls are fish. All the birds in this barn are owls. Therefore no bird in this barn is a fish. S: birds in this barn; M: owls; P: fish. Other accurate invented Celarents are accepted.
  2. The conclusion has the wrong quality: Celarent’s conclusion is No S are P (no irons are gases), not All S are P. The student has drifted into a Barbara conclusion from Celarent premises.
  3. It appears in both premises and must not appear in the conclusion.
