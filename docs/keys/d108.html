Mathematics · Geometry LXI: sine and cosine of complementary angles

Check. 1. The angle sum of a triangle is 180°, already taught. One angle is 90°, so the other two add to 90°. 2. The side opposite A is the side adjacent to the other acute angle, which is 90° − A. Both ratios use that side over the same hypotenuse. 3. Sin A = 3/5, cos A = 4/5, sin(90° − A) = 4/5, cos(90° − A) = 3/5. 4. Sine was taught on Monday and cosine on Tuesday. Today is the complementary relation between them. 5. Model: sine and cosine of complementary angles. Own words should match this idea: The two acute angles in a right triangle are complementary: they add to 90°. The side opposite A is the side adjacent to the other acute angle, B, and B = 90° − A. So sin A = cos B, and sin(90° − A) = cos A. In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5 and cos A = 4/5, while sin B = 4/5 and cos B = 3/5. Degrees only; the radian remains reserved. Near-miss: Not a first look at sine or cosine, already taught, or at the fact that the acute angles of a right triangle add to 90°, which follows from the already-taught angle sum of a triangle. Not a unit-circle identity beyond this triangle fact. Classroom lengths remain invented. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Sin A = 5/13, cos A = 12/13, sin(90° − A) = 12/13.
  2. Sin A = 3/5, because sin A = cos B when A and B are the two acute angles.
  3. This course has used degrees only. The radian remains reserved.
  4. The unit circle has not been taught. Today’s reason is the sides of a right triangle.

Physics · Damping and the shape of a resonance peak, from OpenStax 16.8

Check. 1. Where the driving frequency equals the natural frequency. That is resonance, already defined on Monday. 2. The peak is higher and narrower, because less energy is removed by the damping force. 3. The amplitude stays smaller, but the system responds at more frequencies. 4. OpenStax 16.8 does not give those official figures for use as classroom measurements. They would be invented. 5. Model: damping and the resonance peak. Own words should match this idea: OpenStax 16.8, with its graph of amplitude against driving frequency, shows that resonance still occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, for each amount of damping shown. The greatest response, the highest peak, is for the least damping, because less energy is removed by the damping force. The least damping also gives the narrowest peak: the system responds strongly only very near the natural frequency. Heavy damping lowers the amplitude and makes the system respond at more frequencies. These are OpenStax’s qualitative claims from that figure, not invented official peak heights. Near-miss: Not a first look at Monday’s definitions of driving frequency, natural frequency, and resonance, or at last week’s three names for damped motion, except as already-taught damping now used to read the peak. Not a quantitative formula for peak width, which 16.8 does not give for use here. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Little damping lets the system resonate strongly at a very specific frequency. Heavy damping keeps the amplitude small, which is wanted in a suspension, at the cost of a broader response.
  2. Resonance was defined on Monday. Today is how damping changes the peak.
  3. Damping, already taught, is the difference among the curves in the figure: less or more energy removed as the system is driven.
  4. Section 16.8 does not set out a formula for that width to be used here.

Modern language · Spanish: the imperfect of ver, with iba and era

Vía would drop that e, which this imperfect does not do. 3. Yo veía: I was seeing, or used to see. Yo vi: I saw, a completed event. Yo iba: I was going. Yo era: I was, a past state. 4.
