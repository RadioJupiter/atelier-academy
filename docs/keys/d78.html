Latin · Capitulum XIV: mēcum and tēcum, and a dative of interest

Check. 1. I work with you; with us. 2. A model: Venī tēcum nōn possum is a different idea; Venī mēcum. is enough. Other original sentences accepted. 3. Model: mēcum. Own words should match this idea: Mēcum means with me. With the personal pronouns, cum is written after the pronoun and joined to it: mēcum, tēcum, sēcum, nōbīscum, vōbīscum, not cum mē. Near-miss: Not cum mē as a standard form, and not a new preposition besides cum. The joining of cum to these pronouns is the point. dative of interest. Own words should match this idea: The dative of interest (dativus commodi) names the person for whom something is so, or for whom it matters: Mihi necesse est labōrāre, it is necessary for me to work. Near-miss: Not the dative of possession already met in Capitulum XII (the ‘is-to-me’ having), used as if it were a new first look at that earlier job. Today’s dative names the person for whom the fact holds. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The dative of possession (Capitulum XII) was ‘is-to-me’ having. Today’s dative names the person for whom a fact holds, as in mihi necesse est. Same case, different job.
  2. With me; to/for me.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXVI: the area of a trapezoid

Check. 1. ½(7 + 13) × 6 = 10 × 6 = 60. 2. The height is the perpendicular distance between the bases, 6, not a slanted side. 3. Model: trapezoid. Own words should match this idea: In the American usage used on this site, a trapezoid is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides, called the bases. The height is the perpendicular distance between those bases. Near-miss: Not a parallelogram (which has two pairs of parallel sides). Not the British use of ‘trapezium’ for this shape; this course says trapezoid. area of a trapezoid. Own words should match this idea: The area of a trapezoid is the average of the two parallel bases times the perpendicular height: A = ½(a + b) × h. Near-miss: Not the product of one base and a slanted side, and not ½bh using only one of the two bases. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. If both bases are 9, both pairs of sides would need a closer look: equal parallel sides may make a parallelogram (two pairs of parallels), which this course does not call a trapezoid. The expression ½(9 + 9)h still equals 9h, the parallelogram formula.
  2. ½(5 + 11) × 4 = 8 × 4 = 32.

Writing & Rhetoric · A multi-paragraph outline, for an analytical essay

Check. 1. A model: ‘Virgil’s offer to be a guide is a rescue from the beast, not a promise of an easy road.’ Other arguable, text-bound theses accepted. 2. That because-clause is circular reasoning, yesterday’s fallacy. The outline needs grounds from the poem. 3. Model: multi-paragraph outline. Own words should match this idea: A multi-paragraph outline plans an analytical essay before the draft: a thesis, then a short plan for each paragraph (a claim and the grounds or quotations it will use), written in order. It extends the single-paragraph outline already taught on Day 6. Near-miss: Not a first look at the analytical essay, the thesis, or the single-paragraph outline, which were taught on Days 6 and 9. Not a draft of full paragraphs. Not a précis (week 14), which shortens a source rather than planning an argument. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Complete outlines accepted if they have a thesis, two paragraph plans with grounds, and an other-side note, and if they stay with a course text.
  2. A précis shortens a source in order. An outline plans an argument you will write.

Physics · The simple pendulum, for a small swing

Check. 1. OpenStax 16.4 gives T = 2π√(L/g) for amplitudes less than about 15°. A larger swing is outside that stated limit. 2. Four times the length: T is twice as long, about 4.0 s. 3. Model: simple pendulum. Own words should match this idea: A simple pendulum, in the school model used here, is a bob on a light string or rod of length L that swings through a small angle. For amplitudes less than about 15°, OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.4, gives the period T = 2π√(L/g). The period depends on the length and on g, and is independent of mass. Near-miss: Not a heavy rod whose mass cannot be ignored, and not a large swing, for which OpenStax limits the simple formula to amplitudes less than about 15°. Not Monday’s general oscillation treated as if the formula had already been used. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. In this model the period is independent of mass. The heavier bob, for a small swing, has the same T.
  2. L is one-fourth of 1.00 m, so T is half of about 2.0 s, about 1.0 s.

History · A structure that seems to float, in Dewing I.1.33–34

Check. 1. It seems unsecured, as if it floated and put those inside in peril; actually it is firmly and securely braced. Close wording accepted. 2. A quarter-sphere, in this wording. 3. Model: seeming to float (Dewing I.1.34). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s translation of Buildings I.1.33–34, the upper part of the east structure ends in “the fourth part of a sphere,” and above it a crescent-shaped structure rises, “marvellous in its grace, but by reason of the seeming insecurity of its composition altogether terrifying.” It “seems somehow to float in the air on no firm basis,” yet “actually it is braced with exceptional firmness and security.” Near-miss: Not a claim that the masonry has no supports, and not last week’s light-in-the-shrine wording treated as this floating-and-braced contrast. “Marvellous” is Dewing’s spelling, kept inside the quotation. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Monday described the east half-cylinder rising to a height. Today is the next sentence: the quarter-sphere and the crescent above it.
  2. “Yet actually it is braced with exceptional firmness and security.”

Atelier · A value map of the whole head, still in one light

Check. 1. The light family and the shadow family. 2. The detail is built on a wrong value; the cheek will not sit in the same light as the rest of the head. 3. Model: value map of the head. Own words should match this idea: A value map of the head is a slow, whole-head statement of the light family and the shadow family, in one held light, without adding a new medium and without going back to a feature-by-feature pass as if the families had not been grouped. Near-miss: Not a first look at a value pass on a single feature (week 15), and not yet grisaille in paint (tomorrow). Not a second lamp, and not a finished portrait. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. If the lamp moves, the map is of a different light, and the week’s families cannot be finished.
  2. Tomorrow’s grisaille, in paint or as a monochrome rehearsal, needs a whole-head map to follow, not a set of separate features.

Modern language · Spanish: fui, tuve, estuve, and hice, contrasted

Check. 1. Estuve; tuvo. 2. Fue is the preterite of ser or ir. Estar in the él/ella preterite is estuvo. 3. Model: choosing fui, tuve, estuve, or hice. Own words should match this idea: Fui is the preterite of ser or ir; tuve is tener; estuve is estar; hice is hacer (with z in hizo). They are different verbs, already taught, and they are not interchangeable because they happen to be irregular. Near-miss: Not a first look at any of these four sets. Not a new irregular verb. Not the regular -ar / -er / -ir preterites treated as if they had not been taught. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Hizo, with z.
  2. A model: Ayer fui a la tienda, pero estuve en casa por la mañana. Other clear contrasts accepted.

Elective · Art history: columns in a choral dance, from Dewing I.1.35

Check. 1. The arrangement is a receding semicircle, which is the form Dewing then compares to a choral dance. A straight colonnade would be a different form. 2. It is the next sentence of the same east-end description in Buildings I.1, already begun on Monday. 3. Model: columns in a choral dance (Dewing I.1.35). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s translation of Buildings I.1.35, the columns on either side of the east structure “do not stand in a straight line, but they retreat inward in the pattern of the semicircle, as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance,” and above them hangs a crescent-shaped structure. Near-miss: Not a first look at the half-cylinder or at the seeming-to-float crescent, which today’s history block (or Monday’s and Wednesday’s history) already teaches from I.1.31–34. Today’s new extract is the columns of I.1.35 and Dewing’s dance simile. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. “As if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance.”
  2. It is a diagram of the idea of a receding semicircle, not a surveyed measurement or an official count.
