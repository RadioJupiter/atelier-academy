Latin · Capitulum XI, part 2: pectus, ōs, crūs, and cor

Check. 1. crūr-, from crūris; nominative plural crūra. 2. Puella ōs habet. (ōs is the object and looks like the nominative.) 3. ōs, ōris (long ō) means mouth or face. os, ossis means bone. They are different words. 4. Pectus hominis videō. The accusative singular of pectus is pectus. 5. Model: third-declension neuter (more nouns). Own words should match this idea: The same neuter rule applied to more body nouns of the third family: pectus, pectoris (chest); ōs, ōris (mouth or face, with a long ō); crūs, crūris (leg). Nominative equals accusative in the singular; those cases end in -a in the plural. Do not confuse ōs, ōris with os, ossis, “bone,” which is a different word. Near-miss: Not os, ossis “bone,” and not a masculine accusative in -em. ōs (mouth) has a long ō; keep the two words apart. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Crūra longa sunt.
  2. Puer pectus videt.
  3. cor. It is neuter, so nominative and accusative singular are the same.

Mathematics · Geometry XXVI: AA similarity

Check. 1. The angle sum is 180° in every triangle, so the third angle is determined by the first two. If two pairs match, the third pair matches automatically, and the triangles are similar. 2. Yes. The three angles are the same set, so each pair can be matched (30° with 30°, 60° with 60°, 90° with 90°). AA applies (in fact all three pairs match). 3. Yes. In △GHJ, ∠J = 80°, so the angles are 50°, 50°, and 80°. In △KLM, ∠M = 50°, so the angles are 50°, 80°, and 50°. Both are 50-50-80 triangles, so they are similar. One matching is △GHJ ∼ △KLM with G with K, H with M, and J with L (or G with M, H with K, and J with L). 4. AA needs two pairs of equal angles. A pair of equal sides is not an angle, and one pair of angles is not enough. The student has not yet used AA. 5. Model: AA similarity. Own words should match this idea: If two angles of one triangle are equal to two angles of another triangle, the third angles are equal as well (because the angle sum is 180°), and the triangles are similar. The name AA records that two pairs of equal angles are enough. Near-miss: Not a claim that two equal sides, without equal angles, already give similarity. Not SAS or SSS congruence from Term 1, which decide same size as well as same shape. AA is about similarity, not congruence, unless you have more information. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ∠B = 60°, and ∠Z = 85°. The angles are 35°, 60°, 85° in both, so yes: △ABC ∼ △XYZ, with A with X, B with Y, C with Z.
  2. No. AA gives equal angles, hence similarity. The sides may still be in a ratio other than 1, so the triangles need not be the same size.
  3. Yes. In △PQR, ∠R = 60°. In △STU, ∠T = 30°. Both are 90-30-60 triangles, so they are similar (for example △PQR ∼ △SUT, matching 90°, 30°, 60°).

It is vaguer than the sentence, and it is not what the sentence actually claims. The sentence names a test of whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure. 4. Science · Water: melting and boiling at one atmosphere

Check. 1. They were the original defining points of the Celsius scale for pure water at one atmosphere. With ITS-90, that definition ceased to be valid; the scale is no longer fixed by those two water points. 2. Approximately 99.974 °C, which is 373.124 K. 3. Evaporation is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and it can happen below the boiling point. Boiling is the change throughout the liquid at the boiling point. The earlier term is kept distinct, not renamed. 4. On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 99.974 °C. “About 100 °C” is the ordinary approximation, not an exact ITS-90 value. 5. Model: melting point (at 1 atm). Own words should match this idea: The temperature at which a solid and its liquid are in equilibrium at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. For water, the Celsius scale was originally defined so that this point, at one atmosphere, was 0 °C. That historical definition is no longer how the scale is fixed, but “about 0 °C” remains a good ordinary description of water’s ice point at 1 atm. Near-miss: Not the triple point, which is a different condition (solid, liquid, and vapor together at a unique pressure and temperature). Not a claim that every substance melts at 0 °C. Evaporation, already taught, is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and is not the same as melting. boiling point (at 1 atm). Own words should match this idea: The temperature at which a liquid boils at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. The Celsius scale was originally defined so that this point for pure water, at one atmosphere, was 100 °C. On the International Temperature Scale of 1990, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 99.974 °C (373.124 K). “About 100 °C” remains a good ordinary approximation. Near-miss: Not melting, and not the triple point. Not a claim that the modern measured boiling point is defined to be exactly 100 °C. The historical 100 and the ITS-90 figure must not be blended into a fake official exact 100. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. About 0 °C, and about 100 °C.
  2. 373.124 − 273.15 = 99.974 °C, which matches the IAPWS figure.
  3. Those temperatures depend on pressure. Today’s figures are for one atmosphere; a different pressure would move the point.

Literature · Inferno, Canto I: the opening, in Longfellow’s 1867 translation

Check. 1. “Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway had been lost.” 2. The speaker has lost the direct, right path; that is why he finds himself in the dark forest. 3. Dante’s Italian uses terza rima; Longfellow’s English here is unrhymed. A rhyming version would be a different translation, not the set wording. 4. Later lines of Canto I, and later cantos, remain reserved. A quotation must come from the nine set lines, not from memory of the beasts or of Virgil, which have not yet been assigned. 5. Model: Inferno. Own words should match this idea: The first of the three canticles of Dante’s Divine Comedy, the journey through Hell. In this course the opening is read in Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s 1867 public-domain translation. Later cantos remain reserved. Near-miss: Not a modern news sense of “inferno” as any large fire, and not a claim that this English version keeps Dante’s Italian rhyme. Later of the canticle has not yet been assigned. canto. Own words should match this idea: One of the chief numbered divisions of a long poem. Dante’s Inferno is divided into cantos; this week sets only the opening of Canto I (lines 1–9 in Longfellow’s translation). Near-miss: Not a chapter of a novel, and not a name for the whole Divine Comedy. A canto is one division; Inferno contains many, of which only the opening of the first is set this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. “What was this forest savage, rough, and stern.”
  2. A canto is one of the chief numbered divisions of the long poem; this week sets only the opening of Canto I.
  3. It is a paraphrase, not a quotation of Longfellow. The set first line is “Midway upon the journey of our life.”

Atelier · Proportions of the head, checked against the plate

Modern language · Spanish: preterite and present of regular -ar verbs, side by side

Check. 1. Present: hablo, habla. Preterite: hablé, habló. 2. Tú compraste; tú compras. 3. The nosotros form is spelled the same in both tenses. You can make the past clear with other preterite forms in the same sentence, or (from tomorrow) with a time word such as ayer. 4. Ustedes compraron. Yo canté. The endings must match the person.

Music theory · Triad inversion: the third or the fifth in the bass

Check. 1. First inversion has the third of the triad in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The other two tones are stacked above. 2. Root position, F; first inversion, A; second inversion, C. 3. Interval inversion turns a single two-note interval by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth). Triad inversion keeps all three tones of the triad and changes which of them is in the bass. 4. The three tones are still C, E, and G: a C-major triad, now in first inversion (E in the bass). It has not become a different triad. 5. Model: triad inversion. Own words should match this idea: A change of which triad tone is in the bass: root position has the root in the bass; first inversion has the third in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The three tones of the triad are the same; only the lowest note changes. Near-miss: Not interval inversion, already taught on Day 37, which turns a single interval by moving one of its two notes by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth, and so on). Triad inversion is about which chord tone is in the bass, not about inverting one interval. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. First inversion: F♯–A–D (F♯ in the bass). Second inversion: A–D–F♯ (A in the bass).
  2. Second inversion.
  3. “A fourth” is interval-inversion language for two notes. Today’s job is to name which triad tone is in the bass, not to rename the whole chord as a single interval.
