Latin · Capitulum XIV: uter, neuter, alter, and uterque

Check. 1. Which of the two boys is writing? 2. A model: Uterque discipulus legit. Other original sentences accepted if uterque is used correctly. 3. Model: uterque. Own words should match this idea: Uterque (utraque, utrumque) means each of the two, or both taken as a pair. It refers to a pair, and it often takes a singular verb: uterque puer dormit, each of the two boys is asleep. Near-miss: Not ambo used as a first look here, and not a word for a group of three or more. Uter, by itself, asks ‘which of the two?’; uterque answers ‘each of the two.’ alter. Own words should match this idea: Alter (altera, alterum) means the one, or the other, of two. Near-miss: Not alius, which is ‘another’ when more than two are in view. Alter stays with a pair. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Alter is one of a pair. A word such as alius is the usual choice when the contrast is not limited to two. Today’s job is the pair.
  2. Each of the two; neither of the two.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXIV: the area of a parallelogram

Check. 1. 54. 2. The height must be perpendicular to the base. 7 is a slanted length, so 9 × 7 would not be the area. 3. Model: area of a parallelogram. Own words should match this idea: The area of a parallelogram is the base times the corresponding perpendicular height: A = b × h. The height is perpendicular to the chosen base, not a slanted side. Near-miss: Not the product of two slanted sides, and not a first look at the shape itself (the parallelogram as a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel was taught on Day 14). The new job is the area. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 60.
  2. The height is not a slanted side. Without a perpendicular height, 10 × 8 is not the area.

Writing & Rhetoric · Circular reasoning

Check. 1. A model: ‘This rule is fair because it applies the same time limit to everyone in the class’ (invented ground). Other inspectable grounds accepted. 2. A straw man answers a distorted claim. A circle restates its own claim as if that were support. 3. Model: circular reasoning. Own words should match this idea: Circular reasoning is a flaw in which a claim is supported by a restatement of the same claim, or by a premise that already assumes what was supposed to be shown. It is a kind of informal fallacy, not merely a false statement. Near-miss: Not a merely false claim (a claim can be false and still be argued in a straight line). Not last week’s straw man, false dilemma, or ad hominem, which are different flaws. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: ‘He is the best because no one is better than he is’ (invented), which restates ‘best.’ Other clear circles accepted.
  2. The flaw is in the support, not only in whether the claim happens to be true.

Physics · Oscillation, period, and frequency: a first look

Check. 1. f = 1/4 cycle per second. 2. There is no repeating back-and-forth cycle. 3. Model: oscillation. Own words should match this idea: An oscillation is a repeating back-and-forth motion about a central position, such as a hanging bob swinging to and fro, or a mass on a spring moving in and out, once the motion repeats. Near-miss: Not a single shove that does not repeat, and not a trip that goes forward without returning. Not tomorrow’s formula for a simple pendulum, which is not yet today’s job. period (oscillation). Own words should match this idea: The period, T, is the time for one complete cycle of an oscillation — for example, the time from a leftmost position out and back to that same leftmost position. Near-miss: Not frequency, which is how many cycles happen in one second. Not a calendar period, such as a month, from another subject. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. T = 1/2 s.
  2. It is a classroom figure for this sitting, not an official laboratory certificate, and it is not taken from Wednesday’s formula.

History · The east face of the church, and a half-cylinder, in Dewing I.1.31–32

Check. 1. A half-cylinder: half a circle in plan, not a straight line. Dewing’s wording, or a close honest paraphrase, accepted. 2. It is a diagram to show the curving form, not a surveyed measurement of the building. 3. Model: half-cylinder (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s translation of Procopius, Buildings I.1.32, the east end of the church is described as masonry built not in a straight line, but curving inward on its flanks and receding at the middle, so that it forms the shape of half a circle, “which those who are skilled in such matters call a half-cylinder.” Near-miss: Not a surveyed plan of the church, and not last week’s wording about height, proportion, and light in I.1.27–30 treated as if it were this east-end description. The new extract starts at I.1.31. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Last week described height, proportion, and light. Today begins the east face and its half-cylinder, the next sentences in the same source.
  2. An official number of meters or feet.

Atelier · The light family and the shadow family, on the head

Check. 1. The light family, because that plane still faces the lamp. 2. Core shadow was taught on the egg and the sphere (Days 6 and 41–43). Today it is applied, in the shadow family, on the head. 3. Model: light family and shadow family. Own words should match this idea: The light family is the set of values on the surfaces that still face the light (highlight and midtone). The shadow family is the set of values on the surfaces that have turned away (core shadow, reflected light, and, if it is present, the cast shadow). The two families are kept distinct, rather than averaged into one middle gray. Near-miss: Not a first look at highlight, midtone, core shadow, or reflected light, which were taught on the egg and the sphere (Days 6 and 41–43). Not a new lamp position mid-sitting. Not last week’s feature-by-feature value pass treated as if the families themselves were new words then; last week placed value on features already sited. Today the new job is to group those values into two families on the whole head. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The head flattens; the turn of the form is lost.
  2. Do not move it. One light, held, as already taught.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of tener

Check. 1. Tuvo; tuvimos. 2. A model: Ella tuvo tiempo. Other short invented drills accepted. 3. Model: preterite of tener. Own words should match this idea: The preterite of tener is tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvisteis, tuvieron: a uv-stem, not the regular -er pattern of comer, and not the fui set of ser and ir already taught last week. Near-miss: Not the present tengo, and not hice from last week’s hacer. Not a first look at the preterite as a tense (regular -ar, -er, and -ir, and ser, ir, and hacer, were already taught). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A regular -er preterite would be comí, comiste, comió… Tener uses tuv- and tuve, not tení.
  2. I had; they had (or you formal plural had).

Elective · Logic: Cesare, a second-figure EAE syllogism

Check. 1. No squares are circles (E); all hoops in this drawing are circles (A); therefore no hoops in this drawing are squares (E). Middle term: circles. 2. It is the predicate of both premises. 3. Model: second figure. Own words should match this idea: In a second-figure syllogism, the middle term is the predicate of both premises. That is a different placement from the first figure (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio), already taught, in which the middle term is the subject of one premise and the predicate of the other. Near-miss: Not a first look at Celarent, which is first-figure EAE. Cesare uses the same vowel pattern EAE, but the middle term sits in a different place. Not a new look at informal fallacies, which belong to writing this week and last week. Cesare. Own words should match this idea: Cesare is a second-figure syllogism of the form EAE: a universal negative premise, a universal affirmative premise, and a universal negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. Near-miss: Not Celarent, which is also EAE but first figure (middle term as subject of the first premise and predicate of the second, in the usual school order). The name Cesare is a medieval mnemonic; the vowels E-A-E are the mood. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio were already taught in weeks 13–15. Today opens the second figure with Cesare.
  2. A clear invented EAE with the middle term as both predicates is accepted, if it is labeled invented as a form.
