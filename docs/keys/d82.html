Latin · Capitulum XV: sum and possum, the full present tables

Check. 1. Sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt. 2. Possumus is pot- plus sumus, with t becoming s: pos-sumus. Potestis is pot- plus estis; estis begins with e, so the t stays. 3. You (singular) are not able to hear. 4. Model: possum, the full present. Own words should match this idea: Posse, to be able, is pot- placed in front of esse. The t becomes s when esse begins with s: possum, possumus, possunt. The second-person forms keep t: potes, potestis. It takes a complementary infinitive: recitāre possum. Near-miss: Not a first look at possum, potes, and potest, which were taught with the infinitive in Capitulum X (Day 37). Today the table closes, including possumus, potestis, and a reviewed possunt. Mathematics · Geometry XXXIX: the area of a circle

Check. 1. A = π × 7² = 49π. 2. πr² = 16π, so r² = 16 and r = 4 (a length is positive). 3. Half the circumference is πr; times the height r of the rearranged sectors, that product is πr², the school formula for the area. 4. Model: area of a circle. Own words should match this idea: The area of a circle of radius r is A = πr², with the same π taught yesterday as C / d. This is the school formula used in this course. Near-miss: Not the circumference 2πr, which is a length, and not yesterday’s 10π of a different circle treated as if it were an area. Not a formal Euclidean proof; the sector picture is a limiting argument. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. r = 7, so A = 49π.
  2. C = 6π (a length). A = 9π (an area). They are not the same kind of quantity.
  3. It approaches A = ½ × (2πr) × r = πr², the area of the circle.
  4. 25 × 3.14 = 78.5, using π ≈ 3.14, a labeled school approximation, not an official survey of a real circle.

Writing & Rhetoric · The other-side paragraph

Check. 1. State the other side fairly; concede what is genuinely right in it, if something is; answer it and return to the thesis. 2. A straw man restates the other side in a weaker or distorted form so that it is easy to dismiss. A fair other-side paragraph uses a form the other side could accept. 3. It concedes a real point and then states the judgment still defended. That sentence type was taught on Day 9; today it is used inside a full paragraph. 4. Model: other-side paragraph. Own words should match this idea: An other-side paragraph states a real objection or a real alternative reading, in a form that the other side could accept, and then answers it without sneering. It is part of the same essay, and it is not a withdrawal of the thesis. Near-miss: Not a straw man (already taught), and not a second body paragraph of the case itself. Not a claim that every essay must abandon its thesis. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete paragraph that states, concedes as needed, and replies. Wording will vary.
  2. Both sentences are marked. If the fair statement or the return is missing, that is the fault to repair.
  3. A short phrase heading, such as “caution rather than fear, answered,” not a new thesis in disguise.
  4. A specific concession, or a clear reason that no concession was due, without sneering.

Science · Water: density, and why ice floats

Check. 1. ρ = 12 / 15 = 0.80 g/cm³, less dense than ice at 0.9167 g/cm³ in this table. Invented sample. 2. Ice floats because it is less dense than liquid water (USGS); at 0 °C the NIST/Harvey figures used here are 0.9167 g/cm³ for ice Ih and 0.99984 g/cm³ for the liquid. 3. Different careful sources can give slightly different liquid-water figures, and a blended percentage would pretend they had been reduced to one official school number. 4. Model: density. Own words should match this idea: Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m / V. In this lesson the usual classroom units are grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³), which are the same size as grams per milliliter. Near-miss: Not mass alone, and not volume alone. A large piece and a small piece of the same uniform material have the same density. why ice floats. Own words should match this idea: Ice floats on liquid water because the ice is less dense than the liquid. At 0 °C and standard atmospheric pressure, ice Ih has a density of 0.9167 g/cm³, and liquid water has a density of 0.99984 g/cm³, in the NIST table by Allan H. Harvey used here. Near-miss: Not a claim that every solid floats on its own liquid. Most solids are denser than their melts; ice is an unusual case. Not a blended official percentage: this lesson keeps the two sourced densities rather than inventing one official “percent less.” The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ρ = 7.2 / 8 = 0.90 g/cm³, close to but not identical with 0.9167 g/cm³. You cannot conclude that the cube is a measured sample of ice from this course’s table; the figures are invented and only comparable in size.
  2. They are the same, if the material is uniform: density does not depend on the size of the piece.
  3. The 0.9167 and 0.99984 g/cm³ figures, which come from the NIST/Harvey table, not from the USGS page.
  4. Ice is a solid that floats on its own liquid. The usual pattern (solid denser than liquid) is not a law without exceptions, and this is the exception taught today.

Literature · Inferno II: the saintly Lady, named Beatrice (II.52–74)

Check. 1. “Among those was I who are in suspense.” 2. “A friend of mine, and not the friend of fortune.” He is impeded on the desert slope and “has turned through terror.” 3. “Beatrice am I, who do bid thee go.” “Love moved me, which compelleth me to speak.” 4. Model: the saintly Lady, Beatrice. Own words should match this idea: In Longfellow, Inferno II.52–74, a “fair, saintly Lady” calls to Virgil “among those… who are in suspense,” and later names herself: “Beatrice am I, who do bid thee go.” She asks him to help “a friend of mine, and not the friend of fortune,” impeded on the desert slope. Near-miss: Not a first look at Virgil, or at the will that unwills, from earlier in Canto II. Not a later commentary’s biography of Beatrice brought in as if it were in these lines. This lesson uses the name as Longfellow’s wording uses it. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The friend belongs to her, not to fortune: the help is claimed as hers, not as luck’s.
  2. “Full often will I praise thee unto him.”
  3. These lines name her Beatrice and give her speech; they do not narrate that later biography. This course answers from the set wording.
  4. Lucìa, Rachel, the three Ladies, the recovered courage, and the entry on the “deep and savage way” (II.75–142).

Atelier · Keeping the families while the lay-in dries or sits

Check. 1. Do the families still stand as two large shapes? Has a mid-tone crossed the terminator? Is the terminator still a turning? Have features been started too soon? 2. The eye, or any other feature, will sit on a pattern of light that is still wrong, and later work will have to undo it. The families come first. 3. Stop. A clear lay-in is left to stand; more finish is tomorrow’s or a later sitting’s job, not an automatic next stroke. 4. Model: family check (grisaille). Own words should match this idea: A family check, at this stage, is a short, fixed look at the monochrome lay-in: do the light family and the shadow family still stand as two large shapes? Has a mid-tone drifted from one family into the other? Has the terminator become a line? Near-miss: Not a first look at a critique, and not a two-week plan. Not a finish of the features. Not a first look at the families themselves. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Four written answers, specific to the work in front of you.
  2. A named repair, or an honest stop.
  3. A specific temptation (an eye, a mouth, a highlight) and a reason.
  4. A distance judgment: the families still read, or they do not, with a location if they do not.

Model: puse. Own words should match this idea: Puse is the first-person singular preterite of poner, to put or to place. The stem is pus-: puse, pusiste, puso, pusimos, pusisteis, pusieron. Near-miss: Not a first look at pude, and not last week’s uv-stems or fui and hice. Not the present pongo, already a different irregular. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete invented sentence, such as Ellos pusieron los libros en la mesa. — They put the books on the table.
  2. Pude; puse.
  3. Pudiste; pusiste.
  4. They are third-person singular forms of different verbs, with different stems (pud- / pus-) and different meanings.

Elective · Music: a period, antecedent and consequent

Check. 1. A pair of phrases: an antecedent and a consequent that belong together, usually weaker close then stronger close. 2. The antecedent often ends in a half cadence, on V. The consequent often ends in an authentic cadence, on I. 3. That T is the time for one cycle of an oscillation. Today’s period is a phrase structure in music. 4. Model: musical period. Own words should match this idea: A musical period, in the common-practice school sense taught here, is a pair of phrases that belong together: an antecedent that usually ends with a weaker close, often a half cadence, and a consequent that usually answers with a stronger close, often an authentic cadence, on the tonic. Near-miss: Not a first look at a half cadence (Day 52), an authentic cadence (week 15), a deceptive cadence (last week), or a I–IV–V–I progression (Day 67). Not a first look at a cadence as such. antecedent and consequent (music). Own words should match this idea: The antecedent is the first phrase of a period, often ending on V (a half cadence). The consequent is the answering phrase, often ending on I (an authentic cadence). The two are heard as a question and an answer, not as two unrelated starts. Near-miss: Not the logical terms antecedent and consequent already used in conditionals (Day 21 and Day 41). The names are the same; the job here is musical. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A labeled invented skeleton, with a weaker close then a stronger close, or an honest note of a skeleton sung or spoken.
  2. V–vi lands on the relative tonic instead of I, so the answer does not close in the usual way this lesson described.
  3. In logic, the antecedent is the “if” clause of a conditional. In music, the antecedent is the first phrase of a period. Same name, different job.
  4. The half cadence was taught on Day 52. Today it is used as a typical antecedent close, not named for the first time.
