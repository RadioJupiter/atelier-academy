Latin · Sequor and ēgredior: present subjunctive against the future

Check. 1. She will follow him, future; he goes out, present subjunctive. 2. Sequar is the spelling of both the present subjunctive and the future in the first-person singular. The other persons split. 3. Ēgrediātur is the present subjunctive. The future, she will go out, is ēgrediētur.

Exercises. 1. Fair original sentences may use Puer or Puella, with he or she in the gloss, and must keep ā for the present subjunctive and ē for the future. 2. Sequātur, he follows, or she follows; ēgrediātur, he goes out, or she goes out, both present subjunctive. 3. A purpose clause with ut remains reserved.

Mathematics · Geometry XCIII: sector area from the arc, A = (1/2)r s

Check. 1. A = (1/2)r s. From s = rθ, θ = s/r, so A = (1/2)r²(s/r) = (1/2)r s. 2. The area is 20 square units, and θ = 2/5. The figure is invented. 3. A circular segment waits until tomorrow. Today is still the sector, now found from the arc. 4. Model: sector area from the arc, A = (1/2)r s. Own words should match this idea: Because the already-taught arc length is s = rθ with θ in radians, the sector area A = (1/2)r²θ can be rewritten A = (1/2)r s. If r and the arc s are known, the area can be found without first naming θ. Near-miss: Not A = (1/2)r²s, which treats the arc as if it were an angle. Not s = rθ used as if it were already the area. Not a degree formula applied to s. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. A = (1/2) × 8 × 3 = 12 square units, and θ = 3/8. The figure is invented. 2. s = rθ = 6 × (π/3) = 2π, and A = (1/2) × 6 × 2π = 6π, the same area as Monday. The figure is invented. 3. That formula multiplies by r² and by the arc, as if s were an angle. The arc form is A = (1/2)r s, not (1/2)r²s.

Writing · A commonplace carried through, still on the invented type

Check. 1. It should return to the type of fault and finish the amplification. It must not name a living person, and it must not open a new untried claim. 2. A fair close: a fable remains a fable even when it is well told; a historical record, or a confirmed claim, is held by a different test from repetition or plot. 3. A new untried claim would need a confirmation or a refutation, which is not today’s job.

Exercises. 1. A fair piece stays on the invented type, uses already-taught reasons, contrasts with the isosceles claim, and closes without a new claim or a living person. 2. A confirmation shows why a set claim holds. A commonplace amplifies a general fault already acknowledged. 3. A longer later elaboration, and an encomium, remain reserved.

Physics · The law of reflection, a first look, from OpenStax 25.2

Check. 1. “The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence—θᵣ = θᵢ.” The angles are measured from the perpendicular to the surface at the point where the ray strikes, the normal. 2. θᵣ = 30°. The 30° is an invented classroom figure, labeled invented; the law itself is the book’s official statement. 3. The law of refraction is named in 25.1 as the other law and remains reserved. 4. Model: law of reflection. Own words should match this idea: In OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 25.2, “The law of reflection states that the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence—θᵣ = θᵢ.” The angles “are measured relative to the perpendicular to the surface at the point where the ray strikes the surface.” That perpendicular is the normal. Near-miss: Not the law of refraction, which remains reserved. Not a first look at a focal length, a curved mirror, or an official instrument length. Not a claim that a rough surface has no reflection: the book says a rough surface reflects in many directions, or is diffused. angle of incidence. Own words should match this idea: The angle of incidence, θᵢ, is the angle between the incoming ray and the perpendicular (the normal) to the surface at the point where the ray strikes. It is not the angle between the ray and the surface itself. Wiktionary gives the word incidence from Latin incidere, “to fall upon,” from in + cadere, “to fall.” Near-miss: Not the angle between the ray and the surface, which would be the complement of θᵢ on a flat surface, and is not the book’s angle. Not θᵣ, the angle of reflection, which is equal to θᵢ but is measured on the reflected side. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. θᵣ = 40°, measured from the same normal, not from the surface. The 40° figure is invented. 2. A sheet of paper has a rough surface and diffuses the light, reflecting it in many directions. A mirror has a smooth surface and “reflects light at specific angles.” 3. θᵢ is measured from the normal. The angle with the surface is a different angle.

History · The letter’s claim of friendship at Navidad

Check. 1. It claims “the greatest friendship with the king of that country,” and says “he took pride in calling me his brother, and treating me as such.” 2. It is taught as the letter’s claim, confirmed from the 1870 page, not as a later official judgment of the relationship. 3. Those later sentences are not used in this lesson. Today’s job is the friendship claim as the letter states it. 4. Model: the letter’s claim of friendship at Navidad. Own words should match this idea: In Major’s 1870 wording, immediately after the sailing master, the letter says: “I have also established the greatest friendship with the king of that country, so much so that he took pride in calling me his brother, and treating me as such.” That is the letter’s claim, confirmed from the 1870 page, not a later official judgment of the relationship. Near-miss: Not a later official confirmation or denial of that friendship, which is not taken from a modern source here. Not a first look at the later sentences about hostilities or arms, which are not used in this lesson. Not Thursday’s later close of the letter. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. “Calling me his brother, and treating me as such.” It is the letter’s claim, not a later official fact. 2. A fortress “quite completed,” a sufficient number of men with arms, artillery, and provisions for more than a year, a barge, and a sailing master. 3. The close of the letter, including its date, waits until Friday.

Spanish · Ha, hay, and haya, kept distinct

Check. 1. There is, or there are; that there be; he has, or she has, as a helping verb. Fair sentences: Hay un libro; Espero que haya tiempo; Ella ha hablado. 2. Hemos: we have spoken. Hay would mean there is, or there are, and does not take a regular participle as a helping verb in this lesson. 3. Dicho is an irregular participle and remains reserved.

Exercises. 1. Fair original sentences keep hay, haya, and ha vivido in their own jobs, with glosses. 2. A present-perfect subjunctive has not been taught. Do not invent haya comido as if it were already a taught form. If the job is the already-taught present perfect, Ella ha comido; if the job is “that there be,” haya, without a participle. 3. Irregular participles, and a present-perfect subjunctive, remain reserved.

Seminar · Art history: the Church of Acacius, from Dewing, Buildings I.4.25–26

Check. 1. It had “fallen into ruin,” and Justinian “took it down and rebuilt it from the foundations, so as to make it a building of marvellous size.” 2. It is “carried on all sides on columns of astonishing whiteness,” and the floor of similar stone gives “the impression that the whole church is coated with snow.” 3. One makes a court (peristylos); the other faces the market-place. Official lengths, widths, and column counts are not invented. 4. Model: the Church of Acacius (Dewing, I.4.25–26). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, Buildings I.4.25–26, Justinian found the Church of Acacius “fallen into ruin, and he took it down and rebuilt it from the foundations, so as to make it a building of marvellous size.” “It is carried on all sides on columns of astonishing whiteness, and the floor is covered with similar stone, from which such a brilliant light is reflected that it gives the impression that the whole church is coated with snow.” “And two stoas are thrown out in front of it, one of them making a court (peristylos), the other facing the market-place.” “Marvellous” stays inside the quotation. Official lengths are not invented. I.4.17–24 remains reserved, and I.4.27 onward remains reserved. Near-miss: Not last week’s Church of the Apostles, I.4.9–16, a different shrine already taught. Not I.4.17–24, which remains reserved. Not an official length, width, or column count, which is not invented. Later editorial plans are not treated as figures from Procopius. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The floor reflects “such a brilliant light” that the church seems “coated with snow.” “Marvellous” is Dewing’s British spelling and stays inside the quotation. 2. I.4.17–24 remains reserved. Today’s extract begins at I.4.25, after that reserved stretch. 3. I.4.27 onward, official lengths, and later editorial plans remain reserved or unused as official figures.
