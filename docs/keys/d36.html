Own words should match this idea: More nouns of the third family, still with genitive singular in -is and accusative singular in -em. Mathematics · Geometry XVII: the tangent-chord angle (Euclid III.32)

Check. 1. 35°. III.32 says the two angles are equal (or: both equal 90° − α for the same α). 2. Day 19: the radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent. Day 21: an inscribed angle is half the central angle on the same arc (III.20). 3. Model: alternate segment theorem (III.32). Own words should match this idea: The angle between a tangent and a chord, at the point of contact, equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment of the circle (the segment on the other side of the chord). This is Euclid, Elements, Book III, Proposition 32. Near-miss: Not ‘any angle next to a tangent equals any inscribed angle,’ and not a new definition of a tangent. A tangent still meets the circle at exactly one point, and the radius to that point is still perpendicular to the tangent (Day 19). tangent (origin recalled). Own words should match this idea: A line that meets a circle at exactly one point. The word is borrowed from Latin tangēns, ‘touching’ (in the phrase līnea tangēns, a touching line), the present participle of tangō, ‘touch.’ Near-miss: Not a chord (a chord meets the circle twice), and not a line that merely looks close to the circle on the page. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Tangent-chord angle = 90° − 25° = 65°. ∠AOB = 180° − 2×25° = 130°.
  2. ∠ADB is in the other segment (or stands on a different arc from the one the theorem names). III.32 compares the tangent-chord angle with the angle in the alternate segment.
  3. A labeled sketch with the two equal angles marked.
  4. To make the base angles of triangle OAB equal, so that ∠AOB = 180° − 2α.
  5. A fair set: the radius is perpendicular to the tangent, because that was proved on Day 19; but that fact alone does not name the inscribed angle, so the proof still needs III.20 and the triangle sum.

Physics · A first look at gravitation: an inverse-square force

Check. 1. F becomes 4F. 2. F doubles. 3. Model: Newton’s law of gravitation (first look). Own words should match this idea: Every two masses attract each other with a force along the line joining them. The size of that force is F = G Mm / r², where M and m are the two masses, r is the distance between their centers (in this first look, treated as particles or as spheres), and G is the universal gravitational constant. Near-miss: Not a classroom measurement of G, and not a new value of g as if it were G. g (already used as 9.8 N/kg near the Earth) is the gravitational field, or the acceleration, near the Earth; G is the constant in the general formula. This lesson does not invent an orbital speed. inverse-square (gravitation). Own words should match this idea: A pattern in which a quantity is proportional to 1/r²: if r is multiplied by 2, the quantity is multiplied by 1/4; if r is multiplied by 3, the quantity is multiplied by 1/9. Near-miss: Not “half as far, twice the force” (that would be a simple inverse, 1/r, not 1/r²), and not a claim about sound or light in this lesson. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. G is the constant in F = G Mm / r² (standard value 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²). g is 9.8 N/kg near the Earth, already used as a gravitational field or an acceleration.
  2. G is a standard constant; this class has not made a Cavendish-style measurement, and will not invent one.
  3. The 1/r² factor is 1/9; the mass factor is 2; so F becomes (2/9) of what it was.
  4. A fair set: the force is inverse-square, because r² is in the denominator; but G is not g, so you must not treat 9.8 N/kg as if it were G.
  5. No invented speed, and no claim that every orbit is a circle.

Logic · A first look at the undistributed middle

Check. 1. A three-line example with a named middle term, and content that is actually true in the premises if possible, with a false conclusion, or clearly flagged as a form-test. 2. All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P. M is distributed in “All M are P.” 3. Model: undistributed middle. Own words should match this idea: An invalid syllogistic form in which the middle term never refers to the whole of its class in either premise, so it does not actually link the other two terms. A stock example: All dogs are mammals; all cats are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs. The middle term “mammals” is not distributed in either premise. Near-miss: Not Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), in which the middle term is distributed in the first premise (“All M…”). And not a claim that every argument with a repeated word is invalid. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Affirming the consequent is a failure of if–then form (p → q; q; therefore p). The undistributed middle is a failure of a syllogism’s middle term. Related as invalid forms, not the same pattern.
  2. A repeated word is not enough; Barbara requires a middle term used so that it actually links S and P. This look-alike does not.
  3. Yes, a conclusion can be true by luck; the form would still be invalid (and the argument not sound by Day 6’s test, because validity has already failed).
  4. A fair set: the middle term must be distributed, because otherwise it may not link the two classes; but merely repeating the word is not enough, so the look-alike is invalid.
  5. A copied pair of forms.
