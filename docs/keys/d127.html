Latin · The first- and second-person present of ēgredior, a first look

Check. 1. Ēgredior, I go out or I am going out; ēgrederis, you (s.) go out; ēgreditur, he or she goes out; ēgredimur, we go out; ēgrediminī, you (pl.) go out; ēgrediuntur, they go out. 2. A fair pair: Ego ēgredior. Nōs ēgredimur. 3. Ēgredior means I go out; sequor means I follow. Both are present deponents. Locūtus eram is a pluperfect, I had spoken. 4. Model: first- and second-person present of ēgredior. Own words should match this idea: Ēgredior is a deponent, I go out, still looking passive and translated as active. The first- and second-person present forms, reserved until today, are ēgredior, I go out; ēgrederis, you (s.) go out; ēgredimur, we go out; and ēgrediminī, you (pl.) go out. The third persons are ēgreditur and ēgrediuntur. Near-miss: Not sequor, I follow, yesterday’s table. Not the pluperfect locūtus eram. Not a non-deponent such as exeō used as if it were today’s form. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. You (s.) go out. Puella ēgreditur: she goes out. We go out. 2. Sequor; ēgredior. 3. Ēgrederis; ēgredimur.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXII: sine and cosine at 180° and 270°, a first look

Check. 1. At 180°, P is (−1, 0), cos 180° = −1, sin 180° = 0. At 270°, P is (0, −1), cos 270° = 0, sin 270° = −1. 2. 0°: cos 1, sin 0. 90°: cos 0, sin 1. 180°: cos −1, sin 0. 270°: cos 0, sin −1. 3. Sin 270° = −1. Radians, and the 30°, 45°, and 60° points, wait until later this week or remain reserved as named. 4. Model: sine and cosine at the axis angles. Own words should match this idea: On the unit circle, the four axis angles in degrees have these points: 0°, (1, 0); 90°, (0, 1); 180°, (−1, 0); 270°, (0, −1). Then cos 180° = −1, sin 180° = 0, cos 270° = 0, and sin 270° = −1. The same circle of radius 1 is used; only the direction of the ray has changed. Near-miss: Not a first look at radians. Not a claim that sine is always positive. Not a new radius. The 30°, 45°, and 60° points wait until tomorrow. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The terminal ray at 180° points along the negative x-axis, so x is −1, not 1. The point (1, 0) is 0°. 2. Cos 270° = 0. −1 is the sine at 270°, or the cosine at 180°, not the cosine at 270°. 3. The same unit circle and the same rule are used; only the direction of the ray has changed.

Writing · A short original narrative

Check. 1. A fair answer is a short invented complete action, not the ink jar and not the crow, told in order, with a time and a place, and with no stated moral. 2. A stated moral at the end would make it a fable. Advice in the middle would make it a lecture. 3. So the action stays one complete happening, and the reader can follow what happened first and what happened next.

Exercises. 1. Time and place situate the action. Without them, the account is vaguer than a narrative needs to be. 2. The crow was last week’s fable. Today is a narrative, without a moral, and not a retelling of that set text. 3. Chreia and maxim remain reserved.

Science · Lapis lazuli, a rock whose blue is lazurite, a first look

Check. 1. A rock, not a single mineral, whose blue comes from lazurite. 2. Calcite and pyrite. The USGS HS418 formula (Na,Ca)7–8(Al,Si)12(O,S)24[(SO4),Cl2,(OH)2]. 3. An official percentage of a classroom stone, an official modern price, and an official mine output. Ultramarine remains reserved until Thursday. 4. Model: lapis lazuli. Own words should match this idea: Lapis lazuli is a rock, not a single mineral. In ordinary mineral descriptions used with a USGS sample record for lazurite, the blue color comes from the mineral lazurite; calcite and pyrite are commonly present with it. The USGS Spectral Library record for lazurite (HS418) gives the formula (Na,Ca)7–8(Al,Si)12(O,S)24[(SO4),Cl2,(OH)2]. Invent no official percentage of any mineral in a classroom stone, and no official modern price. Near-miss: Not hematite, last week’s red iron oxide. Not a single pure mineral with an official invented percentage. Not the pigment ultramarine, which is Thursday’s first look: the blue prepared from the rock, not the rock itself. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Hematite is a mineral, Fe2O3, used as a red ochre. Lapis lazuli is a rock that contains more than one mineral, with the blue from lazurite. 2. The elements may be grouped differently in different records. Today the set formula is the USGS sample record, not a second invented official formula. 3. It identifies the rock from which a blue pigment will be prepared, without yet teaching that preparation.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 2: the visit already paid, a first look

Check. 1. He had always intended to visit Bingley, while assuring his wife that he should not go. She has no knowledge of it until the evening after the visit is paid. 2. It begins with “I hope Mr. Bingley will like it Lizzy,” said while she is trimming a hat. The narrator has already said the visit was paid; Mrs. Bennet still speaks as if they are not to visit. 3. British or older 1813 forms, and the novel’s words for the household, stay inside quotations or clearly in the book. The rest of Chapter 2, including “To-morrow fortnight” and the open announcement that he has already called, waits until Thursday. 4. Model: the undisclosed visit (Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 2). Own words should match this idea: In the 1813 wording of Volume I, Chapter 2, “Mr. Bennet was among the earliest of those who waited on Mr. Bingley. He had always intended to visit him, though to the last always assuring his wife that he should not go; and till the evening after the visit was paid, she had no knowledge of it.” The chapter then discloses the visit in conversation, beginning when he addresses Lizzy, “I hope Mr. Bingley will like it Lizzy,” while she is “employed in trimming a hat.” The joke is that the visit has already been paid, against what he had led his wife to expect. Near-miss: Not an official biography of a living person, and not a claim that the visit is a historical event outside the novel. Not Chapter 1’s opening irony, already taught, used as if it were this new disclosure. British spellings and older forms inside the 1813 quotations stay inside the quotations. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The quotation is the 1813 wording of the narrator’s account of his intention and his assurance. 2. It is an event in the novel, not a documented official visit in a historical register. 3. Do not identify a character with a living person, or with a historical person the chapter does not name.

Atelier · A short scale in the mixed earth hue

Check. 1. The mixed earth hue, plus white and black only. Four or five even steps are enough. 2. A jump that bunches the values, or a change of the ochre-to-red proportion from step to step. 3. Comparing neighboring steps as values, or half-closing the eyes. A fifth tube, ultramarine as a palette color, and a portrait remain reserved.

Exercises. 1. A light mix with white, a less light step, the unmixed mixed-hue if it sits in the run, then two darker steps with black, even in value. 2. So the scale is one hue in several values, not a row of different hues. 3. The job is a scale of a mixed hue, not a likeness of a face.

Modern language · Spanish: irregular future of tener, poner, salir, and venir

Check. 1. Tendré, I will have; pondré, I will put or place; saldré, I will go out or leave; vendré, I will come. 2. A fair pair: Mañana tendremos tiempo, invented. Mañana vendré a las dos, invented time. 3. *Teneré would treat tener as regular in this tense. Other irregulars such as podré, sabré, and querré wait until tomorrow.

Exercises. 1. Tomorrow I will put it here, invented place. They will go out, or they will leave. We will have time, invented, not an official claim. 2. He or she will have; he or she will put or place; we will come. 3. Tendré; vendré.

Seminar · Music: ternary form, A–B–A, a first look

Check. 1. Ternary form is A–B–A: a first strain, a contrasting middle, and a return of the first. Binary form is A then B, without a required return of A. 2. Four bars of A, 1–3–5–3; four of B, 2–4–6–4; the same four of A again. The figure is invented and written only in scale degrees, so no copyrighted tune is being copied. 3. Ending after A–B, or replacing the return with a new strain C. A longer form, or a varied return of A, remains reserved. 4. Model: ternary form. Own words should match this idea: Ternary form is a three-part musical shape, A–B–A: a first strain, a contrasting middle strain, and a return of the first strain. The return is the same A, or a close written-out repeat of it, not a third new strain. The idea is taught on a short invented figure in scale degrees, not on a copyrighted score. Near-miss: Not last week’s binary form, which is A then B, without a required return of A. Not a verse-and-chorus name from a copyrighted song. Not a claim that every three-part piece is ternary, and not a copyrighted score quoted as a classroom example. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The third part is a coming-back of the first strain, not a new idea that would make the shape A–B–C. 2. A period already taught may sit inside A or inside B, but today’s first look does not require that. 3. The lesson teaches the shape on an invented figure. A copyrighted melody is not the example, and is not to be copied into the exercise.
