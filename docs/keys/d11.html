Latin · Capitulum V, part 1: the ablative, and in, ex, ab

Check. 1. Ablative singular. The phrase means ‘in the garden’. 2. Puer ex hortō venit. 3. Model: ablative. Own words should match this idea: The case used in this chapter after the prepositions in (when it means ‘in’ or ‘on’), ex (‘out of’), and ab (‘away from’), and later also after cum and sine. In the first and second declensions its singular ends in a long vowel (−ō or −ā) and its plural ends in −īs. Near-miss: The ablative is not the accusative: in hortum would be a different pattern (motion into), which this chapter does not yet use with in. Do not drop the macron in writing: rosā (ablative) is not rosa (nominative). preposition (with the ablative). Own words should match this idea: A small word placed before a noun to show a relation such as place or accompaniment. In Capitulum V the prepositions in, ex, ab, cum and sine are used with the ablative. Near-miss: The case is chosen by the preposition, not by a guess from English ‘in’ or ‘from’. Today only in, ex and ab are practiced; cum and sine follow tomorrow. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. hortō, vīllā, cubiculō.
  2. hortīs, vīllīs, cubiculīs.
  3. Mārcus est in ātriō.
  4. The boys are in the gardens. hortīs is ablative plural.
  5. Clear, even reading, with the new endings heard.

Mathematics · Geometry V: constructions with compass and straightedge

Check. 1. With opening greater than half of PQ, swing arcs from P and from Q; they meet at two points. The line through those points is the perpendicular bisector. Every point on it is equidistant from P and Q because that line is the locus of such points (Day 8), and the construction places its two points on that locus. 2. Swing an arc centered at A cutting both rays, at X and Y. With the same opening, swing an arc centered at A′ cutting the given ray at X′. Open from X to Y and mark the same length from X′ on the new arc, at Y′. Draw A′Y′. Then △A′X′Y′ ≅ △AXY by SSS, so the angles at A and A′ are equal. 3. Model: construction. Own words should match this idea: A drawing made with only an unmarked straightedge and a compass, justified by axioms and earlier theorems, not by measuring with a ruler. Near-miss: A construction is not a freehand sketch, and it is not a measurement taken off a marked ruler. The compass opening is a transferred length, not a number of centimeters. perpendicular bisector. Own words should match this idea: The line that cuts a segment in half and meets it at a right angle. It is the locus of points equidistant from the two endpoints. Near-miss: It is not merely ‘a line through the midpoint’ (that line might not be perpendicular), and it is not a perpendicular that misses the midpoint. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Copy the first segment onto a ray; from the new endpoint, copy the second segment onward. The total segment is the sum.
  2. Construct the perpendicular bisector; its intersection with the segment is the midpoint.
  3. From the point, mark two points on the line, equally far from it, one on each side. The perpendicular bisector of that new segment is the required perpendicular.
  4. Any such P lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB. (It need not be equidistant from C unless you add a second locus.)
  5. Swing an arc of opening AB from A and from B; they meet at a third point C on one side of AB. △ABC is equilateral (three sides equal by construction).

Writing & Rhetoric · Sentence combining and transitions

Check. 1. For example: The boys walk to the town, which is near Rome. Or: The boys walk to the town, a place near Rome. 2. The locative looks like a genitive; however, it does not show possession here. (A but combination is also acceptable.) 3. Model: sentence combining. Own words should match this idea: The skill of joining two or more short, complete sentences into one sentence that keeps all the meaning and is easier to follow, using a conjunction, a relative clause, an appositive, or a joining punctuation mark. Near-miss: Combining is not gluing sentences with a comma alone (a comma splice), and it is not leaving out a fact in order to make the sentence short. transition. Own words should match this idea: A word or short phrase that shows the relation between two sentences or two parts of a sentence: addition, contrast, cause, result, or sequence (for example, however, therefore, next, for instance). Near-miss: A transition is not a decoration added at the start of every sentence, and ‘however’ is not a synonym of ‘and’. The relation has to be real. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: The treaty survives in two languages: a cuneiform copy from Hattusa, and a hieroglyphic copy on a wall at Karnak.
  2. The tangent meets the circle once, and the radius is perpendicular at that point. Or use a semicolon, or a so.
  3. The therefore version should be two sentences; the because version should be one. The cause and result must be the same fact.
  4. Examples: however — contrast; therefore — result; for example — addition/illustration; next — sequence.
  5. The revision should be tighter and still complete.

Physics · Friction and the normal force

Check. 1. N = 39.2 N, fk = 9.8 N, Fnet = 20 − 9.8 = 10.2 N, a = 10.2/4 = 2.55 m/s². 2. fs,max = 0.3 × 49 = 14.7 N. 3. Model: normal force. Own words should match this idea: The force a surface exerts on an object, perpendicular to the surface. On a level surface, if the object is not accelerating up or down, the normal force balances the object’s weight. Near-miss: ‘Normal’ here does not mean ‘usual’. It means perpendicular (the older English sense). The normal force is not always equal to the weight: on a slope, or if you push the object down, it changes. friction. Own words should match this idea: A force between surfaces in contact that resists their relative sliding. Kinetic friction acts while they slide, often written fk = μk N. Static friction acts to prevent sliding, up to a maximum fs,max = μs N, and can be less than that maximum if a smaller force already keeps the object still. Near-miss: Friction is not ‘what stops everything by itself’, and μ is not a force. μ is a measured ratio, different for static and kinetic cases, and it is not a property of one surface alone. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. N becomes mg + 10 N, so it increases, and fs,max = μs N also increases.
  2. 0.2 is a coefficient (a ratio), not a force, and you must say whether it is static or kinetic, and you need N before you have a force in newtons.
  3. Constant velocity means a = 0, so fk = 6 N. N = 19.6 N, so μk = 6/19.6 ≈ 0.31.
  4. The horizontal net force is F − fk = ma.
  5. C. Equal forces on a sliding block mean zero acceleration; it can still be moving (Newton’s first law, Day 6).

History · Egypt: the Old Kingdom and the pyramids

Check. 1. It is a royal tomb (in a funerary complex). This course does not prefer the unsupported ‘granary’ or ‘riddle with no evidence’ stories. 2. The mastaba, and/or the stepped pyramid (Djoser at Saqqara). 3. Model: Old Kingdom. Own words should match this idea: The period of Egyptian history in which the great pyramids of Giza were built, conventionally the Third to Sixth Dynasties. Exact years in BCE depend on which chronology is used; this course will not treat a single year-number as certain. Near-miss: The Old Kingdom is not ‘all of ancient Egypt’, and it is not a name Egyptians used for themselves in this form. Later periods (Middle Kingdom, New Kingdom) are different, and must not be collapsed into one ‘pyramid age’. pyramid (Egyptian). Own words should match this idea: A royal tomb of this period, with a square base and four sloping faces, built in stone, of which the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza is the largest. It is a monument of kingship and of the afterlife, not a granary and not a temple of the later Greek or Roman type. Near-miss: A pyramid is not a mystery object without a purpose in the evidence we have: it is a royal tomb, surrounded by a funerary complex. Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, is a later traveler, not a 4th-Dynasty official. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A list of three claims with an honest test for each; labor-numbers are hard to check from the monument alone.
  2. A labeled sketch with the three names, plus the note.
  3. For example: primary for what a 5th-century Greek traveler was told about Egypt; secondary for the 4th Dynasty itself.
  4. Because published chronologies differ by decades, and the course already treats such dates as dependent on the chronology used.
  5. The Pyramid Texts (beginning with the pyramid of Unas).

Atelier · The cube: planes and foreshortening

Check. 1. Foreshortening is the apparent shortening of a receding length or shape. On a cube below eye level it appears in the top plane and in any side that turns away: those faces are not drawn as true squares. 2. Hold the pencil at arm’s length from the fixed point and compare the angle or the relative length of the receding edge to a vertical (plumb) or to a known front edge, then take that comparison to the paper. 3. Model: plane (of a form). Own words should match this idea: A flat face of a form, or a flattened patch used to understand a curve. A cube has six square planes; a drawing of the cube starts by finding those planes and the angles between them, not by rounding the corners early. Near-miss: A plane is not a scribble of tone, and it is not ‘shading for its own sake’. If the planes are wrong, later modeling will not save the cube. foreshortening. Own words should match this idea: The apparent shortening of a length or a shape that recedes from you. A square plane of the cube that turns away no longer looks square on the page; a side that comes toward you looks shorter than you know it is. Near-miss: Foreshortening is not a trick of style, and it is not an excuse to guess. It is the same proportion skill as Day 2, applied to a length that points toward or away from you. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A constructed cube with clear planes, a fixed viewpoint, and a cast shadow. No invented rounding, no wandering light.
  2. The planes and the main value-changes are marked honestly.
  3. Three sentences that name a real measurement, a real foreshortened shape, and a real correction.
  4. Either a second study with a controlled change of height, or a short, correct reason for not moving.
  5. Same: one light, light family and shadow family, a cast shadow. Different: the cube turns at planes and edges; the egg turns continuously.

Modern language · Spanish: stem-changing verbs, e → ie

Check. 1. pienso, piensas, piensa, pensamos, pensáis, piensan. 2. Ellos/Ellas empiezan la lección el lunes. (Empiezan, not ‘empezan’, in the they-form.) 3. Model: stem-changing verb (e → ie). Own words should match this idea: A verb whose stem vowel e becomes ie in the present, in every form except nosotros and vosotros. The infinitive still shows e. Near-miss: A stem change is not a new tense, and it is not ‘irregular in every form’. Nosotros and vosotros keep the e: pensamos, no ‘piensamos’. diphthong (ie). Own words should match this idea: Two vowel sounds in one syllable. In these verbs, the e of the stem becomes the diphthong ie when that syllable is stressed. Near-miss: A diphthong is not a separate word, and it is not an accent mark. The written accent, when it appears (empiezo has none extra; prédica would be a different word), is a spelling fact, not the cause of the stem change. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. quiero; queremos.
  2. A real question such as ¿Prefieres estudiar o dibujar? and a full-sentence answer with prefiero or preferimos.
  3. It must be pensamos: nosotros does not take the ie change.
  4. ¿Quieres hablar español?
  5. Three grammatical sentences, with ie only where the stem is stressed.

Logic · De Morgan’s laws

Check. 1. No. A distinguishing row is P true, Q false: ¬P ∧ Q is false (F ∧ F), but ¬(P ∧ Q) is true (¬F). The two columns do not match, so they are not equivalent. 2. ¬(P ∨ Q), which is equivalent to ¬P ∧ ¬Q: the line is not parallel, and the angle is not right. 3. Model: De Morgan’s laws. Own words should match this idea: Two equivalences that move a negation through ‘and’ and ‘or’, and switch the connective: not (P and Q) is equivalent to (not P) or (not Q); not (P or Q) is equivalent to (not P) and (not Q). Near-miss: De Morgan does not say that ‘not P and Q’ is the same as ‘not (P and Q)’. Parentheses matter: ¬P ∧ Q is a different claim from ¬(P ∧ Q). logical equivalence. Own words should match this idea: Two statements that have the same truth value in every possible case. We write ⇔ or ‘is equivalent to’ only after the tables (or a proof) show that match. Near-miss: Equivalence is not ‘sounds similar in English’, and it is not the same as ‘one implies the other’ in a single direction (Day 6’s validity can hold one way without equivalence). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. P ∨ Q is F only when both are F, so ¬(P ∨ Q) is T only in the last row (F F). ¬P ∧ ¬Q is T only in that same row. The columns match.
  2. ‘Not both P and Q’ is ¬(P ∧ Q). ‘Neither P nor Q’ is ¬P ∧ ¬Q, which is ¬(P ∨ Q), not the first law.
  3. Both say: it is not at once isosceles and equilateral — i.e. at least one of those fails. (Whether that can happen in geometry is a separate question; the equivalence is logical, not a geometry theorem.)
  4. ¬P ∨ Q versus ¬(P ∨ Q) — or ¬P ∨ Q versus (¬P) ∨ Q, which is the same thing. A fair pair is ¬(P ∨ Q) versus (¬P) ∨ Q, which are not equivalent. Or ¬P ∨ Q versus ¬(P ∨ Q). The student should show one row where the two chosen readings differ.
  5. A real pair, with symbols that actually match a De Morgan law.
