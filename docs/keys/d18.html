Latin · Capitulum VI, part 4: on the road

Check. 1. Puerī Rōmam eunt. 2. The subject is singular, so the verb must be it, not eunt: Mārcus ad vīllam it. 3. Model: via. Own words should match this idea: The Latin word for a road or way (also, by extension, a journey). It is a first-declension noun: via, viam, viā, viae, viīs. Near-miss: via is not only the English word via meaning ‘by way of’ (though English borrowed it). In this lesson it is a noun you decline, not a preposition. īre. Own words should match this idea: The Latin verb meaning ‘to go’. In this chapter you use the third-person forms it (he/she/it goes) and eunt (they go). Near-miss: it is not English it, and eunt is not ‘aunt’. īre is a different verb from venīre (‘to come’): it is ‘go’, venit is ‘comes’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. via, viam, viā, viae.
  2. Each sentence should show the right case after the preposition.
  3. Clear reading, with the map in use.
  4. For example, Mārcus Rōmam it. Mārcus ex hortō venit.
  5. Posteā Mārcus Rōmam it.

Mathematics · Geometry IX: the circle, chords

Check. 1. Half the chord is 9, so the distance is √(15² − 9²) = √(225 − 81) = √144 = 12. 2. Half is 7, so the distance is √(25² − 7²) = √(625 − 49) = √576 = 24. 3. Model: chord. Own words should match this idea: A segment joining two points of a circle. A diameter is a chord through the center; it is the longest chord. Near-miss: A chord is not an arc (the curved part of the circumference between the same two points), and it is not a tangent (a tangent meets the circle at exactly one point). distance from the center. Own words should match this idea: The perpendicular distance from the center of a circle to a chord. A radius to an endpoint, that perpendicular, and a half-chord form a right triangle. Near-miss: This distance is not the length of the chord, and it is not a radius unless the chord is a single point (a degenerate case you will not need). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Radius = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10.
  2. The radius is √(12² + 5²) = √(144 + 25) = √169 = 13. Then the longer half-chord is 12, and the distance is √(13² − 12²) = √(169 − 144) = √25 = 5.
  3. Radius 10, half-chord 8, distance √(100 − 64) = √36 = 6.
  4. Let M be the foot on chord AB. Then OA = OB (radii), OM is common, and the right angles at M (if we use the converse later — better: triangles OMA and OMB have OA = OB, OM common, and AM, BM not yet). Standard proof: OA = OB, OM common, AM and BM are what we want. Use two right triangles only after we know they are right, or: the two triangles are congruent by SSS? We don't have AM = BM yet. Correct proof: OA = OB, so the triangle is isosceles; the perpendicular from the apex to the base of an isosceles triangle meets the base at its midpoint (or: the line from the center to the midpoint is perpendicular, and uniqueness of the perpendicular from a point to a line). Write: drop a perpendicular from O to AB at M. Then △OMA and △OMB are right-angled at M, OA = OB, OM common, so they are congruent by Hypotenuse–Leg, hence AM = BM.
  5. Equal chords have equal half-chords; the right triangles with the same hypotenuse (the radius) then have equal second legs, so the distances are equal. The converse is the same argument reversed.

Writing & Rhetoric · Quotations and attribution

Check. 1. A passing answer names Johnson and Rasselas, quotes a short phrase, and explains how the main verb is delayed, in the student’s own words. 2. Name Lincoln, and the Gettysburg Address (or the Declaration, if the phrase is being traced there), in the introducing sentence. ‘Someone once said’ is not an attribution. 3. Model: quotation. Own words should match this idea: Someone else’s exact words, placed in quotation marks (or indented as a block, for a longer extract), introduced by a sentence of your own, and followed by an explanation in your own words. Near-miss: A quotation is not a substitute for a topic sentence, and a sentence that is only a quotation is not a paragraph. Do not let a quoted clause do the arguing for you. attribution. Own words should match this idea: The naming of who said or wrote the words, and in what work or on what occasion, so that a reader can find the source. In this course a short prose quotation is attributed in the sentence that introduces it. Near-miss: Attribution is not a bibliography entry alone, and it is not ‘someone once said’. If the writer or the work is missing, the quotation is unearned. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Three complete, attributed, explained quotations, each short enough to use.
  2. The shortened quotation is no longer than the explanation can carry.
  3. Every quotation has marks, a name, and a work.
  4. Summarize when the exact wording is not itself the evidence; quote when the wording is the point, or when a short official phrase must be exact.
  5. The missing source is added.

Physics · Momentum

Check. 1. Total p = 20 kg·m/s, total mass 6 kg, v = 10/3 m/s (about 3.3 m/s) in the original direction. 2. Δp = 0 − 6 = −6 kg·m/s. F = Δp/Δt = −6/0.3 = −20 N (20 N opposite the original velocity). 3. Model: momentum. Own words should match this idea: The product of mass and velocity, p = m v, a vector in the same direction as the velocity. The SI unit is kg·m/s. Near-miss: Momentum is not the same as inertia (a property of mass alone), and it is not the same as kinetic energy (which uses v² and is not a vector). A heavy body at rest has zero momentum. conservation of momentum. Own words should match this idea: If two (or more) bodies interact and the net external force on the system is zero (or is negligible for a short collision), the total momentum of the system does not change. Internal action–reaction pairs (Day 10) cancel in the total. Near-miss: Conservation of momentum is not ‘every body keeps its own momentum’, and it is not a claim that energy is always conserved in a collision (a sticking collision loses kinetic energy). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Total p = 3×2 + 3×(−2) = 0, so v = 0.
  2. The total p is a vector sum that the internal pair cannot change. Kinetic energy is a scalar that can leave the motion (heat, deformation) without being a vector that the pair has to conserve.
  3. C. Newton’s third law: the two forces are equal and opposite, even if the masses differ.
  4. p = 2×3 + 1×6 = 12, total mass 3 kg, v = 4 m/s.
  5. Friction from the track, a hand still pushing, or a bump at the end of the track, if any of these is not negligible during the collision.

History · The Hellenistic world and Ptolemaic Egypt

Check. 1. 323 BCE; 30 BCE; Alexandria. 2. A period of successor kingdoms using Greek as a language of rule, in a mixed world; not ‘Egypt became simply Greek’, and not a synonym of classical Athens. 3. Model: Hellenistic. Own words should match this idea: The period, after Alexander’s death in 323 BCE, in which his empire was divided among successor kingdoms (the Diadochi and their heirs) whose courts used Greek as a language of rule, while older cultures continued. It lasts, in a common textbook frame, down to the Roman take-over of those kingdoms (in Egypt, 30 BCE). Near-miss: Hellenistic is not ‘Greek instead of Egyptian’. The word names a mixed and often bilingual world. It is also not ‘Hellenic’ as a synonym of classical Athens (Week 2). Ptolemaic Egypt. Own words should match this idea: The Hellenistic kingdom in Egypt ruled by the dynasty founded by Ptolemy, a companion of Alexander (the same Ptolemy Arrian later preferred as a source), from the late 4th century BCE until 30 BCE. The kings ruled from Alexandria and used both Greek and Egyptian forms of kingship. Near-miss: Ptolemaic Egypt is not the Old Kingdom, and the Ptolemies are not native 4th-Dynasty kings. Calling them ‘pharaohs’ needs a note: they took on Egyptian royal titles as well as Greek ones. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The six-point sequence in order, with the treaty and the pyramids in the right periods.
  2. More than one audience: Greek court and Egyptian temples or cities, for example.
  3. No: it is a Ptolemaic text of 196 BCE, about a later decree, not a narrative of 334–323.
  4. A sentence that names Ptolemy once as Arrian’s source and once as founder of the dynasty.
  5. A precise limit, not a fake concession.

Atelier · Lost-and-found edges

Check. 1. A lost edge is a boundary that almost disappears because values nearly meet or the form turns slowly. A smear is an uncontrolled blur that does not follow that look and may hide a still-sharp form. 2. A white halo is a leftover of the paper around the object, an even, found edge of the wrong value. Bring the background (or the neighboring shadow) up so the values can meet and the edge can go. 3. Model: lost-and-found edge. Own words should match this idea: A contour or an inner edge that is sharp in one passage (found) and soft or gone in another (lost), according to the light and the turn of the form. The drawing’s outline is not a wire of even thickness all the way around. Near-miss: A lost edge is not a smudge of dirt, and a found edge is not a cartoon outline. Both must be seen: you lose an edge where the values of two neighboring passages nearly meet, or where the form turns slowly from you. edge quality. Own words should match this idea: How sudden or gradual a change of value is at a boundary: razor-sharp, firm, soft, or lost. Edge quality is decided after the large values are in, or along with them, not as a decorative outline drawn first and never revised. Near-miss: Edge quality is not ‘blending everything’, and it is not ‘outlining everything’. It is a choice that follows the look of the set-up. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A continued study with deliberate, varied edges and no decorative wire. The form still reads.
  2. Four edges marked with reasons that refer to the set-up, not to a formula.
  3. Three sentences that report real decisions, including a halo or its absence.
  4. A small cube study that actually looks for both a firm and a possibly lost edge.
  5. An honest stand-back judgment, with a specific edge to find again if needed.

Modern language · Spanish: irregular yo forms

Check. 1. pongo, pones, pone, ponemos, ponéis, ponen; veo, ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven. 2. Salgo a las cinco; traigo el libro; hago el trabajo. (A las cinco is acceptable for ‘at five’.) 3. Model: irregular yo form. Own words should match this idea: A present-tense first-person singular that does not follow the ordinary stem + -o pattern, while the other persons are often regular (or follow a pattern already taught). Today’s set: hago, pongo, salgo, traigo, vengo, digo, oigo, conozco, sé, veo, doy. You already know soy, estoy, voy, and tengo. Near-miss: An irregular yo is not a new tense, and it does not make every form irregular. Do not write *haco or *sabo. saber and conocer. Own words should match this idea: Two verbs that both translate as ‘to know’. Saber is to know a fact, or to know how to do something (saber + infinitive). Conocer is to be acquainted with a person, a place, or a work. Their yo forms are sé and conozco. Near-miss: Do not swap them: *Conozco la respuesta is not the usual way to say you know a fact, and *Sé a Ana is wrong. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. sé; Conoces.
  2. Yo sé la lección. / Sé la respuesta.
  3. The six forms copied correctly; a sentence such as Digo la verdad.
  4. Vengo, doy, y soy estudiante.
  5. Four grammatical sentences that actually use three irregular yo forms and conocer a.

Art history · The kouros and contrapposto

Check. 1. A conventional upturned mouth on many Archaic faces. It is not safely read as a candid feeling (‘this person is happy’). 2. Claim only what remains, and mark reconstructions and missing color or attributes as such. Do not invent the lost part as a fact. 3. Model: kouros. Own words should match this idea: An Archaic Greek standing youth, typically in marble, usually nude, with the left foot forward, the weight still even on both legs, the arms at the sides, and often an ‘Archaic smile’. The plural is kouroi. The word is Ionic Greek for ‘boy’ or ‘youth’. Near-miss: A kouros is not a Classical athlete in contrapposto, and it is not a Cycladic folded-arm figure. The even stance and the smile are part of the Archaic convention, not a candid photograph of a mood. contrapposto. Own words should match this idea: A standing pose in which the weight rests mainly on one leg, the other is relaxed, and the hips and shoulders tilt in opposite directions, so the spine forms a slight S-curve. The Early Classical marble youth known as the Kritios Boy (Acropolis, Athens, early fifth century BCE) is a standard example. Near-miss: Contrapposto is not ‘standing on one foot as a trick’, and it is not the same thing as a walking figure with both legs stiff. It is a counterpoise of the whole body. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A paragraph that describes and that names a limit.
  2. A definition matching the counter-tilt of weight, plus a specific pointing at hip and shoulder.
  3. Likenesses may include frontality and a shared foot-forward idea; differences may include nudity, the absence of composite view, and the treatment of the body in the round. The paragraph should be specific, not a slogan.
  4. The pose is in the marble, which you can describe whether or not the historical sculptor’s name is certain.
  5. A real although-sentence: although both are standing marble youths, the balance (even weight versus contrapposto) and the face (smile versus its absence) belong to different conventions.
