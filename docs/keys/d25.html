Latin · Capitulum VII: hic, haec, hoc, and a Friday check

Check. 1. Hic puer Iūliae rosam dat. 2. The first sentence uses in hortō (ablative: place where). The second uses in hortum (accusative: motion into). sē is used because Marcus sees himself, not someone else. 3. Model: hic, haec, hoc. Own words should match this idea: The near demonstrative: ‘this’ (near the speaker), introduced in Capitulum VII. You will meet the full set of forms over this week and next; today you learn a first working set: hic (masc. nom.), haec (fem. nom.), hoc (neuter nom./acc.), and the accusatives hunc, hanc. Near-miss: hic the pronoun is not the adverb hīc (‘here’), which has a long ī. And ‘this’ is not yet ‘that’ (ille), which arrives in Capitulum VIII. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. hic, haec, hoc.
  2. Mārcus sē in aquā videt.
  3. in vīllam / in vīllā.
  4. Iūlia Mārcō rosam dat.
  5. The pronoun means ‘this’ and changes with gender and case. The adverb hīc means ‘here’ and has a long ī; it is not declined in this lesson.

Mathematics · Algebra VII: the sum of an arithmetic progression

Check. 1. S = 20/2 × (2×6 + 19×3) = 10 × (12 + 57) = 10 × 69 = 690. 2. S = 12/2 × (7 + (−15)) = 6 × (−8) = −48. 3. Model: sum of an arithmetic progression. Own words should match this idea: The sum of the first n terms of an arithmetic progression with first term a and last term ℓ is Sn = n/2 · (a + ℓ). Equivalently, Sn = n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d), where d is the common difference. Day 20 already defined the progression and the nth term; today adds the sum. Near-miss: This is not the nth term itself, and it is not the sum of a geometric progression (next week). Do not add n × the middle term unless n is odd and you know the middle term, or unless you are really using n times the average of the first and last, which is the same formula. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. a=4, d=3, n=15, last=4+14×3=46, S=15/2 × (4+46)=15/2 × 50=375.
  2. 210 = 10/2 × (3 + ℓ), so 210 = 5(3+ℓ), 42 = 3+ℓ, ℓ=39.
  3. The middle term is exactly the average of the first and last, so it plays the role of a half-pair. Algebraically, 2S = n(a+ℓ) never required n to be even.
  4. Terms: 3, 7, 11, 15, 19. Sum 55. Formula: 5/2 × (3+19)=5/2 × 22=55.
  5. a=1, d=1, n=20, last=20, S=10×(1+20)=210.

Physics · Friday mix: work, friction, and kinetic energy

Check. 1. W_pull = 40 J, W_friction = −19.6 J, W_net = 20.4 J. 2. Zero: the supporting force is vertical, the displacement is horizontal, and they are perpendicular (cos90° = 0). Constant height is the clue that the upward force has no displacement along itself. 3. Model: net work. Own words should match this idea: The sum of the work done by every force acting on the object along the path, equal (in this course) to the work done by the net force along the path, and equal to the change in kinetic energy. Near-miss: Not the work of one force alone, unless that force is the only one (or the only one with a component along the path). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. W_net = −25 J. Over 5 m, F_net × 5 × (−1) if the force is opposite, or W = F s cos180° = −25, so F = 5 N opposite the motion.
  2. KE = 12 J at 2 m/s. At 4 m/s, KE = ½ × 6 × 16 = 48 J, so the net work needed is 36 J. (Doubling speed quadruples KE: 4 × 12 = 48, and 48 − 12 = 36.)
  3. W_force = 8 × 4 × ½ = 16 J; W_friction = −1 × 4 = −4 J; W_net = 12 J.
  4. On a level floor the normal force is vertical and the displacement is horizontal, so θ = 90° and W = 0.
  5. ½ × 4 × v² = 20.4, so 2 v² = 20.4, v² = 10.2, v = √10.2 m/s (about 3.2 m/s, approximate).

History · The Twelve Tables as a class of source

Check. 1. Later quotations and paraphrases of fragments survive; a complete original inscription of all twelve does not (not in this course’s evidence, and not as a text we can treat as intact). 2. Polybius is a second-century observer-analyst of a working government. The Tables come down as a later-cited code, not as a complete document in their first form. 3. Model: Twelve Tables. Own words should match this idea: Rome’s early written law code, in the tradition dated to the mid-fifth century BCE (often 451–450 BCE). The Tables do not survive as a complete original inscription; later writers quote or paraphrase fragments. This course treats them as a class of source: a remembered written law, known in pieces, not as a book you can open and read straight through. Near-miss: Not a complete surviving bronze or stone text we can photograph end to end, and not a modern statute book. The traditional date is a tradition, even if it is a widely used one. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. An invented clause would look like a source and would not be one. The course will not add a fake table to fill the gap.
  2. That the wording is assembled from later quotations, not copied from a surviving complete original.
  3. In the tradition, writing the law down made it public and less easy to keep in a few hands. That is a reason the story itself gives; it is not a window into every official’s mind.
  4. No. Archaeology can recover objects and inscriptions that do survive; it cannot, by itself, put back a text that was lost. A new inscription, if one were found, would be new evidence, not a guarantee of the whole code.
  5. A plan that dates the tradition, states that we have fragments through later authors, and refuses to treat a reconstruction as an original photograph.

Check. 1. The lit family and the shadow family (with middles kept from mixing the two into one fog). 2. A lost edge where the form turns slowly or meets a background of similar value; a found edge where the form is cut clearly, often at an accent or at a plane’s firm turn. Own words should match this idea: A sitting devoted to organizing light and shadow on the head (or plaster) in a few values — usually a clear light, a clear large shadow, and one or two middles — rather than to adding new outline. Near-miss: Not a new drawing, and not a sudden rendering of every eyelash. The block-in stays; the values are brought into order. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  2. The two families have been flattened into each other; relight the difference between a lit plane and an unlit one, even if both are close in a soft light.
  3. Planes still explain where the value should change; a value pass is not a reason to smear the turns away.
  4. A specific edge (jaw into neck, or hair mass into shadow) with a reason (similar value, or a slow turn).
  5. Next week works a feature (eye, nose, mouth, or ear) in the context of this head, not as a new floating study.

Elective · Artistic anatomy 3: the torso — rib cage and pelvis

Check. 1. For example: the sternal notch, the lower margin of the cage, the front points of the pelvis; not a count of every rib as a drawing drill. 2. The weight leg and free leg already imply a pelvic drop on the free side; the torso’s counter-tilt is how that stance continues above the hips, in the copies we study as copies. 3. Model: rib cage (artistic). Own words should match this idea: The bony thorax as an artistic form: a rounded cage, wider than it is deep in a typical academic model, attaching to the spine, with a lower opening (the costal margin) that is not a straight cut-off. In the figure it is a volume, not a flat shield. Near-miss: Not a medical anatomy course, and not a count of every rib you must memorize as a drawing drill. You need the volume, the tilt, and the way the cage meets the abdomen and the shoulders. pelvis (artistic). Own words should match this idea: The bony basin at the base of the trunk, a volume that carries the abdomen and meets the thigh at the hip, with a front plane (including the anterior superior iliac spines as useful landmarks) and a tilt of its own. Near-miss: Not a second rib cage, and not a flat bow-tie drawn on the surface. Like the head’s egg, it is a solid first. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A simple labeled sketch or a clear verbal diagram: cage, pelvis, abdomen, and a opposite tilt.
  2. Do not force the navel to a memorized halfway mark if the particular form places it elsewhere; use it as a soft landmark and then look.
  3. Between cage and pelvis. They shorten, lengthen, or fold as the blocks tilt (a compressed side and a stretched side, in a typical contrapposto).
  4. An oblique would have an origin and an insertion on or toward these bony volumes, with a tendon or an aponeurosis as the attachment; the volume still comes first.
  5. A plan that locates the two bony volumes and their tilts before any surface detail.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. The dative marks the person to or for whom (Mārcō). The accusative marks the thing given (rosam). They are different jobs; Mārcum would make Marcus the thing given. 2. pedir is e → i: pido. pensar is e → ie: pienso. They are different families; “the vowel changes” is not enough. 3. Chlorophyll absorbs more in the red and the blue than in the green, so more leftover green light reaches the eye. The near-miss treats the seen color as the most absorbed color, which is backwards in this account. 4. The converse swaps the two sides (If Q, then P). The contrapositive swaps and negates both (If not Q, then not P). Only the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original. 5. los libros is the subject; gustan agrees with that plural. *Yo gusto los libros copies the English shape and makes yo the subject of a verb meaning “like.” 6. Livy wrote in the late first century BCE and early first century CE, centuries after the early kings. A late narrative can still show later tradition and argument; it cannot, by itself, prove a named year of the eighth century BCE. 7. Books 1–4, whose foreground is Telemachus in Ithaca, Pylos, and Sparta. The war is already over and the return is already delayed; we enter a story in motion, and Odysseus is not yet on stage. 8. Any well-explained caution: for example, 753 BCE is a traditional founding date, not an eyewitness year; the Twelve Tables survive as later fragments, not a complete original; a Doryphoros in marble is a later copy of a bronze type; Evans’s restorations at Knossos are modern layers; the Egyptian–Hittite treaty should be dated by a king’s year and a century, not by a false exact year such as 1269 BCE.
