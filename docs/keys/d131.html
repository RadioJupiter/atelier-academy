Latin · The pluperfect of sequor, a first look

Check. 1. The perfect participle secūtus plus the imperfect of sum: secūtus eram, secūtus erās, secūtus erat, secūtī erāmus, secūtī erātis, secūtī erant. 2. He had followed me (Puer mē secūtus erat). We had followed him. 3. Locūtus eram is the pluperfect of loquor, I had spoken. The pluperfect of ēgredior, ēgressus eram, waits until tomorrow. 4. Model: pluperfect of sequor. Own words should match this idea: The pluperfect of sequor uses the already-taught pattern of a deponent pluperfect: the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum. The participle is secūtus. The six forms are secūtus eram, secūtus erās, secūtus erat, secūtī erāmus, secūtī erātis, secūtī erant: I had followed, you had followed, and so on. It is not the present sequor, and it is not locūtus eram, which is the already-taught pluperfect of loquor. Near-miss: Not sequor, I follow, or I am following. Not secūtus sum, the perfect, I followed or I have followed. Not locūtus eram, I had spoken. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. You (s.) had followed; they had followed. 2. Secūtus eram; secūtī erant. 3. The grammar is taught in original sentences. A textbook narrative is not being copied here.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXV: related angles on the unit circle, a first look

Check. 1. Sin(180° − θ) = sin θ, and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. Sin(360° − θ) = −sin θ, and cos(360° − θ) = cos θ. 2. Sin 150° = 1/2, cos 150° = −√3/2. Sin 330° = −1/2, cos 330° = √3/2. 3. Sin 135° = √2/2, cos 135° = −√2/2. Radians remain reserved; today’s work stays in degrees. 4. Model: related angles on the unit circle. Own words should match this idea: On the unit circle already taught, an angle and 180° minus that angle have the same sine and opposite cosines: sin(180° − θ) = sin θ, and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. An angle and 360° minus that angle have opposite sines and the same cosine: sin(360° − θ) = −sin θ, and cos(360° − θ) = cos θ. These are read from the coordinates, still in degrees. Radians remain reserved. Near-miss: Not a new radius, and not a claim that the two angles are the same angle. Not a conversion into radians, which remain reserved. Not the law of sines, which waits until later this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. 120° = 180° − 60°, so sin 120° = sin 60° = √3/2, and cos 120° = −cos 60° = −1/2. 2. 315° = 360° − 45°, so sin 315° = −sin 45° = −√2/2, and cos 315° = cos 45° = √2/2. 3. Radians, the law of sines, and the law of cosines.

Writing · A chreia, a first look, after last week’s narrative

Check. 1. A short, useful anecdote that attributes a saying, a deed, or both to a named person. A narrative need not end in a famous saying, and has no stated moral. A fable has a stated moral, and often uses animals. 2. Alexander finds Diogenes lying in the sun and asks whether he wanted anything. Diogenes says, “I would have you stand from between me and the sun.” 3. A fair paraphrase: Diogenes asks only that Alexander not block the sun. It fits because Diogenes, already lying in the sun, treats that as enough, and asks for no gift. A full later elaboration, and a maxim, remain reserved. 4. Model: chreia (progymnasmata). Own words should match this idea: A chreia, in this first look, is a short, useful anecdote that attributes a saying, a deed, or both to a named person. It is not a fable, which has a stated moral and often uses animals, and it is not last week’s narrative, which tells who, what, when, and where, in order, without a required famous saying. The set example is from Plutarch’s Life of Alexander, in the 1859 Clough edition of the Dryden translation, as posted on Wikisource. Near-miss: Not last week’s narrative, which need not end in a famous person’s saying. Not a fable with a stated moral. Not a full eight-part elaboration, which remains reserved. Not an official biography of a living person. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The lesson uses the set Plutarch passage. Added scenes or official claims about a living person, or about Diogenes beyond that passage, are not being taught. 2. They are the wording of the 1859 translation. Teaching prose may say anything. 3. A maxim is a general saying without the anecdote of a named person on an occasion, and it remains reserved.

Physics · Sound, a first look, from OpenStax 17.1

Check. 1. A disturbance of matter transmitted from its source outward, and a wave. Hearing is the perception of sound, as vision is the perception of visible light. 2. Compressions are slightly higher-pressure regions, rarefactions slightly lower-pressure regions, moving out as a longitudinal pressure wave of the same frequency as the source. 3. Fluids have almost no shear strength. Sound waves in solids, which can be both transverse and longitudinal, are set aside. Decibels, and the numerical speed of sound, remain reserved until later this week. 4. Model: sound. Own words should match this idea: In OpenStax 17.1, sound is a disturbance of matter that is transmitted from its source outward. Sound is a wave. In air and most fluids it is a longitudinal pressure wave: compressions, regions of slightly higher pressure, and rarefactions, regions of slightly lower pressure, move out from the source and have the same frequency as the source. Hearing is the perception of sound, as vision is the perception of visible light. Near-miss: Not last week’s intensity I = P/A as if it already named sound, and not a transverse wave in air, which OpenStax sets aside because fluids have almost no shear strength. Sound waves in solids, which can be both transverse and longitudinal, remain beyond this first look. Decibels remain reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The energy is spread over a larger and larger area, and is also absorbed by objects and converted to thermal energy. 2. Today’s lesson is the nature of the wave, not a table of official speeds. Official speeds, when they appear, will be taken from OpenStax 17.2, not invented. 3. The wave in air is already the longitudinal wave taught last term. Intensity I = P/A was last week’s general wave quantity; it is not yet being used here as a sound formula, and decibels remain reserved.

History · The Treaty of Tordesillas, June 7, 1494: the line of 370 leagues, a first look

Check. 1. A treaty of June 7, 1494, in Davenport’s 1917 translation, Document 09. It draws a pole-to-pole line “at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands.” 2. The May 4 bull measured one hundred leagues from the Azores and Cape Verde. This treaty measures 370 leagues from the Cape Verde Islands. 3. Do not convert the 370 leagues into kilometers or into an official modern longitude. The assignment of the two sides, and the first-voyage letter, remain reserved. 4. Model: Treaty of Tordesillas (June 7, 1494). Own words should match this idea: The Treaty of Tordesillas, concluded June 7, 1494, is used here in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 English translation, Document 09, as posted on Wikisource. It draws a pole-to-pole line “at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands,” farther west than last week’s Inter Caetera of May 4, which measured one hundred leagues from the Azores and Cape Verde. The 370 leagues are the treaty’s wording, not an official modern length in kilometers, and not an official longitude. Near-miss: Not last week’s May 4 bull, which drew a line one hundred leagues west and south from the Azores and Cape Verde. Not an official conversion of 370 leagues into kilometers or into a modern longitude. The first-voyage letter remains reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. The line is to be drawn “at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, being calculated by degrees, or by any other manner as may be considered the best and readiest, provided the distance shall be no greater than abovesaid.” 2. The introduction says Portugal found the hundred-league limit too narrow. That is Davenport’s historical note, not the treaty’s own sentence about the 370 leagues. 3. The treaty gives a distance in leagues, not a modern official longitude, and none is being invented here.

Modern language · Spanish: the simple conditional of regular verbs, a first look

Check. 1. The whole infinitive plus -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían. Hablaría, hablarías, hablaría, hablaríamos, hablaríais, hablarían. 2. A fair trio: Mañana hablaría con ella, invented. Nosotros comeríamos más tarde, invented. Ellos vivirían aquí, invented place. 3. Hablaré is the simple future, I will speak. Irregular conditionals such as haría and tendría wait until later this week. The subjunctive remains reserved. 4. Model: simple conditional of a regular verb. Own words should match this idea: The simple conditional of a regular Spanish verb keeps the whole infinitive, as last week’s regular future did, and adds the endings -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían. Hablaría, I would speak; comerías, you would eat; viviríamos, we would live. The endings are not the future endings -é, -ás, -á. Irregular conditionals, such as haría and tendría, wait until later this week. Near-miss: Not the simple future hablaré, I will speak. Not the imperfect hablaba, I was speaking, or I used to speak, which uses a different stem. Not an official schedule. Irregular conditionals remain reserved until later this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. You (tú) would eat; I or he or she would live; you (vosotros) would speak. 2. Hablaría; comeríamos. 3. Times and places in the examples are invented classroom sentences, not official schedules.

Seminar · Logic: mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), a first look

Check. 1. They cannot both happen in the same outcome. Then P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), on an equally likely set. 2. P(4 or 5) = 1/3; P(even or 5) = 2/3. 3. Those events overlap at 4 and 6, so the union is 2, 4, 5, and 6, or 2/3, not 1/2 + 1/2. Official probabilities of real events, and a full treatment of overlapping events, are not being invented or taught as a complete method today. 4. Model: mutually exclusive events. Own words should match this idea: Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen in the same outcome. If they are mutually exclusive, and the outcomes are the equally likely set already taught, then P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). If they can overlap, the simple sum is a miss, because the overlap would be counted twice. Near-miss: Not a lesson in gambling, and not an official probability of a real event. Not last week’s complementary events as if “or” were the same as “not.” Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved. A full treatment of overlapping events, sometimes called inclusion-exclusion, remains reserved except as a miss: P(even or greater than 3) is not 1/2 + 1/2. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4, because the two named sectors cannot both occur on one spin. 2. Yes: a single face cannot be 4 and not 4. P(not 4) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6, from last week, which also equals 5/6 by adding the other five faces. 3. Outcomes that are not equally likely, official probabilities of real events, and a full addition rule for overlapping events.
