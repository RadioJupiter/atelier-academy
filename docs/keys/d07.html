Latin · Capitulum III, part 2: verbs and pronouns

Check. 1. vident. 2. The subject is plural, so the verb should be vident. 3. Model: verb ending. Own words should match this idea: The last part of a Latin verb, which shows who is acting (I, you, he or she, we, you plural, they) and, later, tense. Near-miss: The ending is not decoration, and English ‘you’ will not tell you whether a Latin verb is singular or plural. pronoun. Own words should match this idea: A short word that stands in for a noun (I, you, he, she, it, we, they), often already marked by the verb ending in Latin. Near-miss: A pronoun is not a noun, and dropping the Latin pronoun is not the same as dropping the ending. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (a) Dominus eum vocat. (b) Puer eam videt. (c) Puella eum audit.
  2. (a) Quem servus videt? (b) Quis cantat? (c) Cūr puer plōrat?
  3. E.g., Servus dormit quia fessus est (if your book has glossed fessus), or quia nōn labōrat. Check the verb ending and quia.

Mathematics · Algebra III: inequalities and the mean

Check. 1. x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) ≥ 0 outside the roots, including the roots: x ≤ 2 or x ≥ 3. 2. By AM–GM, (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), so √(ab) ≤ 5 and ab ≤ 25, with equality when a = b = 5. 3. Model: inequality. Own words should match this idea: A statement that one quantity is greater or less than another. Adding the same number, or multiplying by a positive number, preserves the inequality; multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the sign. Near-miss: You must not keep the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative. A quadratic inequality is also not solved by ‘just doing the same to both sides’ without using the roots and the shape of the graph. AM–GM inequality. Own words should match this idea: For a, b ≥ 0, (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b. It is a tool for maxima and minima. Near-miss: AM–GM does not apply to a negative number, and equality is not ‘almost when a is near b’ — it holds only when a = b. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (a) x > 4 (b) x ≥ −4 (sign reversed) (c) 2 < x < 3 (d) x ≤ −3 or x ≥ 3.
  2. a² + b² − 2ab = (a − b)² ≥ 0. ∎
  3. By AM–GM with a = x, b = 1/x: (x + 1/x)/2 ≥ √1 = 1. Equality at x = 1.
  4. A 50 cm × 50 cm square (area 0.25 m²).
  5. Square both: 17 + 2√70 versus 22 + 2√57. Since √70 < 9, 17 + 2√70 < 35; since √57 > 7.5, 22 + 2√57 > 37. So √3 + √19 is larger.
  6. a + b ≥ 2√(ab), b + c ≥ 2√(bc), c + a ≥ 2√(ca). All sides are positive, so multiply: product ≥ 8√(a²b²c²) = 8abc. ∎

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 2: reconstruction

Check. 1. So you practice finding a clear structure, not only remembering the original order. 2. Time order, or claim then reason (or effect then cause, if you choose it on purpose).

There is no single answer; the original paragraph is the key. Things students commonly notice: Addison's balanced antitheses (to enliven Morality with Wit, and to temper Wit with Morality); the arithmetic joke of "Twenty Readers to every Paper"; the extended metaphor of the fallow mind needing "Culture"; the climactic final sentence that sets Addison beside Socrates. If your version kept the ideas but lost the antithesis and the metaphor, you have found exactly what Franklin found: what you need to learn is the stock of words and figures, not the ideas. 3. Model: revision. Own words should match this idea: A second (or third) pass that changes structure, order, or wording after a draft exists, using the original as a standard of comparison. Near-miss: Revision is not proofreading alone, and it is not copying the original back in when your version is thin. diction. Own words should match this idea: The choice of words: precise, ordinary, or borrowed from the model, each chosen on purpose. Near-miss: Diction is not handwriting, and it is not ‘using the longest word’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Science · Phenomenon: why is the sky blue and the sunset red?

Check. 1. The Moon has almost no atmosphere, so there is essentially nothing to scatter sunlight across the sky. 2. Model: Rayleigh scattering. Own words should match this idea: The scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength. Short wavelengths are scattered much more strongly than long ones (the 1/λ⁴ law). Named after John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh. Near-miss: It is not the reason clouds are white: cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength and scatter all colors about equally. wavelength. Own words should match this idea: The distance from one crest of a wave to the next. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to about 700 nm (red). Near-miss: Wavelength is not the same as the speed of light, and it is not ‘how strong the light is’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. From the side, a faint bluish tint (short wavelengths scattered sideways); looking through, the light looks yellow-orange (blue removed from the direct beam). More milk deepens the effect, like a longer path through the atmosphere at sunset.
  2. (700/450)⁴ ≈ 1.556⁴ ≈ 5.9 times.
  3. Sunlight contains less violet than blue to begin with, some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and human eyes are much less sensitive to violet than to blue; the mixture of scattered wavelengths looks sky-blue to us.
  4. The Moon has almost no atmosphere, so there is essentially nothing to scatter sunlight across the sky.

Literature · Iliad, Books 4–6

Check. 1. Hector fights for his city and family; Achilles (so far) for his personal honor, which he has withdrawn from the common cause. A good answer stays with Books 1–6, not the end of the poem. 2. Model: aristeia. Own words should match this idea: A hero’s ‘best moment’: a stretch of the poem in which one warrior dominates the battlefield. Near-miss: It is not ‘the whole war’, and it is not a guarantee that the hero will survive the poem. aidōs. Own words should match this idea: Shame before others; a sense of honor that answers to the community’s gaze. Hector names it when he refuses to stay on the wall. Near-miss: It is not the same as private guilt, and it is not ‘cowardice’. It can drive a person toward danger. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It shows what the war destroys (family, household, the next generation) and makes Hector, and Troy, human. Every later scene of fighting is read in its light; the reader knows, as Hector half-knows, that Troy will fall.
  2. He says he would feel shame before the Trojans if he shirked battle; he has learned to fight in the front ranks. Honor in the eyes of the community outweighs his own safety, even though he foresees Troy's fall and Andromache's slavery.
  3. Hector fights for his city and family; Achilles (so far) for his personal honor, which he has withdrawn from the common cause.
  4. Any accurate detail with a reason (e.g., the nodding crest seen from the child's point of view; the laughter of both parents; Hector setting the helmet on the ground).

Atelier · Cast drawing 1: set-up and block-in

Check. 1. Set-up and block-in; not modeling or accents. 2. So every comparison is made from the same place.

Check from the marked spot: the block-in should overlay the cast's silhouette in sight-size, with shadow shapes drawn as precisely as the outline. If a proportion is off by more than 3% of the height, correct it before the next session. 3. Model: cast (sculpture). Own words should match this idea: A plaster (or similar) copy of a sculpture, used as a still, pale subject for training the eye in shape and value. Near-miss: A cast is not a Bargue plate (a plate is a printed drawing). You now draw from a solid form, still using sight-size. set-up. Own words should match this idea: The fixed arrangement of viewing point, easel, subject, and light, which must not wander from session to session. Near-miss: A set-up is not ‘wherever you happen to stand today’. If the viewpoint moves, yesterday’s proportions will not match. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Modern language · Spanish: tener

Check. 1. You (tú) have. 2. tienen. 3. Model: tener. Own words should match this idea: The Spanish verb ‘to have’, irregular in the present: tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen. Near-miss: Tener is not ser or estar, and tengo is not hablo. ‘I have’ and ‘I am’ are different verbs. irregular present. Own words should match this idea: A present-tense pattern that does not simply drop -ar, -er, or -ir and add the regular endings; the stem or the ending (or both) must be learned as they are. Near-miss: Irregular does not mean ‘no pattern at all’: tengo, tienes, tiene share a family, which you learn together. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (a) Tenemos hambre. (b) ¿Tienes frío? (c) Tiene razón. (d) Tienen que pintar hoy. (e) Tengo tres pinceles.
  2. (a) tengo (b) tienen (c) tienes.

Music theory · The major scale and key signatures

Check. 1. E♭ major. In flat keys, the second-to-last flat names the major key (here the flats are B♭, E♭, A♭). 2. Model: major scale. Own words should match this idea: A scale whose steps follow the pattern whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half (W W H W W W H). Near-miss: A major scale is not ‘any eight notes’, and starting on a note other than C will need sharps or flats to keep the pattern. key signature. Own words should match this idea: The sharps or flats placed at the start of the staff to keep a scale’s pattern, added in a fixed order (sharps: F C G D A E B; flats: B E A D G C F). Near-miss: A key signature is not a time signature, and the sharps are not added in random order. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. G A B C D E F♯ G (half steps B–C, F♯–G). E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E (G♯–A, D♯–E). B♭ C D E♭ F G A B♭ (D–E♭, A–B♭).
  2. Three: F♯, C♯, G♯.
  3. B♭, E♭, A♭: the second-to-last is E♭, so E♭ major.
