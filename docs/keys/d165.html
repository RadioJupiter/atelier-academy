Latin · Friday mix: purpose clauses with ut and nē

History · A clove-scented leaf, two hours, and a fall of water, from the second-voyage letter

Check. 1. A leaf with “the finest smell of cloves,” “like a laurel leaf.” They “remained only two hours,” and concluded the island “was uninhabited.” 2. It “looked of the breadth of an ox,” and “appeared to fall from the sky.” Some wagered that it was white rocks, others that it was water. 3. The later name is Major’s footnote, not wording that stands in the body of this extract. 4. Model: a clove-scented leaf, two hours, and a fall of water. Own words should match this idea: In Major’s 1870 wording, confirmed from the page, the island “was filled with an astonishingly thick growth of wood.” “We found there a tree whose leaf had the finest smell of cloves that I have ever met with; it was like a laurel leaf, but not so large: but I think it was a species of laurel.” “We found no signs of any people in this island, and concluded it was uninhabited; we remained only two hours, for it was very late when we landed, and on the following morning we left for another very large island, situated below this at the distance of seven or eight leagues.” “At three leagues distance, we could see an immense fall of water, which looked of the breadth of an ox, and discharged itself from such a height that it appeared to fall from the sky.” Men on board laid wagers, “some maintaining that it was but a series of white rocks, and others that it was water.” Nearer, “it was the most beautiful thing in the world to see from how great a height and from what a small space so large a fall of water was discharged.” Those claims are the letter’s. A footnote naming “Guadaloupe” is Major’s note, not the body. No official modern conversion of the letter’s leagues, or of “the breadth of an ox,” is taken. Not a later official island name written as if it stood in the body. Not an official modern conversion of “seven or eight leagues,” or of “the breadth of an ox.” Not later reserved sentences of the same letter. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
