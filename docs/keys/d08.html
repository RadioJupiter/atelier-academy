Mathematics · Geometry IV: inequalities in triangles

Check. 1. No. 5 + 12 = 17, which is not greater than 17; the three lengths are degenerate (they make a straight segment, not a triangle). 2. The perpendicular bisector of the segment AB.

  1. (a) No: 3 + 4 < 8. (b) Yes: 5 + 7 > 11. (c) No: 6 + 6 = 12 gives a degenerate "triangle" (a segment).
  2. 5 < x < 13.
  3. Opposite 50° < opposite 60° < opposite 70°.
  4. If PH ⊥ line at H and PM is oblique, triangle PHM has a right angle at H, which is its largest angle (the other two add to 90°). By theorem 1, the side opposite it, PM, is the longest: PH < PM. ∎
  5. (⇒) If P is on the perpendicular bisector through midpoint M, triangles PMA and PMB are congruent by SAS (PM common, AM = MB, right angles), so PA = PB. (⇐) If PA = PB, triangle PAB is isosceles, so the median PM from P is also the altitude (Day 4, exercise 3): P lies on the perpendicular bisector. (If P is on AB itself, P = M.) ∎
  6. For quadrilateral ABCD: AC < AB + BC and AC < AD + DC. Adding: 2AC < perimeter. ∎ Same for BD.
  7. Extend BP to meet AC at D. In triangle PDC: PC < PD + DC. So PB + PC < PB + PD + DC = BD + DC. In triangle ABD: BD < AB + AD. So BD + DC < AB + AD + DC = AB + AC. ∎

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium II, logic: claim, evidence, warrant

Check. 1. Validity is about the link; soundness is validity plus true premises. 2. Yes, but the argument has not earned it; the conclusion is not guaranteed.

  1. Claim: Athens is a model for other states and truly a democracy. Grounds: rule by the many; equal justice; office by merit; poverty no barrier. Warrant: these features make a state admirable and worthy of imitation. Weakest: the grounds, which hold only for male citizens; and the speech is a eulogy, so it selects only favourable facts.
  2. (a) The person who practises most deserves the role. (b) Age makes a painting valuable. (c) What every textbook says is correct. Each warrant is doubtful, which is why the arguments are weak.
  3. Check that all five parts are present and labelled, and that the qualifier and rebuttal are honest (e.g., "for most students"; "unless the student already draws accurately from life").

Physics · Newton's second law

Check. 1. 20 / 5 = 4 m/s². 2. It doubles.

  1. C. a = F/m.
  2. B. Constant velocity ⇒ net force zero.
  3. C. Slowing while moving up means acceleration is downward, so the net force is downward: tension < weight.
  4. a = (30 − 10)/10 = 2.0 m/s².
  5. F = 1200 × 2.5 = 3,000 N.
  6. N − mg = ma ⇒ N = 60(9.8 + 1.0) = 648 N (the scale reads more than the person's weight of 588 N).

History · The trial of Socrates

  1. Plato would present Socrates favourably; the speech is a literary work, not a transcript. Other evidence: Xenophon's own Apology (another student, with a different emphasis), Aristophanes' comic portrait in The Clouds, and the legal and political context.
  2. The horse is Athens: large, noble, sluggish. The gadfly is Socrates, stinging it awake by questioning. It implies Athens needs criticism to stay good, and that killing the critic harms the city.
  3. Some of those associated with Socrates (such as Critias, one of the Thirty Tyrants, and Alcibiades) had harmed the city; after defeat and civil strife, questioning traditional beliefs could look like undermining the community.
  4. Pericles praises Athens as a finished model; Socrates sees it as needing constant examination and correction.

Art history · Egypt: the art of permanence

  1. Check that the analysis names the composite view, the use of clear contour lines, flat areas of color, registers (horizontal bands) and hierarchical scale where present.
  2. Lost in the single-viewpoint version: the full, ideal shape of each part (the shoulder and eye become foreshortened). Gained: unity of viewpoint and a sense of real space.
  3. The atelier trains the eye to record appearances from one viewpoint; Egyptian art recorded idealised, lasting forms by rule. Both are systematic, and both use grids or measurement, but for opposite goals.
