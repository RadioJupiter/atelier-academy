Latin · Capitulum VII, part 4: in with the accusative or the ablative

Check. 1. Puerī in hortum intrant (or in hortum eunt). 2. Mārcus in aquā natat. The accusative would mean motion into the water, not swimming there. 3. Model: in + accusative. Own words should match this idea: With in, the accusative marks motion into or onto a place: in hortum, into the garden. Near-miss: Not ‘in the garden’ as a place where he already is — that is in hortō (ablative). The preposition is the same word; the case does the work. in + ablative. Own words should match this idea: With in, the ablative marks place where: in hortō, in the garden — no motion into the place, just location. Near-miss: Not motion into the garden (in hortum), and not a town name (Rome still uses the locative Rōmae for ‘at Rome,’ which you already have). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. in aquam; in aquā.
  2. Puer in hortum it.
  3. For example, Iūlia in vīllam it; Iūlia in vīllā est.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. Town names drop the preposition: accusative alone (Rōmam) for motion toward, and the locative Rōmae for ‘at Rome.’

Mathematics · Geometry XIII: intersecting chords

Check. 1. ED = 10, because 5 × 6 = 3 × ED, 30 = 3 × ED. 2. 4 × 9 = 6 × (the other segment), so the other segment is 6. (The chords can be 13 and 12.) 3. Model: intersecting chords. Own words should match this idea: If two chords of a circle meet at a point E inside the circle, then the products of the segments of each chord are equal: AE · EC = BE · ED. This is Euclid III.35. Near-miss: The equality is of products of segments, not of the lengths of the chords, and not of the segments themselves. AE need not equal BE. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 6x = 24, x = 4.
  2. 3 × 9 = s × s, so s² = 27, s = √27 = 3√3. The second chord has length 6√3.
  3. AC and BD are the whole chords. The theorem multiplies the two pieces of a chord, AE and EC, not the whole chord by one piece or by the other whole chord.
  4. 1 × 12 = 3x, so x = 4.
  5. E is the midpoint of both chords (the chords bisect each other). That is a special position; we do not assume it in the general theorem.

Near-miss: Hints are not a précis (a précis is a short complete statement of the meaning). They are also not a quotation list. Science · Photosynthesis as a net process

Check. 1. Reactants: carbon dioxide and water. Energy input: light. Products: a carbohydrate (written as C6H12O6 in the school equation) and oxygen. 2. The lesson states, without a classroom proof, that in the modern account the O2 released comes from water, not from CO2. The student has used a reasonable-looking bookkeeping guess that this course does not adopt. 3. Model: photosynthesis. Own words should match this idea: The process by which green plants (and some other organisms) use light to build sugars from carbon dioxide and water, and release oxygen as a product. A widely taught net equation is 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2. That equation is a school-level summary of a much longer process; it does not describe every intermediate step, and it is not a claim that a plant makes a single glucose molecule in one jump. Near-miss: Not respiration run backwards as a claim about every enzyme, and not “plants eat sunlight” as if light were a food. Light is the energy input; the carbon in the sugar comes from carbon dioxide. carbon dioxide. Own words should match this idea: A gas, CO2, that is one of the carbon sources for the sugars made in photosynthesis. In the net school equation, six molecules of CO2 are used for each C6H12O6 written on the product side. Near-miss: Not the same as oxygen (O2), which is written as a product in the net equation, and not the same as the carbon of a pigment molecule itself. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2. It does not show the intermediate steps (or that C6H12O6 is a representative sugar, not every product).
  2. It helps capture light. It is not itself the sugar, and it is not a reactant in the net equation as written.
  3. Because rates and yields depend on the plant, the light, and the measurement, and this course will not invent them.
  4. No: as a light-driven whole, the net process taught today does not run in the dark. (Respiration is a different process and was not today’s lesson.)
  5. Chlorophyll absorbs some of the light (more in the red and the blue than in the green); some of the energy so captured is the energy input to photosynthesis, and the leftover green light is a large part of why the leaf looks green.

Literature · Odyssey, Books 5–8: Calypso, the raft, and the Phaeacians

Check. 1. A song of Troy (of a war he lived) moves him to tears, and that emotion leads toward the revealing of his name. 2. The Phaeacians host a wrecked stranger with gifts, games, and a hearing; the suitors consume a house that is not theirs and press a wife whose husband is missing. 3. Model: in medias res (Odyssey). Own words should match this idea: Starting a narrative in the middle of the larger story. The Odyssey has already used this in the Telemachy; in Books 5–8 Odysseus appears on Calypso’s island, near the end of his wanderings, and only later (Books 9–12) will he tell the earlier adventures in order. Near-miss: Not a failure of chronology, and not the same as a narrator simply forgetting the early years. The telling is arranged. Phaeacians. Own words should match this idea: The people of Scheria, who receive Odysseus after his raft wreck, host him, hear his name, and (in the poem’s design) will send him home. Books 6–8 are this hospitality and the games and the asking of the guest’s name. Near-miss: Not Ithaca, and not Calypso’s island (Ogygia). The three places of this lesson — Ogygia, the raft, Scheria — should stay distinct. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. He leaves Calypso on a raft, a storm wrecks him, and he comes ashore in Scheria. No Cyclops, no cattle of the Sun, no landing in Ithaca yet.
  2. The wanderings of Books 9–12 (Cyclops, Circe, the dead, and the rest) and the actual return to Ithaca.
  3. Both are divine pushes that move a human situation stuck in delay (the son to act; the nymph to release the man). They are not the same scene, and the poem still leaves the humans to do the work of voyage and speech.
  4. A short, attributed phrase, or a clearly labeled paraphrase. No long paste.
  5. He is among the Phaeacians, who have not yet sent him home; Ithaca and the suitors are still ahead.

Modern language · Spanish: the gustar pattern

Check. 1. Le gusta la clase a Ana (or A Ana le gusta la clase). 2. Nos gusta leer. (infinitive → singular gusta) 3. Model: gustar (pattern). Own words should match this idea: A verb pattern in which the thing that is pleasing is the grammatical subject, and the person pleased is an indirect object: Me gusta el libro. Me gustan los libros. It is not built like English “I like the book” (subject + verb + object). Near-miss: Not *Yo gusto el libro, in the meaning “I like the book.” (gustar can mean “to be pleasing”; the English subject is usually the Spanish indirect object.) Number agreement is with the thing: me gusta el libro, me gustan los libros. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Me gusta la frase. Me gustan las frases.
  2. For example: ¿Te gusta el café? Sí, me gusta. (or No, no me gusta.)
  3. gustar is not a regular “we like” verb with a subject nosotros and a direct object. The books are the subject: Nos gustan los libros.
  4. It clarifies or emphasizes who is pleased; it does not replace me / nos.
  5. Two heard forms, each with a noun of the right number.

Economics & personal finance · Specialization and trade (a classroom table)

Check. 1. For example: Ana (who has 10 pages) trades 3 pages for 8 staves: she then has 7 pages and 8 staves (beat her split of 5 and 4). Beau has 3 pages and 12 staves (beat his split of 2 and 10). Other fair trades also work; the arithmetic must be shown. 2. Because the numbers are a classroom model, not a measured study of two real people or of a country. Invented figures must be labeled. 3. Model: specialization. Own words should match this idea: The choice to concentrate work on a narrower set of tasks, and to rely on others for the rest, rather than making everything oneself. In this course it is first a classroom idea, then a reason trade can help both sides — not a command to specialize, and not a history of a particular factory. Near-miss: Not “always better, in every case.” Learning a second skill can still be wise, and this course will not invent a percentage of “gains from specialization.” trade (classroom model). Own words should match this idea: An exchange of goods or tasks in which each side gives up something and receives something. In this lesson, numbers in a table are invented and labeled as invented, so that a gain from trade can be seen without pretending to be a national statistic. Near-miss: Not a claim about any real country’s trade balance, and not automatically “free trade is always good,” which is a larger argument than this lesson. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. What you give up of one good (or task) to obtain a unit of the other, read from the hourly rates in the table.
  2. The model shows a possible gain in a two-task pile; it does not prove a life rule, and school may value the weaker skill on purpose.
  3. No, or not necessarily: the table maximizes the pile of pages and staves, not the learning of the weaker task. A different goal can reasonably reject today’s split.
  4. If they later exchanged with a price, that price would be a signal of how many pages a stave is going for in their trade — still in the model, still not a national statistic.
  5. A caption that names the invented rates, the gain from specializing, and the limit that the table is a model.
