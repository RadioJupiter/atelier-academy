Latin · Capitulum VI, part 1: prepositions with the accusative

Check. 1. ad takes the accusative: for example ad vīllam. ab takes the ablative: for example ab oppidō. 2. Via est inter vīllam et oppidum. 3. Model: preposition (with the accusative). Own words should match this idea: In Capitulum VI a new closed list of prepositions takes the accusative: ad (to, toward), ante (in front of, before), post (behind, after), inter (between, among), prope (near), circum (around), apud (at the house of, with), per (through). Near-miss: These are not interchangeable with yesterday's ablative prepositions. ad takes the accusative; ab takes the ablative. English ‘to’ and ‘from’ are a useful pair, but the Latin case is fixed by the preposition. ad and ab. Own words should match this idea: A pair of opposites: ad + accusative means ‘to, toward’; ab + accusative is wrong — ab takes the ablative and means ‘away from’. Near-miss: ad vīllā and ab vīllam are both ungrammatical. The preposition, not the English word, chooses the case. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. ad hortum; ex hortō or ab hortō; prope ōstium; circum oppidum.
  2. ad vīllam; ab hortō; prope hortum.
  3. Each sentence must show the right case after the preposition.
  4. Clear reading.
  5. post; both ante and post take the accusative.

Mathematics · Geometry VII: the parallelogram

Check. 1. Consecutive angles are supplementary, so 2∠Q + ∠Q = 180°, 3∠Q = 180°, ∠Q = 60°, ∠P = 120°. The angles are 120°, 60°, 120°, 60°. 2. O is the midpoint, so AO = 9 and BO = 5. 3. Model: parallelogram. Own words should match this idea: A quadrilateral whose opposite sides are parallel (both pairs). In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal, opposite angles are equal, consecutive angles are supplementary, and the diagonals bisect each other. Near-miss: A parallelogram is not ‘any slanted rectangle’, and a quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides is a trapezoid in this course, not a parallelogram. diagonal. Own words should match this idea: A segment joining two vertices of a polygon that are not already joined by a side. In a parallelogram the two diagonals bisect each other: they meet at their midpoints. Near-miss: A diagonal is not a side, and it is not true that the diagonals of every parallelogram are equal (that happens in a rectangle). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Diagonal AC. AB ∥ DC and AD ∥ BC give alternate interior angles. Then △ABC ≅ △CDA (ASA). Opposite angles, and opposite sides, are equal.
  2. Let the diagonals meet at O. Triangles AOB and COD are congruent (ASA or AAS, using alternate interior angles and the vertical angles at O), so AO = CO and BO = DO.
  3. The perimeter is 2 × (9 + 14) = 46. The diagonals cannot be found from the sides alone (the angle is still free).
  4. ∠D = 125°, ∠DAE = 27.5°, so in △ADE the angles are 27.5°, 125°, 27.5° (isosceles, AD = AE is not claimed — wait). AD is a side, AE is a bisector to DC. ∠ADE = ∠D = 125°, ∠DAE = 27.5°, so ∠AED = 180 − 125 − 27.5 = 27.5°. Yes, △ADE is isosceles with AD = AE.
  5. Triangles AOB and COD are congruent by SAS (vertical angles and the two pairs of equal half-diagonals). Alternate interior angles are then equal, so AB ∥ DC and AD ∥ BC.

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method: hints from a new model

Check. 1. Each row should be short, ordered, and free of copied clauses. If a phrase is word-for-word Johnson, it is too long. 2. The main verb often arrives after a condition or a pair of conditions; the delay holds the thought together as one period rather than as two short announcements. 3. Model: hint (for reconstruction). Own words should match this idea: A brief cue — a word or a short phrase — written down in order, after you have read a model and turned it over, so that you can later rebuild the passage from the cues rather than from memory of the exact wording. Near-miss: A hint is not a complete outline, and it is not a quotation. If the hint is itself a copied clause, the exercise collapses into memorization. period (a long constructed sentence). Own words should match this idea: A sentence that holds several related clauses in a designed order, often delaying the main point until the thought is complete. Johnson frequently writes in periods. Near-miss: A period is not ‘any long sentence’. A sentence that is long because it rambles, or because commas splice new thoughts, is not a period. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete hint sheet, usable on Day 17.
  2. Every main verb marked.
  3. The student period should be grammatical and should delay the main verb honestly, not by a splice.
  4. A typical answer: Johnson’s clauses are longer, and the main verb is later.
  5. An accurate copy of the first sentence only.

Science · Autoxidation and the oil film

Check. 1. Autoxidation is a reaction with oxygen; evaporation is a liquid becoming a gas. The oil smear stays and hardens; the water smear shrinks and disappears. 2. Thinner layers let oxygen reach the whole thickness, so the film can harden through rather than skin over a soft interior. 3. Model: autoxidation. Own words should match this idea: A reaction in which a substance combines with oxygen in the air, without being set on fire. In a drying oil, oxygen attacks the unsaturated parts of the oil’s molecules and begins a process that links those molecules together. Near-miss: Autoxidation is not burning with a flame, and it is not evaporation. It can be sped up by heat, by light, and (historically) by certain metal compounds used as ‘driers’, but those are aids, not a different process. polymerization. Own words should match this idea: A process in which small molecules join into much larger ones (polymers). In a drying oil, after oxygen has started to react with the oil, the molecules become cross-linked and the liquid becomes a solid network, the paint film. Near-miss: Polymerization here is not the same as the making of modern plastics in a factory, though the word is the same, and it is not ‘drying’ in the sense of wetness leaving. The everyday word ‘dry’ is the painter’s word; the chemical word is this one. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. An honest record, judged against the model; ‘not yet firm’ is acceptable.
  2. Oxygen does not evaporate out of the oil; oxygen from the air reacts with the oil. A repaired sentence should say that.
  3. They sped the hardening of the film; they are avoided because lead compounds are toxic. (No experiment.)
  4. A linseed smear should, as a rule, become firm sooner than a poppy or walnut smear of the same thickness, in the same room. No invented number of days is required.
  5. The open film, because it has a supply of oxygen. The sealed ampoule should remain liquid much longer.

Literature · Iliad, Books 11–16: Patroclus

Check. 1. A run of excellence in battle. Diomedes lives; Patroclus’s aristeia ends in his death. 2. It shows that a death can be central and still not be something Zeus simply cancels; the poem’s order, and the other gods, constrain him. A good answer is careful and does not invent a speech Zeus does not make in the book as you read it. 3. Model: aristeia. Own words should match this idea: A hero’s run of excellence in battle, a stretch of the poem in which one fighter dominates the action. The word is Greek for ‘excellence’ or ‘best moment’ (you met it as a name in Week 2’s summary of Book 5, Diomedes). Near-miss: An aristeia is not ‘the whole war’, and it is not a guarantee of survival. Patroclus’s best moment is also the path to his death. Patroclus in Achilles’ armor. Own words should match this idea: The decision, in Book 16, that Patroclus will put on Achilles’ armor, lead the Myrmidons, and drive the Trojans from the ships, but go no further — a command Achilles gives and Patroclus does not keep. Near-miss: This is not a ‘costume change’ for variety, and it is not Achilles returning. The poem makes the difference tragic: the friend is not the hero, and the limit is spoken and then crossed. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A page that quotes sparingly, if at all, and that shows the limit and the crossing.
  2. With the chiefs out and the ships in danger, Achilles will give Patroclus and the armor, not himself — that is the pressure and the partial yield.
  3. The simile should be identified (book, approximate place) and its extra work named (scale, pathos, an everyday comparison, and so on).
  4. Although the armor frightens the Trojans as if Achilles had come, it is still Patroclus inside it, and he can be killed.
  5. A labeled prediction: a fight over the body; Achilles will hear; his anger will change its object. (The next lesson will test the prediction, not grade it as a spoiler.)

Atelier · Cross-hatching and control of value

Check. 1. It is a later layer, at a new angle, still following the form, used to deepen or tighten. You refuse it on a highlight, on a passage already dark enough, and as a cover for a wrong shape. 2. Because the plane is flat: a curving hatch would invent a turn. The cross should lie in that plane, almost straight, at a shallow angle to the first layer. 3. Model: cross-hatching. Own words should match this idea: A second (or later) layer of hatch laid across the first, at a new angle, to deepen value or to tighten form. The new strokes still follow the surface, or they cross the first layer at a shallow angle; they do not cancel the form-following of the first layer. Near-miss: Cross-hatching is not a grid drawn as a decoration, and it is not a way to hide a wrong contour. If the first layer is careless, a second layer will not invent the form. control of value. Own words should match this idea: The skill of making a patch as light or as dark as you intend, and of keeping a family even where the form is even. On a nine-step scale (Day 4), you should be able to place a hatch at a chosen step and not overshoot into a black that you cannot lift. Near-miss: Control is not ‘as dark as the pencil can go’, and it is not a single formula of three fixed layers. You stop when the relation is right. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A drawing whose darks were built in controlled layers, with families intact and a reserved light.
  2. The second-layer passages are marked, and the aimed step is named honestly.
  3. Three sentences that report a stop, the fate of reflected light, and a real almost-overshoot or overshoot.
  4. Two small rectangles of even, named value, the second darker by a modest cross.
  5. A specific, honest comparison with an earlier drawing.

Modern language · Spanish: direct-object pronouns

Check. 1. Ella lo compra. / Nosotros los vemos. 2. La queremos encontrar. or Queremos encontrarla. 3. Model: direct-object pronoun. Own words should match this idea: A small word that stands in for a direct object already known: me, te, lo, la, nos, os, los, las. In a simple present sentence it stands just before the conjugated verb. Near-miss: A direct-object pronoun is not the subject pronoun (yo, tú, él). Lo veo is not ‘he sees’; it is ‘I see him/it’, with yo understood or added: Yo lo veo. placement of the direct-object pronoun. Own words should match this idea: Before a conjugated verb: Lo veo. Attached to an infinitive, or placed before the conjugated verb that governs the infinitive: Voy a verlo or Lo voy a ver. Both of those last two are correct. Near-miss: Do not leave a gap and do not put the pronoun after a plain conjugated verb (Veo lo is wrong in this course). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Sí, los tengo. / Sí, la veo.
  2. Lo veo. The pronoun stands before the conjugated verb.
  3. No la entiendo.
  4. Two grammatical pairs, with lo/la/los/las matching gender and number.
  5. Los van a mirar. or Van a mirarlos.

Economics & personal finance · Markets and prices

Check. 1. Yes: buyers and sellers can meet and trade used books. The closed storeroom, with no offers to sell or buy, is not a working market in this sense; it is a stock of goods. 2. An invented case: a mild winter lowers the price of coats. Buyers are willing to take more at that lower price; some sellers offer fewer new coats or discount to clear stock. The example must stay labeled as invented. 3. Model: market. Own words should match this idea: A meeting of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service, in which they can trade. It need not be a single building or a single hour; it is the arrangement that lets offers to buy and offers to sell meet. Near-miss: A market is not only a covered hall, and it is not ‘the economy’ as a whole. There is a market for a good, not one single market for everything. price (as a signal). Own words should match this idea: The amount of money (or other payment) asked or agreed for a good or service, which also serves as a signal: a high price discourages some buying and encourages some selling and production; a low price does the reverse, other things equal. Near-miss: A price is not a moral grade (‘expensive means better’), and it is not by itself a proof of a bargain. It is information, mixed with other information. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Because income, tastes, quality, or other prices may have changed at the same time, and then the change in quantity is not a pure price-signal story.
  2. A price can rise because a good is scarce, because costs rose, or for other reasons; ‘better’ is a quality claim, not the meaning of a higher price.
  3. When a good is scarce, a price rations who uses it and signals that it would pay to offer more or to use a substitute. People may dislike that rationing; the lesson still names the function.
  4. A fair answer names a real requirement (clear ownership, a way to pay, information about quality, a place or network in which to meet).
  5. Four careful sentences, labeled as invented, with a two-sided signal and no fake numbers presented as facts.
