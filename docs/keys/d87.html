Latin · Capitulum XV: domī, and a close of the chapter’s new grammar

Check. 1. At home. In this use it is a locative and takes no preposition. 2. The boys are at home. 3. Both are locatives answering ubi? Rōmae was taught in Capitulum VI. Domī is the new form today. 4. Model: domī. Own words should match this idea: Domī means at home, with no preposition. It is a locative form of domus. The locative case itself was already taught in Capitulum VI for the names of towns (Rōmae, at Rome). Domī is one of a small set of common words that also keep a locative, and it is the first look at this particular form. Near-miss: Not a first look at the locative as a case, and not a first look at Rōmae. Not in plus the ablative (in casā), which is a different construction. Domī does not take a preposition in this use. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Magister domī est. Domī nōn sumus.
  2. Ego domī sum, at tū in lūdō es. (Sed is also acceptable if you are correcting rather than opposing two sides.)
  3. No. In casā is in plus the ablative. Domī is a locative without a preposition.
  4. A complete sentence such as Sī magister domī est, discipulī in lūdō recitant.

Mathematics · Geometry XLIII: a right prism, volume as base area times height

Check. 1. V = 15 × 8 = 120 cubic units. 2. 140 = 35 × h, so h = 4. 3. h is the perpendicular distance between the bases, as in the area formulas already taught. A slanted edge is longer than that height and is not B × (that edge). 4. Model: volume of a right prism. Own words should match this idea: A right prism has two parallel, congruent bases, and its side edges are perpendicular to the bases. Its volume is V = B × h, where B is the area of one base and h is the perpendicular height between the bases. A right rectangular prism is the special case B = ℓ × w, so V = ℓ × w × h, already taught yesterday. Near-miss: Not a first look at the area of a triangle or a rectangle, already taught, and not a first look at yesterday’s box formula. Not a cylinder, which is tomorrow’s job. Not a slanted prism, whose volume is not taught today. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. B = ½ × 5 × 12 = 30; V = 30 × 4 = 120 cubic units.
  2. B = 9 × 2 = 18; V = 18 × 6 = 108 cubic units. (Any face may be chosen as the base, so long as h is perpendicular to that face: 9 × 6 × 2 and 2 × 6 × 9 give the same 108.)
  3. The second volume is twice the first, 120 against 60, because V is proportional to h when B is fixed.
  4. The area of the triangle is B, a number of square units. The volume is B × h, in cubic units.

Science · The enthalpy of fusion of ice, from the Harvey/NIST table

Check. 1. It is the energy needed to melt one gram of ice at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, with no change of temperature, from Harvey’s NIST/CRC table. 2. 10 × 333.4 = 3,334 J, or 3.334 kJ. Invented mass. 3. ΔfusH is an energy per gram for the change of state at 0 °C, not a new temperature. The ice and the melt remain at 0 °C in this account while melting proceeds. 4. Model: enthalpy of fusion of ice. Own words should match this idea: The enthalpy of fusion of ice, written ΔfusH in the Harvey/NIST table, is the energy needed to melt ice at the transition, per gram, without a change of temperature. At 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the table gives ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g. The same quantity is often called the latent heat of fusion in school physics. Near-miss: Not a first look at melting at 1 atm, already taught when the Water module opened, and not a first look at last week’s densities. Not a temperature change, and not a boiling figure. Not an official liquid-water specific heat, which is not taken from a table row this week. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  2. 0.9167 g/cm³ is a density of ice Ih at 0 °C, from the same table’s density row. ΔfusH is a phase-transition energy, in J/g, not a density.

Elective · Music: contrasting and parallel periods

Check. 1. Parallel: the consequent begins like the antecedent. Contrasting: the consequent begins differently. 2. A period often already has a weaker cadence then a stronger one. Contrasting names a difference at the openings, not that fact about cadences. 3. No. Antecedent and consequent name the two phrases. Parallel or contrasting names whether their openings match. You still have to look at how the second phrase begins. 4. Model: contrasting period. Own words should match this idea: A contrasting period, in this lesson’s school sense, is a period whose consequent does not begin like the antecedent. The two phrases still form one period, with cadences already taught: often a weaker cadence at the end of the antecedent and a stronger one at the end of the consequent. Near-miss: Not a first look at a period, or at antecedent and consequent, taught last week. Not a first look at a half cadence or an authentic cadence. Not a parallel period, which is today’s other name. parallel period. Own words should match this idea: A parallel period, in this lesson’s school sense, is a period whose consequent begins like the antecedent, usually with the same opening figure, before it continues to a stronger cadence. Near-miss: Not a first look at a period, and not a contrasting period, which begins differently. Not a claim about every later textbook’s extra types, which are reserved. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Two clearly labeled invented examples, one of each.
  2. They still help you hear where each phrase ends, as last week taught. Today does not reteach their definitions as new.
  3. The period, with antecedent and consequent, was taught last week. Today names two relations of the openings.
  4. This lesson teaches only these two names. Further types remain reserved, and are not to be invented as if they had been taught.
