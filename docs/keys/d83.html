Latin · Capitulum XV: nōs and vōs, and licet

Check. 1. You (plural) are pupils. 2. Nōbīs audīre licet. 3. Recitāre possumus: we are able to recite. Nōbīs recitāre licet: we are allowed to recite. Ability and permission are not the same. 4. Model: nōs and vōs. Own words should match this idea: Nōs means we or us; vōs means you (plural). The dative and ablative forms are nōbīs and vōbīs. Like ego and tū, these pronouns are written for contrast or clarity; the verb ending already names the person. Near-miss: Not a first look at ego, tū, mē, tē, mihi, or tibi, or at nōbīscum from last week. The new work is the plural pronouns and nōbīs / vōbīs. licet. Own words should match this idea: Licet is an impersonal verb: it is allowed. It has no personal subject. The person who is allowed stands in the dative, and what is allowed is an infinitive: Nōbīs recitāre licet. Near-miss: Not the same as posse. Posse says what someone is able to do; licet says what is permitted. Recitāre possum is I can recite; Mihi recitāre licet is I am allowed to recite. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Nōs: we/us. Vōs: you (plural). Nōbīs: to/for us. Vōbīs: to/for you (plural).
  2. I am not allowed to be silent.
  3. Vōs recitātis, at nōs tacēmus. (At is taught tomorrow; sed is already known and may be used today: Vōs recitātis, sed nōs tacēmus.)
  4. The ending -tis already means “you (plural).” Vōs is added only to stress or contrast that person.

Mathematics · Geometry XL: a sector of a circle

Check. 1. (90/360) × π × 10² = (1/4) × 100π = 25π. 2. 16π = (θ/360) × 64π, so 16 = 64θ/360, θ = 90°. 3. The sector is bounded by an arc, not by the chord. The triangle cuts off a smaller region than the sector (unless the “arc” has collapsed, which is not today’s case). 4. Model: sector of a circle. Own words should match this idea: A sector of a circle is the region bounded by two radii and the included arc. Its area is the same fraction of the circle’s area as the central angle is of 360°, or as the arc is of the circumference: A = (θ/360) × πr², when θ is measured in degrees. Near-miss: Not a triangle with two radii and a chord, and not the area of the whole circle. Not a first look at a central angle, which was taught on Day 21. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (72/360) × 100π = (1/5) × 100π = 20π.
  2. (90/360) × 2π × 6 = (1/4) × 12π = 3π.
  3. (180/360) × 16π = 8π.
  4. The fraction of the circle is the fraction of the turn at the center. An inscribed angle stands on an arc but is not that fraction in the same direct way (it was already taught as half the central angle on the same arc).

Writing & Rhetoric · The opening and the close

Check. 1. It names the set text or question and states the thesis. It refuses a long warm-up, a tour of the outline, and a vast claim the essay will not defend. 2. It returns to the thesis in new wording and stops. A new reason belongs in a body paragraph; the close is too late to start one. 3. It announces a tour instead of stating the claim. The reader still does not know what will be defended. 4. Model: essay opening. Own words should match this idea: The opening of this short analytical essay names the set text or question and states the thesis, without a long warm-up and without announcing the outline (“In this essay I will…”). Near-miss: Not a first look at a thesis, and not the first body paragraph. Not a biography of the author, unless the set task has made that the question, which this week’s essay has not. essay close. Own words should match this idea: The close of this short analytical essay returns to the thesis in new wording, after the body and the other side have been heard, and stops. It does not tack on a new reason, and it does not merely copy the opening sentence. Near-miss: Not a first look at a conclusion as a school word in general, and not a moral for the reader’s life unless the set text itself has made that the question. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A short opening that names the text or question and states the thesis. Wording will vary.
  2. A close that returns to the thesis in new wording and stops.
  3. Faults, if any, are marked: missing thesis, copied opening, or a new late reason.
  4. The mention shows what remains standing after the objection, or it is cut as decoration.

Physics · The period of a mass on a spring

Check. 1. T = 2π√(0.45/20) = 2π√(0.0225) = 2π × 0.15 = 0.30π s. 2. T is multiplied by 2, because T is proportional to √m, and √4 = 2. 3. The mass-spring period depends on mass; the small-swing pendulum period does not. The pendulum formula uses L and g, not k. 4. Model: period of a mass-spring. Own words should match this idea: For a mass m on a spring that obeys Hooke’s law, and with no damping, the period is T = 2π√(m/k). T is larger for a larger mass and smaller for a stiffer spring. It does not depend on the amplitude. Near-miss: Not last week’s simple-pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g). A pendulum’s period, for a small swing, does not depend on mass; a mass-spring period does. simple harmonic motion. Own words should match this idea: Simple harmonic motion, in OpenStax’s wording, is oscillatory motion for a system whose net force can be described by Hooke’s law. A mass on a spring, without damping, is the standard example. Near-miss: Not every oscillation. Last week’s pendulum is close to this for a small swing (less than about 15°), which is why a similar square-root formula appeared, but the pendulum formula is not F = −kx written in the same direct way, and mass drops out of it. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. No. T is independent of amplitude. A clock built on such an oscillator keeps the same period whether the swing is a little larger or a little smaller, as OpenStax notes.
  2. π/2 = 2π√(m/32), so 1/4 = √(m/32), 1/16 = m/32, m = 2 kg. Invented figures.
  3. About 1.36 Hz, as in OpenStax 16.3 (they compute f first from (1/(2π))√(k/m)).
  4. L and g are the pendulum’s quantities. A horizontal mass-spring system is not swinging from a length L in the same sense; its T uses m and k.

History · The four triangles and the dome that seems suspended (Dewing I.1.42–49)

Check. 1. They fill the places between the arches of a four-cornered plan, each contracted to a point below and ending in the segment of a circle which they support; the dome rests on that circle. 2. “suspended from Heaven” (in “to cover the space with its golden dome (sphaira) suspended from Heaven”). The support just named is the circle upon which the dome “rests.” 3. Each detail draws the eye on; the vision constantly shifts; observers cannot settle on one detail or fully understand the craftsmanship, and they depart overwhelmed. 4. Model: four triangles under the circle (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing I.1.43–45, because the arches form a four-cornered plan, “the stonework between the arches produces four triangles (trigôna).” Each triangle is contracted to a point below, “by the coming together of each pair of arches,” and “ends in the segment of a circle which it supports.” Upon that circle rests the dome. Near-miss: Not a first look at Monday’s four piers and four arches. Not a measured modern plan of the triangles. dome that seems suspended (Dewing). Own words should match this idea: In Dewing I.1.45–46, “upon this circle rests the huge spherical dome (sphairoeidês tholos) which makes the structure exceptionally beautiful. Yet it seems not to rest upon solid masonry, but to cover the space with its golden dome (sphaira) suspended from Heaven.” Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s crescent, and not an official height of the dome. Not a claim that the dome has no support. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The structure is interrupted at short intervals; openings are left as channels for the admission of light in sufficient measure.
  2. Like: Dewing states a seeming (float; suspended) after or beside a real support (bracing; the circle). Unlike: last week’s image was a crescent at the east end; today’s is the huge spherical dome over the center.
  3. It is Dewing’s British spelling. A précis may write “skillful”; a quotation may not change it.
  4. I.1.50 begins the next stretch (“It was by many skilful devices…”). It is reserved, because today’s set extract ends at “So much, then, for this.”

Atelier · Modeling inside the shadow family, still monochrome

Check. 1. Inside the shadow family only, or almost only. The light family, and the features, are left. 2. Too many or too strong value changes can turn one family into patches. The test is whether the two families still read at a distance. 3. The terminator is a turning, not an outline. A new line fences the form and undoes last week’s and Monday’s work. 4. Model: modeling in the shadow family. Own words should match this idea: Modeling in the shadow family, at this stage, means small, monochrome changes of value inside the shadow, to show a turn of the form, while the whole family remains darker than the light family. The light family is left alone, or nearly so. Near-miss: Not a first look at core shadow, and not a first look at the families. Not full modeling of the light, and not full color. Not a finish of the features. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. One modest turn, visible in the work.
  2. A clear distance judgment.
  3. A specific place in the light left alone.
  4. An honest repair note, or a confirmed clear family.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of saber

Check. 1. Supe, supiste, supo, supimos, supisteis, supieron. 2. Yesterday I found out the truth. 3. The preterite, as taught here, names the occasion of finding out. English “I knew” often names a state, which is a different kind of meaning. 4. Model: supe. Own words should match this idea: Supe is the first-person singular preterite of saber. The stem is sup-: supe, supiste, supo, supimos, supisteis, supieron. In the preterite, the usual school sense is a completed event of finding out, or coming to know, on that occasion, not a general state of knowing. Near-miss: Not a first look at the present sé, and not a first look at pude or puse. Not the imperfect, which is not today’s job. Not last week’s supe invented as a uv-stem: this is a different verb and a different stem. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A complete invented sentence, such as Ayer supimos la verdad. — Yesterday we found out the truth.
  2. Supe; pude; puse.
  3. Supo.
  4. Tuve is tener, uv-stem, already taught last week. Supe is saber, sup- stem, “found out” in this lesson’s school sense.

Elective · Art history: the pendentive, as a later name for Dewing’s four triangles

Check. 1. A concave triangular transition from a square base of arches to the circle of a dome. Dewing calls them “four triangles (trigôna).” 2. It rests upon the circle that the triangles support. I.1.46 is a seeming (“suspended from Heaven”), not a denial of that support. 3. The Pantheon, as taught, is a circular temple; its dome sits on a circular plan, with an oculus, not on four triangles in the corners of a square of arches. 4. Model: pendentive. Own words should match this idea: A pendentive is a concave triangular section of vaulting that makes the transition from a square base to the circle of a dome and helps carry the dome’s weight. In this lesson it is taught as the later architectural name for the four triangles Dewing describes in Buildings I.1.43–45, “the stonework between the arches” that “produces four triangles” and “ends in the segment of a circle which it supports.” Near-miss: Not a first look at Dewing’s four arches or four piers (Monday), or at the dome’s seeming (today’s history lesson, if you have already read it, or the same extract used here). Not a first look at the Pantheon, already taught on Day 38 as a circular temple with a concrete dome and an oculus, generally treated as a Hadrianic rebuild. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The word is a later school term, borrowed from French. Dewing’s wording is “triangles.” This course may use both, but it must not put the later word into his mouth.
  2. The piers (I.1.37–38) are the four great supports from which the arches spring. The pendentives are the triangular stonework between the arches, up at the transition to the circle.
  3. Dewing does not give that span here, and this course does not invent one.
  4. The circular, cylindrical structure is interrupted by openings that admit light “in sufficient measure.” That is the drum or circular structure, not the triangle itself.
