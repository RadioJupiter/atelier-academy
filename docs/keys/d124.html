Latin · The deponent pluperfect, a second mixed practice

Check. 1. A fair sentence: Puerī locūtī erant, et tum sēdērunt. They had spoken, and then they sat. 2. A fair sentence: Cotīdiē puerī loquēbantur. They used to speak daily. 3. The absolute is a loosely attached phrase with no sum. The main verb, if pluperfect, would still need eram, erās, erat, or the plural.

Exercises. 1. Puella cōnāta erat, et tum locūta est: she had tried, and then she spoke. 2. Cōnābātur: cotīdiē asks for a habitual past, he used to try, not he had tried before another past moment. 3. Locūtus eram, locūtus erās, locūtus erat, locūtī erāmus, locūtī erātis, locūtī erant.

Mathematics · Geometry LXX: skew lines, a first look

Check. 1. They do not meet, they are not parallel, and they do not lie in one and the same plane. 2. They do not meet, they are not parallel, and no single plane contains both; one is the front floor edge, the other the back top edge. 3. A sheet of paper is one plane. In one plane, two lines either meet or are parallel, so they cannot be skew. 4. Model: skew lines. Own words should match this idea: Two lines in space are skew if they are not parallel, do not meet, and do not lie in one and the same plane. Skew lines are a space relation; two lines in a single plane are either parallel or intersecting, and cannot be skew. Near-miss: Not two parallel edges, which never meet but do lie in one plane (or in many, if one wishes to name a plane of two parallels). Not two edges that meet at a corner. Not two lines drawn on one sheet of paper. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. AB and DC are parallel, in the front plane, not skew. AB and AD meet at A, not skew. AB and the top back edge are skew. 2. Yes. DA and BF do not meet, are not parallel (one is vertical, one runs along the floor to the right and back), and they do not lie in one face or in one plane. 3. Parallel edges do not meet, but they are parallel, and they lie in one plane (the floor). Skew lines are not parallel.

Writing · A tighter original fable

Check. 1. A fair tighter fable is four or five short sentences of action, then one moral. It is not Townsend’s crow unless you were still retelling, which is not today’s job. 2. A fair cut is any sentence of advice that appeared before the last action, or a second moral such as “care matters” in the ink-jar draft. 3. If you removed the remaining events, the moral would no longer be earned. If the moral still fits after the cuts, the cuts were only lectures, not load-bearing action.

Exercises. 1. Both are short invented narratives with a stated moral after the action. They do not share the crow, the pitcher, or Townsend’s moral unless you have slipped back into a retelling. 2. The narrative stops being a narrative, and the moral is no longer reserved for the end. 3. One sentence, at the end, that follows from the action you kept.

Science · Hematite, and a red from a yellow ochre, a first look

Check. 1. Hematite is the anhydrous iron oxide Fe2O3, and it is the usual red ochre mineral in the USGS sources. 2. A red ocher can be made by calcining common yellow ocher, because the color depends mainly on the degree of hydration. 3. An official iron percentage of a lump, and an official kiln temperature. Lapis lazuli and ultramarine remain reserved. 4. Model: hematite. Own words should match this idea: Hematite is the anhydrous iron oxide Fe2O3. In the USGS pigment sources used this week, hematite is the usual red iron-oxide mineral, and earthy hematite is a red ochre. It is not a hydrated yellow ochre until that hydration is taken into account as a different mineral. Near-miss: Not goethite or limonite, the hydrated oxides that give yellow, buff, or brown. Not an official iron percentage invented for a classroom lump. Not lapis lazuli. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Hematite is anhydrous Fe2O3 and is red in these sources. Limonite or goethite are hydrated iron oxides and give yellow, buff, or brown. 2. The formula names the pure mineral. A classroom lump is an earthy mixture; no official percentage of that lump is being claimed. 3. The color is identified from the mineral and from a USGS description of ochre, not from a store name alone.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 1: Netherfield, and the conversation

Check. 1. Bingley, a single man of large fortune from the north of England, in Mrs. Bennet’s report. She names “four or five thousand a year,” which is the novel’s figure, not an official salary. 2. Visit him as soon as he comes, because she is “thinking of his marrying one of them.” 3. “Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters.” He has “a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends,” heard of “these twenty years at least.”

Exercises. 1. “Four or five thousand a year.” “Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters.” 2. They are the novel’s words about a fictional household, not a description of anyone outside the book. 3. Do not identify a character with a living person, or with a historical person the chapter does not name.

Atelier · A small still-life block in the four colors, value first

Check. 1. A simple block, or two, under one lamp, painted with yellow ochre, an earth red, black, and white only. 2. Value first: light family, dark family, and a reflected-light note if one is visible; then a lean toward ochre or toward earth red, without losing the value. 3. Chasing hue first and losing the families, or turning the study into a portrait. Invent no official length of the block.

Exercises. 1. By matching lightness, as in yesterday’s ochre-and-earth-red value match, even though the hues differ. 2. The subject is a block study, to keep value inside the four paints. A likeness of a face is later work. 3. Lapis, ultramarine, a bright modern red, and a full-color portrait remain reserved.

Modern language · Spanish: the simple future, mixed short sentences

Check. 1. A fair trio uses visible infinitives and invented times or places, for example: Mañana hablaré. Mañana comeremos. Un día viviremos cerca del río. 2. Comeremos: mañana asks for the simple future, we will eat. 3. Vivo: ahora asks for the present, I live, or I reside, here. The place is invented.

Exercises. 1. Hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablaréis, hablarán. 2. I will eat; we will eat; they will eat. 3. Irregular futures such as haré and iré. Do not invent an official timetable, address, or appointment and present it as a fact.

Seminar · Economics: a sales tax on an invented price, a first look

Check. 1. The tax is $4. The total is $84, as 80 + 4 or as 80 × 1.05. 2. $76 treats the 5 percent as a discount, subtracting it from the price. Last term’s markup-then-discount was a different two-step change of an invented price, not a tax added to a price. 3. The rate and the price are invented classroom figures, not a published official rate of a real place, and not a form to file. 4. Model: sales tax (invented figures). Own words should match this idea: A sales tax, in this lesson, is a named percent of an invented price, added to that price to give a total. It is not a discount, and it is not an official living tax rate. Today’s invented figures: 5 percent of $80 is $4, and the total is $84. Near-miss: Not a 5 percent discount, which would leave $76. Not last term’s markup-then-discount. Not an official tax rate of a real place, and not a form to be filed. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Exercises. 1. Tax $3; total $63, as 60 + 3 or as 60 × 1.05. 2. Tax $8; total $88, as 80 + 8 or as 80 × 1.10. 3. Insurance and later macroeconomics remain reserved. The $80 is not an official price of a real good.
