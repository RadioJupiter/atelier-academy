Latin · Capitulum II, part 2: cuius? and the genitive in use

Check. 1. Whose? 2. With the thing owned (vīlla est mea). 3. Model: cuius. Own words should match this idea: The question word ‘whose?’, asking for a possessor. The adjective that answers (meus, mea, meum) agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner. Near-miss: Mea does not agree with the owner’s gender. That is the usual English mistake. possessive adjective. Own words should match this idea: An adjective such as meus, mea, meum (‘my’) that shows ownership and agrees with the noun it describes. Near-miss: It is not a genitive noun ending, and it does not stay in one form for every noun. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. puerī, puerī, puerōrum; virī, virī, virōrum; servī, servī, servōrum; fēminae, fēminae, fēminārum; oppida, oppidī, oppidōrum.
  2. (a) oppidum Italiae (b) fluviī Germāniae (c) insulae Graeciae (d) numerus puerōrum.
  3. XIV, XLIX, XCIX, CDXLIV, MLXVI, MMXXVI. XXIV = 24, LXXX = 80, MCMXC = 1990.

Mathematics · Algebra II: quadratic equations

Check. 1. (x − 1)² = 9, so x − 1 = ±3 and x = 4 or −2. Vieta: sum 4 + (−2) = 2; product 4 · (−2) = −8. ✓ 2. D = (−4)² − 4·1·5 = 16 − 20 = −4. D < 0, so there are no real roots. 3. Model: completing the square. Own words should match this idea: Rewriting x² + px as a square plus or minus a constant, so a quadratic can be solved or a parabola’s vertex read off. Near-miss: It is not the same as factoring by inspection, though a completed square can be checked with factors or with Vieta. discriminant. Own words should match this idea: The quantity that decides how many real roots a quadratic has: two if D > 0, one if D = 0, none if D < 0. Near-miss: The discriminant is not the same as the vertex, and a negative D does not mean ‘the equation is illegal’; it means the roots are not real. Vieta's formulas. Own words should match this idea: For the roots r and s of x² + px + q = 0, the sum of the roots is −p and the product is q. (Named after François Viète.) Near-miss: Vieta does not find the roots by itself if you do not already have them or an equivalent fact; it checks them and can build an equation from known roots. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (x + 3)² = 16 ⇒ x + 3 = ±4 ⇒ x = 1 or x = −7.
  2. x = (3 ± √(9 + 16))/4 = (3 ± 5)/4 ⇒ x = 2 or x = −1/2. Vieta on x² − 1.5x − 1 = 0: sum 1.5 ✓, product −1 ✓.
  3. D = k² − 36 = 0 ⇒ k = ±6.
  4. r + s = 5, rs = 3. r² + s² = 25 − 6 = 19; 1/r + 1/s = (r + s)/rs = 5/3.
  5. Sum 4, product (2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 3 = 1 ⇒ x² − 4x + 1 = 0.
  6. ac < 0 ⇒ −4ac > 0 ⇒ D = b² − 4ac > 0. ∎
  7. Let y = x²: y² − 5y + 4 = 0 ⇒ y = 1 or 4 ⇒ x = ±1, ±2.

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium I, grammar: the anatomy of a sentence

Check. 1. The subject and the finite verb of the main clause. 2. No: it is a relative (subordinate) clause. 3. Model: grammar. Own words should match this idea: In the trivium, the craft of accurate sentences: parts of speech, agreement, and punctuation that make the structure visible. Near-miss: Grammar here is not a list of errors to fear, and it is not ‘style’. It is how you mark what a sentence is doing. clause. Own words should match this idea: A group of words with a subject and a verb. A main clause can stand as a sentence; a subordinate clause cannot. Near-miss: A clause is not a phrase (a phrase has no finite verb), and it is not automatically a sentence. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. (a) S I, V bought, DO it. (b) S I, V thought, DO the writing, object complement excellent. (c) S The Mind, V sprouts up; dependent clause that lies fallow but a single Day modifies Mind.
  2. (a) compound; (b) complex; (c) complex (after the flame went out is a dependent clause); (d) simple.
  3. Compound: The candle went out, and the gas cooled, so the water rose. Complex: When the candle went out, the gas cooled and the water rose. Simple: After the flame's death, the cooling gas drew the water upward.

Physics · Free fall

Check. 1. Neither: both have g. 2. 10 m/s downward. 3. Model: free fall. Own words should match this idea: Motion under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. Near Earth, the acceleration has size g and is directed downward, the same for all objects. Near-miss: Free fall is not ‘only motion going down’: a ball thrown upward is still in free fall after it leaves the hand, with the same downward acceleration. gravity. Own words should match this idea: The attraction that gives a free-falling object its downward acceleration g near Earth. In these lessons g is taken as about 9.8 m/s², downward. Near-miss: g is not a force, and it is not the same as mass. A larger mass is pulled more strongly, but it also resists acceleration more, so a is the same. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. C. The acceleration does not depend on mass.
  2. C. Horizontal motion does not change vertical motion; both start with zero vertical velocity.
  3. B. Without air resistance, motion up and down is symmetric.
  4. t = √(2 · 20/9.8) ≈ 2.0 s; v = gt ≈ 20 m/s (19.8).
  5. h = v²/2g = 225/19.6 ≈ 11.5 m; t = 15/9.8 ≈ 1.5 s.
  6. t = √(0.30/9.8) ≈ 0.17 s. Typical results are 0.15–0.25 s.

History · Greece: Herodotus and the idea of "inquiry"

Check. 1. A chronicle lists what happened; history asks why. Seeking causes means weighing evidence and explaining, which is what historians still do. 2. Model: inquiry. Own words should match this idea: A systematic asking of questions in order to find out and to explain, not only to list events. Herodotus’s word historiē means inquiry. Near-miss: Inquiry is not the same as a chronicle (a list of what happened) and not the same as a royal inscription that commands and praises. historiē. Own words should match this idea: The Greek word (ἱστορίη) meaning inquiry, investigation. It is the source of the English word history. Near-miss: It does not yet mean ‘a school subject’ or ‘everything that happened’. In Herodotus it is the investigation itself. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. So that human deeds are not forgotten; so that great achievements of both Greeks and non-Greeks keep their fame; and, above all, so that the causes of the war are remembered.
  2. No: "works great and marvellous … some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians" gives both equal credit. (Barbaroi was not yet as pejorative as "barbarian" is in English.)
  3. The stele glorifies one ruler and commands; Herodotus writes as a private inquirer, explains causes, and gives credit to both sides. The stele is a primary source for Hammurabi's self-image; Herodotus is a secondary source for earlier events and a primary source for 5th-century Greek thought.
  4. A chronicle lists what happened; history asks why. Seeking causes means weighing evidence and explaining, which is what historians still do.

Atelier · Bargue plate: modeling and completion

Check. 1. The big light and the big shadow. 2. The edge where the form turns from light into shadow.

The finished copy should match the plate's value pattern when both are viewed squinting from the marked spot. Typical beginner faults: shadows too dark and too varied, light side over-modeled, outlines left as hard lines where the plate has none. One Bargue plate commonly takes students many hours; finishing in a week is an achievement. Do not rush accuracy for speed. 3. Model: modeling. Own words should match this idea: Using value to turn a shape so that it reads as a form in light, not as a flat cut-out. Near-miss: Modeling is not coloring in, and it is not adding outlines. If the values are wrong, more line will not fix the form. form. Own words should match this idea: The three-dimensional shape of the subject, as it is turned by light and as it must read on a flat page. Near-miss: Form is not the same as outline, and it is not ‘style’. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Modern language · Spanish: present tense of -ar verbs

Check. 1. You (tú) speak. 2. dibujan. 3. Model: -ar verb. Own words should match this idea: A Spanish verb whose infinitive ends in -ar. In the present tense it follows a regular pattern of endings once the -ar is removed. Near-miss: The infinitive hablar is not a present-tense form, and hablarse is not needed for these first sentences. infinitive. Own words should match this idea: The dictionary form of the verb (in Spanish, the form ending in -ar, -er, or -ir), naming the action without a subject. Near-miss: The infinitive is not the same as the yo form, even when they look close. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  2. (a) Estudiamos latín. (b) ¿Pintas? (c) No trabajan los domingos. (d) Escucho música. (e) Necesita un lápiz.
  3. Check the endings against the person of each verb.

Elective · Artistic anatomy 1: the skeleton under the skin

Check. 1. About 1/2. Beginners usually place the eyes too high (too close to the top of the head). 2. Model: subcutaneous landmark. Own words should match this idea: A place where bone lies just under the skin and does not change shape with fat or muscle, so it is a reliable measuring point in figure drawing. Near-miss: A landmark is not a soft tissue bulge that moves when the person gains or loses weight, and it is not a guessed ‘middle of the arm’. clavicle. Own words should match this idea: The collarbone: a subcutaneous landmark running from the sternum to the point of the shoulder (near the acromion). Near-miss: The clavicle is not the jaw, and the acromion is not the same as the whole clavicle: it is the bony tip of the shoulder blade that meets it. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Check positions against an anatomy reference (see SOURCES.md for recommended books).
  2. Close to 1/2 for most adults.
  3. They show the direction of the foot and the axis of the ankle: the inner malleolus is higher than the outer, so the line joining them slopes down toward the outside.

Vocabulary review

Check. 1. The remainder is what is left after a part has been taken, and the next fraction is of that leftover, not of the original whole. A simple two-part whole is just two parts that add to the total, with no second cut of what was left. 2. Velocity is displacement over time and has a direction; speed is distance over time and does not. A lap that returns to the start has speed > 0 and average velocity 0. 3. An axiom is a starting statement accepted without proof; a theorem is a statement proved from axioms, definitions, and earlier theorems. 'It looks right in the figure' is neither. 4. An epithet is a fixed, reusable descriptive phrase ('swift-footed Achilles'). A one-off metaphor is invented for a single moment and is not a standing name. 5. Ser for identity, origin, characteristics, and time: La ciudad es antigua. Estar for place or a temporary condition: El libro está en la mesa / Estamos cansados. 6. Sight-size sets the drawing so that, from a fixed viewing point, the drawing and the subject appear the same size, and lengths can be compared at arm's length. If the viewing point moves, yesterday's proportions will not match.
