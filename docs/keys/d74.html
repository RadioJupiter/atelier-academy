Latin · Capitulum XIII: counting inclusively backward

Check. 1. prīdiē Īdūs Septembrēs is 12 September (the day immediately before the 13th). ante diem tertium Īdūs Septembrēs is 11 September (13 + 1 − 3 = 11), not 10 September. Inclusive counting, not an English subtraction of 3 from 13, is the method. 2. Add one to the numbered day on which the Nones or Ides fall, then subtract the Roman day-number in the date. The named day is counted as one of the days. 3. Model: inclusive counting in Roman dates. Own words should match this idea: Days other than the named day and the day before it are counted backward from the next Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs, and the starting point is counted as one of the days. Near-miss: Not ordinary English “four days before,” which would not count the Ides themselves as day 1, and not prīdiē, which is only the day immediately before. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The Ides of April are the 13th (April is not one of the four later months). 13 + 1 − 5 = 9 April.
  2. A model: Ante diem quartum Īdūs Septembrēs puer in agrīs labōrat. Other original sentences are acceptable if the date is formed correctly.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXIII: an isosceles triangle, its height, and its area

Check. 1. The base splits into 5 and 5. 5² + h² = 13², so 25 + h² = 169, h² = 144, h = 12. A = ½ × 10 × 12 = 60. 2. The two sides are equal, so the two right triangles formed by the altitude are congruent; the base is therefore split into equal parts. (The perpendicular is part of the same altitude.) 3. Model: height of an isosceles triangle. Own words should match this idea: In an isosceles triangle the altitude to the base meets the base at its midpoint and is perpendicular to it. That altitude can be found by the Pythagorean theorem, and the area is then ½ × base × that height. Near-miss: Not treating a slanted equal side as the height, and not splitting the base into unequal parts when the two sides are equal. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. The base splits into 4 and 4. 4² + h² = 5², so 16 + h² = 25, h² = 9, h = 3. A = ½ × 8 × 3 = 12.
  2. 5² + 5² = 50, which is not 6² = 36, so the triangle itself is not right-angled. The right angle is at D, where the altitude meets the base, inside a smaller triangle.

Writing & Rhetoric · Ad hominem

Check. 1. It accepts a claim because of a trait of the person (being liked), instead of testing the argument. Ad hominem can run in either direction: reject or accept. 2. A model: “5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13², so the check is right,” or an equivalent arithmetic answer. 3. Model: ad hominem. Own words should match this idea: A fallacy in which a claim is rejected (or accepted) because of a trait of the person who offers it, instead of an answer to the claim or to the reasons given for it. Near-miss: Not every mention of a person is this fallacy. A question about whether a witness was in a position to see an event can be a relevant challenge to testimony. The flaw is using a personal trait as if it settled a claim that needs a different kind of test. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model (other honest inventions accepted). Ad hominem: “You only learned that yesterday, so it cannot be fourth declension.” Sound: “The genitive plural Īduum is the fourth-declension form, as in this week’s lesson.”
  2. A straw man distorts the claim and answers the distortion. Ad hominem leaves the claim untested and points at the person instead. They can be combined, but they are different flaws.

Science · Water: dilute and concentrated solutions

Check. 1. When the sum of the mole fractions of the solutes is small compared with unity. 2. “More dilute” here compares how much solute is present in a given amount of solution for the same solute and solvent. A different pair of substances is a different comparison. 3. Model: dilute solution. Own words should match this idea: A solution in which the sum of the mole fractions of the solutes is small compared with unity, in the IUPAC Gold Book’s wording; in school language, a solution with relatively little solute in a given amount of solution. Near-miss: Not “containing no solute,” which would not be this week’s solution, and not a named official percentage. Saturated solutions remain reserved. concentrated solution. Own words should match this idea: In this week’s school language, a solution with relatively much solute in a given amount of solution, compared with a more dilute one. No official percentage or molarity is named. Near-miss: Not a synonym of “saturated,” which remains reserved, and not an official bank of percentages. “Concentrated” here is comparative: more solute in a given amount of solution than another solution of the same substances. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Plain water, treated as one substance, is not a salt solution. A solution, on the Gold Book definition used yesterday, contains more than one substance, with a solute and a solvent. (A later, more technical treatment of very dilute limits is not begun here.)
  2. No. Last week’s IAPWS table was for ordinary water, and this lesson invents no official surface-tension number for a salt solution.

Literature · Inferno I, lines 112–136: “I will be thy guide”

Check. 1. He will hear “the desperate lamentations,” and see “the ancient spirits disconsolate, / Who cry out each one for the second death,” and also “those who contented are / Within the fire, because they hope to come, / Whene’er it may be, to the blessed people.” 2. “A soul shall be for that than I more worthy,” left at Virgilius’s departure, because “that Emperor, who reigns above, / In that I was rebellious to his law, / Wills that through me none come into his city.” 3. Model: Virgilius as guide (Longfellow, Inferno I). Own words should match this idea: In Longfellow’s Inferno I, lines 112–136, Virgilius offers to be the speaker’s guide “through the eternal place,” and at the end “he moved on, and I behind him followed.” Near-miss: Not a claim that Virgilius will also lead the speaker into “his city” above: the same speech says that “through me none come into his city,” and that a soul “more worthy” will be left for that ascent. Those later stages are not begun as a new canticle today. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. “Poet, I thee entreat, / By that same God whom thou didst never know, / So that I may escape this woe and worse, / Thou wouldst conduct me there where thou hast said, / That I may see the portal of Saint Peter.” (The following line, “And those thou makest so disconsolate,” may be included.)
  2. Tuesday’s set lines used “honor.” Today’s set lines do not make that word the quotation test; “Whene’er,” “thy guide,” and “Saint Peter” are the wording to keep here.

Atelier · Value on the mouth and the ear

Check. 1. That turn or groove often goes darker than the two masses themselves. 2. You must not move them or restart their value from a second invented light. The pass is one light. 3. Model: value on the mouth and the ear. Own words should match this idea: The already-placed mouth, as two masses, and the already-placed ear, on the side plane of the head, receive value from the same light: the groove or turn between the two masses of the mouth is often darker than the masses themselves; the ear, set back on the side plane, is often in half-tone rather than in the full light of the front of the face. Near-miss: Not a new placement of mouth or ear, not a line-drawing of every tooth or every fold of the ear, and not a full-light ear pasted onto a side plane that is itself in half-tone. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It breaks the light: a side plane that has turned away should not carry a feature that is treated as if it faced the light fully.
  2. A critique of the whole value pass across the features already placed, not a new medium and not a new placement.

Elective · Economics: the amount after simple interest

Check. 1. I = 400 × 0.03 × 2 = 24. A = 400 + 24 = 424. 2. I = 260 − 200 = 60. Then 200 × r × 3 = 60, so r = 60 / 600 = 0.10 per year, still an invented rate that matches those invented figures. 3. Model: amount after simple interest. Own words should match this idea: The amount A after simple interest is the original principal plus the interest: A = P + I, where I = P × r × t, on the original principal only. Near-miss: Not compound interest, already taught and not retaught as new, and not an official rate at a named bank. Classroom rates remain invented and labeled invented. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It is not an official rate at a named bank. Official rates are not invented.
  2. A = P + (P × r × t), or A = P(1 + r t).
