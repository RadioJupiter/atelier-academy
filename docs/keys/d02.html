Latin · Capitulum I, part 2

Check. 1. magnus (masculine). 2. magna (feminine). 3. Model: agreement. Own words should match this idea: The rule that an adjective takes the same gender and number (and, later, the same case) as the noun it describes. Near-miss: Agreement is not ‘the adjective that sounds nicest’, and a masculine ending is not a default you can use for every noun. gender (Latin). Own words should match this idea: The class of a Latin noun — masculine, feminine, or neuter — which adjectives and some pronouns must follow. You cannot always guess it from the meaning. Near-miss: Grammatical gender is not the same as the sex of a person or animal, and it is not optional. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. magnus; parva; parvum; magnī; parva.
  2. (a) Nīlus fluvius est (or fluvius magnus). (b) Corsica insula est. (c) Tusculum nōn est oppidum magnum, sed parvum. (d) Aegyptus in Āfricā est.
  3. (a) insulae sunt; (b) fluvius magnus; (c) oppida parva.

Mathematics · Geometry I: definitions, axioms and the first proofs

Check. 1. The vertical angle is 70° (vertical angles are equal). An adjacent angle on the straight line is 110° (adjacent angles on a straight line are supplementary). 2. 3x + (x + 20°) = 180°, so 4x = 160°, x = 40. The angles are 120° and 60°. 3. Model: axiom. Own words should match this idea: A starting statement accepted without proof, so that theorems can be proved from it. Near-miss: An axiom is not a theorem, and it is not ‘anything that looks obvious in a drawing’. theorem. Own words should match this idea: A statement that is proved from definitions, axioms, and earlier theorems. Near-miss: A theorem is not an axiom, and ‘it looks right in the figure’ is not a proof of one. vertical angles. Own words should match this idea: A pair of angles whose sides are the extensions of each other’s sides: the opposite angles formed when two lines meet. Near-miss: Vertical angles are not simply ‘any two equal angles’, and they are not the adjacent angles on a straight line. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Two different angles cannot be vertical (vertical angles are equal), so they are adjacent: x + (x + 40°) = 180°, x = 70°. 70°, 110°, 70°, 110°.
  2. Let the angles be α and β with α + β = 180°. Each bisector makes α/2 and β/2 with the common side, so the angle between the bisectors is α/2 + β/2 = 90°. ∎
  3. Let ∠AOB and ∠COD be vertical, each equal to α (OA opposite OC, OB opposite OD). Let OM bisect ∠AOB and ON bisect ∠COD. Then ∠MOB = α/2, ∠BOC = 180° − α (supplementary to ∠AOB), ∠CON = α/2. Sum: α/2 + (180° − α) + α/2 = 180°, so OM and ON form a straight angle: M, O, N are collinear. ∎
  4. Six rays leave the point, forming C(6,2) = 15 pairs of rays; 3 pairs are opposite rays (straight angles), leaving 12 angles. Each has exactly one vertical partner, so there are 6 pairs.
  5. Converse: "Equal angles are vertical." False: e.g., two separate right angles in different places are equal but not vertical.
  6. If ∠A = ∠B, then 180° − ∠A = 180° − ∠B. ∎

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: copywork and copia

Check. 1. Copy exactly first. 2. The skill of saying the same idea in many accurate ways. 3. Model: imitatio. Own words should match this idea: The Renaissance school practice of first copying an excellent passage exactly, noticing how it is built, then writing a new version that keeps the structure and changes the content. Near-miss: Imitatio is not plagiarism for display, and it is not ‘write whatever you like first’. The copy comes before the variation. copia. Own words should match this idea: Plenty of expression: many accurate ways of saying the same idea, produced on purpose. Near-miss: Copia is not padding, and it is not a list of synonyms that do not actually fit the sentence. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Target: zero errors. Common slips are modernising try'd and compleat, and dropping the commas around "and, making short hints … ".
  2. (a) The short sentences move fast, like rapid events; the long sentence slows down to explain a careful process. (b) took the papers → made hints → laid them by → tried to complete them without the book → (by) expressing each sentiment fully. Clarity comes from chronological order, parallel participles (making, looking, expressing) and commas that mark each step.
  3. Check that each slot is filled and the sentence stays grammatical. Model: With this view I took six of the master drawings, and, tracing only the main angles of each, put them away for a week, and then, without looking at the originals, tried to redraw them, by rebuilding every proportion from those few lines.
  4. Examples: Your letter delighted me. · I was greatly pleased by your letter. · Nothing could have pleased me more than your letter. · How your letter cheered me! · Your letter brought me real joy. · Reading your letter was a pleasure. · Your letter arrived and my day brightened. · What pleasure your letter gave me! · Your letter was a gift. · I cannot say how much your letter pleased me. Check that all ten keep the same meaning.

Literature · Homer, Iliad, Book 1

Check. 1. Model points: Apollo answers Chryses' prayer with the plague; Athena stops Achilles from drawing his sword; Thetis petitions Zeus; the gods quarrel on Olympus, mirroring the human quarrel. Two accurate examples are enough. 2. Model: invocation. Own words should match this idea: The opening move of an epic in which the poet asks the Muse to sing, and names the subject of the poem. Near-miss: An invocation is not a plot summary of the whole war, and it is not the poet speaking only in his own person with no Muse. in medias res. Own words should match this idea: ‘Into the middle of things’: the story begins not at the first event, but in the midst of an already ongoing action. Near-miss: It is not ‘a flashback’, though flashbacks may follow, and it is not a failure to start at the beginning. epithet. Own words should match this idea: A fixed descriptive phrase attached to a name, used and reused in oral poetry (‘swift-footed Achilles’, ‘rosy-fingered Dawn’). Near-miss: An epithet is not a one-off metaphor invented for a single line, and it is not automatically ‘a personal insult’ (that is a later English sense). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Model points (your answer should use your own words and line references): 1. The wrath (anger) of Achilles. Agamemnon: he dishonors a priest, causes the plague, and takes Briseis to save face. Achilles: he nearly draws his sword on his commander, and asks for his own army to suffer. Atelier · Bargue plate: the block-in

Check. 1. To place the largest shapes and proportions in light line. 2. The greatest height and the greatest width.

The plate itself is the key: compare in sight-size from the marked spot, then in the mirror. Standard for a beginning block-in: overall height-to-width ratio within 3%, and every major alignment that is vertical on the plate is vertical on yours. Do not shade until the block-in passes this check. 3. Model: block-in. Own words should match this idea: The first stage of a Bargue copy: the largest shapes, the big angles, and the main proportions, all in light line, with no features yet. Near-miss: A block-in is not a finished outline, and it is not the time for eyes, lips, or shading. proportion. Own words should match this idea: The relation of one length or shape to another, and to the whole, as seen from the viewing point. Near-miss: Proportion is not ‘making the drawing pretty’, and it is not the same as value (light and dark). The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

Modern language · Spanish: nouns, gender and articles

Check. 1. la (la ciudad). 2. The pencil. 3. Model: gender (Spanish). Own words should match this idea: The class of a Spanish noun, shown by the article: el with many masculine nouns, la with many feminine nouns. The article must match. Near-miss: Gender is not always predictable from the meaning (el lápiz is masculine), and you should not learn a noun as a bare word without its article. article. Own words should match this idea: The small word placed before a noun (el, la; later un, una) that must agree with the noun’s gender and number. Near-miss: The article is not optional in these first sentences, and el is not a universal default. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. el libro, la mesa, el día, la canción, el mapa, la ciudad, la mano, el problema, la ventana, el cuaderno.
  2. los lápices, las ciudades, los papeles, las canciones, los días. (canción → canciones loses the written accent, because the stress now falls on the second-to-last syllable by rule.)
  3. un lápiz; unas ventanas; los mapas; una canción; la mano.

Music theory · Pitch, staff and clefs

Check. 1. E, B, F, A, E. 2. Model: pitch. Own words should match this idea: How high or low a note sounds: the perceived frequency of the sound. Near-miss: Pitch is not the same as volume (how loud the note is), and it is not the name of a clef. clef. Own words should match this idea: The sign at the start of a staff that fixes which line is a named pitch: the treble (G) clef or the bass (F) clef in this lesson. Near-miss: A clef is not a note, and the bass clef’s two dots do not sit around the same line as the treble clef’s curl. staff. Own words should match this idea: The five lines and four spaces on which notes are written. Near-miss: A staff is not a scale, and it is not a bar in a bar model. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. E, B, F, A, E.
  2. G, F, A, E.
  3. half, whole, half, whole, whole.
  4. D → E (whole step) → F (half step).
