Latin · Capitulum XIII: the year and the months

Check. 1. A model: Annus duodecim mēnsēs habet. Other original sentences are acceptable if both nouns are used correctly. 2. Iānuārius, Februārius, Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September, October, November, December. 3. Model: mēnsis. Own words should match this idea: The Latin word for a month. It is a third-declension noun (mēnsis, mēnsis, masculine). Near-miss: Not a day (diēs), and not the year (annus). Mēnsis names the month as a span, not a single named day of it. the Latin month names. Own words should match this idea: The twelve month names in Latin: Iānuārius, Februārius, Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September, October, November, December. Near-miss: Not the English names used as if they were Latin, and not a claim that Iūlius and Augustus were always the names of those months. Allen and Greenough §630 notes that Quīntīlis was renamed Iūlius, and Sextīlis was renamed Augustus. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Mārtius, Maius, Iūlius, and October (March, May, July, and October). The later placement of Nonae and Īdūs in those four months is taught tomorrow; this item only names the four.
  2. The same section says Quīntīlis received the name Iūlius, and Sextīlis was called Augustus. The names were not always Iūlius and Augustus.

Mathematics · Geometry XXX: the area of a triangle

Check. 1. A = ½ × 10 × 6 = 30. 2. The rectangle has area 36, so each triangle has area 18. 3. Model: area of a triangle. Own words should match this idea: The number of unit squares a triangle covers. A triangle is half of a rectangle that has the same base and the same perpendicular height, so its area is one-half of base times height: A = ½bh, where h is perpendicular to the base. Near-miss: Not one-half of base times a slanted side. The height must meet the base at a right angle. The area is also not the perimeter. perpendicular height. Own words should match this idea: The distance from a vertex of a triangle to the line of the opposite side, measured along a line that meets that side at a right angle. Near-miss: Not a slanted side from C to A or C to B, even if that side is labeled with a length. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A = ½ × 7 × 8 = 28.
  2. The square marks a right angle, so that length is the perpendicular height, not a slanted side.

Writing & Rhetoric · Informal fallacies: a first look

Check. 1. A slip in arithmetic can make a claim false without being one of this week’s named patterns of bad reasoning (though it is still a mistake). Today’s fallacy is a pattern in how reasons are offered, not every error. 2. A warrant is the step that shows why the evidence is supposed to support the claim. A fallacy often offers a reason that does not actually do that job. 3. Model: fallacy. Own words should match this idea: A flaw in the reasoning of an argument: the support does not actually establish the claim in the way the argument pretends, even if some statement in it happens to be true, or happens to be false for another reason. Near-miss: Not merely a false claim, and not a typo or a wrong calculation by itself. A true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacious route, and a false claim can be false without this week’s named patterns. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model (other honest inventions accepted): “9-12-15 is right-angled because the sides are multiples of 3.” Being multiples of 3 is not the test; the squares are (81 + 144 = 225). The conclusion is true; the reason given is not the one that establishes it.
  2. A précis is a faithful short restatement of a passage, not an argument offered as a proof. It can be inaccurate, but that is not the pattern being taught today.

Physics · Stable, unstable, and neutral equilibrium

Check. 1. Neutral equilibrium. It does not return to the old place, so it is not stable; it also does not run away down a slope. 2. Not tipping is last week’s test about the base of support. Today’s three names classify what a small displacement does. A block that does not tip may still need that further question (for example, a wide block sitting on a level table is in a stable kind of rest against tipping, but the lesson does not collapse the two weeks into one word). 3. Model: stable equilibrium. Own words should match this idea: A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a restoring effect: the object tends to return to that position. Near-miss: Not merely “not moving.” An object can also rest in an unstable or a neutral position. Last week’s “does not tip” is related, but today’s names are about what a small displacement does. unstable equilibrium. Own words should match this idea: A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a further move away: the object does not return, and it tends to leave that position. Near-miss: Not simply “moving.” At the exact top the marble can be at rest; the position is unstable because a small nudge ends the rest. neutral equilibrium. Own words should match this idea: A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a new rest nearby, without a tendency to return or to run away. Near-miss: Not stable (it does not return to the old place) and not unstable (it does not run away down a slope). Flat and level is part of this invented picture; a slanted table is a different case. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. No official steepness is given; the demonstrations are invented. You can still name the rest at the bottom as stable.
  2. A small roll moves the center of mass’s vertical line off the narrow “base” of the exact top, and the marble goes down the hill. The line-and-base idea is used, not retaught as a first look.

History · Anthemius of Tralles and Isidorus of Miletus

Check. 1. “The most learned man in the skilled craft which is known as the art of building,” or a short faithful paraphrase of that phrase. 2. Those sections describe the finished church’s height, proportion, and light, which is Wednesday’s set job, not today’s. 3. Model: Anthemius of Tralles. Own words should match this idea: The master-builder named by Procopius in Buildings I.1.24, in Dewing’s translation: “the most learned man in the skilled craft which is known as the art of building,” who regulated the artisans’ tasks and prepared designs of the future construction. Near-miss: Not Isidorus of Miletus, who is associated with him in the same section as “another master-builder,” and not a modern architect’s name substituted for Dewing’s wording. Isidorus of Miletus. Own words should match this idea: The second master-builder named by Procopius in Buildings I.1.24, in Dewing: “another master-builder, Isidorus by name, a Milesian by birth, a man who was intelligent and worthy to assist the Emperor Justinian.” Near-miss: Not Anthemius of Tralles, and not a later Isidorus the Younger from an editor’s note about a later dome, which is not in today’s set lines. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. It quotes the wording as it stands, and does not tidy it as if Dewing had written a different sentence.
  2. Last week ended with the church fired and then rebuilt “so finely shaped.” Today names the builders Procopius gives for that construction, still in the same chapter.

Atelier · A value pass across the features already placed

Check. 1. The socket is a recess; its plane turns away from a light from above, so it takes a darker value than the forehead, which faces the light more directly. 2. It marks where a form turns out of the light, so the values on either side of it stay distinct instead of being smeared into one tone. 3. Model: a value pass across the features. Own words should match this idea: A sitting that assigns light, mid-tone, and dark to the features already placed (sockets, nose, mouth, ear), according to how each plane faces the light, without moving those features to a new place. Near-miss: Not a new placement of the features, not a first value scale as if weeks 5–8 had not happened, and not a change of medium. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. A model: light on the front plane of the nose; mid-tone on a side plane of the cheek or of the ear; dark in the well of the socket. Other honest assignments accepted if they follow the planes and the light.
  2. Weeks 5–8 already taught light on forms and a value pass on the head. This week applies that work to last week’s features.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of ser and ir

Check. 1. A model: Ayer fue un día largo (ser). Ayer fue a la escuela (ir). Other short correct sentences accepted. 2. Hablé is the regular -ar yo preterite of hablar, already taught. Ser and ir do not take that ending in this tense. 3. Model: the preterite of ser and ir. Own words should match this idea: The verbs ser and ir share one preterite set: fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fuisteis, fueron. The forms are the same; the meaning (to be, or to go) is told by the sentence. Near-miss: Not the regular -ar yo form hablé, and not a claim that ser and ir share their present-tense forms as well. Only this preterite set is shared. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. fuimos; fuisteis.
  2. Each heard form used in a short preterite sentence, with ser or ir labeled. Model: Fui (ir) a la biblioteca; fuiste (ser) el primero; fueron (ir) al parque.

Elective · Logic: Ferio, a first-figure EIO syllogism

Check. 1. EIO. No M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P. 2. Darii’s conclusion is particular affirmative (some S are P). Ferio’s is particular negative (some S are not P). The majors differ too: Darii’s is “all M are P”; Ferio’s is “no M are P.” 3. Model: Ferio. Own words should match this idea: A valid first-figure syllogism of the form EIO: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P. The major premise is universal negative, the minor particular affirmative, and the conclusion particular negative. Near-miss: Not Darii (AII), already taught, and not Celarent (EAE), already taught. Ferio’s conclusion stays particular and negative: “some S are not P,” not “no S are P,” which the particular minor does not give. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

  1. Ferio’s major is universal negative (no M are P), not a particular affirmative major. The first-figure major in this set is universal.
  2. Honest invented examples accepted if they are EIO in the first figure, with M the middle term in both premises.
