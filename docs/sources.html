Weeks 3–4 (additional sources)

  • Kiselev, A. P. Planimetry (constructions, parallelograms, circles: chords and tangents). The course’s construction sequence follows this tradition; the trapezoid is defined here as a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides.
  • Lincoln, A. Gettysburg Address, 19 November 1863 (public domain). Used as an imitatio model in writing, week 3.
  • Johnson, S. The History of Rasselas, Prince of Abissinia (1759), chapter 1 (public domain). Used with Franklin’s hints in writing, week 3.
  • Treaty of year 21 of Ramesses II with Ḫattušili III: Akkadian tablet tradition from Hattusa (e.g. KBo 1.7 / CTH 91) and the hieroglyphic text at Karnak (year 21). The date is cited as year 21 of Ramesses II and as conventionally mid-13th century BCE, not as an exact year such as 1269 BCE. The object displayed at the United Nations is a replica.
  • Pyramid Texts of Unas, Saqqara: a class of Old Kingdom funerary text, named as a source for kingship and afterlife language; particular spells are not quoted as if from a classroom edition that has not been opened in the lesson.
  • Arrian, Anabasis of Alexander, trans. E. J. Chinnock (1884), public domain. Alexander’s dates in the course are 336–323 BCE (campaign from 334; Issus 333; Gaugamela 331; death at Babylon 323). Arrian is a late source and is treated as such.
  • Oil-paint drying: a drying oil film hardens by autoxidation and then polymerization (crosslinking), not mainly by evaporation of the oil. A drying oil film may gain mass as it takes up oxygen; a watercolor wash loses mass as water evaporates. Lead driers are not used in the studio exercises. (Standard conservation / drying-oil chemistry; no invented percentages.)
  • Homer, Iliad, Books 7–24, as in weeks 1–2: Butler (1898), Project Gutenberg #2199, is a public-domain option; verse translations remain recommended for reading. Quotes in the lessons, when used, are short and attributed.
  • Shakespeare, Sonnets 18 and 73 (public domain), for caesura, enjambment, and the volta.
  • Evans’s name ‘Minoan’ and the restorations at Knossos (including work associated with the Gilliéron workshop) are treated as modern layers on Bronze Age remains, not as photographs of the second millennium BCE.
  • Kritios Boy: Acropolis, Athens, early fifth century BCE, used as the type example of Early Classical contrapposto in this course. The name records a traditional attribution.
  • Surface gravity and lunar periods, when used in cross-site work or in later checks: NASA Moon fact sheet (surface gravity 1.62 m/s² against Earth 9.80 m/s², ratio about 0.165, about 1/6; synodic month 29.53 days; sidereal month 27.3217 days); NASA Mars fact sheet (mean surface gravity 3.73 m/s² against Earth 9.82 m/s², ratio about 0.380).

Autumn color: unmasking of carotenoids as chlorophyll breaks down, with a caution that reds and purples in many species involve anthocyanins made in the season, not only unmasked pigments.   • United States Declaration of Independence (1776), public domain, used as a model of form (parallelism, imitatio, Franklin hints, précis), not as a political assignment.
  • Homer, Odyssey, Books 1–12: Butler (1898), Project Gutenberg #2199, as a public-domain option for short, attributed quotation; verse translations remain recommended for reading.
  • Early Rome and the Republic: Livy is treated as late (late first century BCE–early first century CE). Traditional dates such as 753 BCE and 509 BCE are labeled traditional. The Twelve Tables are treated as a mid-fifth-century tradition that survives in later fragments, not as a complete original text. Polybius (second century BCE) is used as an analyst of mid-Republican government and as a source for the Punic Wars. Standard chronology for those wars: 264–241, 218–201 (Cannae 216, Zama 202), 149–146 BCE, with the destruction of Carthage in 146. Caesar’s Commentaries are tagged as participant sources and as advocacy; Cicero’s letters as primary and from a position.
  • Parthenon: conventional construction dates about 447–432 BCE, used as a conventional scholarly range. Doryphoros of Polykleitos: a fifth-century BCE bronze type known from later marble copies; no invented canon measurements. Nike of Samothrace: usually dated to the second century BCE (often around the early second century); fragmentary; Louvre display is not the original setting. The Vatican Laocoön: date and original-or-copy status are debated and are not settled in the lesson.
  • William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794), public domain, for imagery and metaphor.

Weeks 7 (additional sources)

  • Functions and function notation; graphs of y = mx + b; quadratic graphs, with the vertex from completing the square or from x = −b/(2a). π is not used as a new claim here; loci are the circle (points at a fixed distance from a center) and the perpendicular bisector (PA = PB), with Euclid III.31 named only as a contrast (the circle on diameter AB is a different locus).
  • Francis Bacon, “Of Studies” (1625), public domain. The course uses one short attributed sentence as a model of form: “Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability.” No longer extract is quoted on the lesson pages.
  • Sophocles, Antigone: a public-domain English translation (for example Jebb 1891, or another named public-domain version) may be used in class for short, attributed quotation. The week 7 pages do not lift a long passage and do not tell the end of the play.
  • Power and the watt as an SI derived unit (1 W = 1 J/s): BIPM, The International System of Units (SI Brochure). The unit is named after James Watt. Torque, in a first look, is force times perpendicular distance, named in newton-meters and not called a joule.
  • Seeing: a first anatomical map (cornea, pupil, lens, retina, optic nerve, blind spot) without invented focal lengths, travel times, or receptor counts. Etymologies used: cornea from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn”; retina from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.”
  • Actium, 31 BCE; the settlement of 27 BCE as a conventional start of Augustus’s principate, not as a single “fall of the Republic” day. The Res Gestae Divi Augusti is treated as a first-person record (known from later copies, notably at Ankara) and as advocacy. Tacitus and Suetonius are dated to the early second century CE, about a century after Augustus’s death (14 CE).
  • Augustus of Prima Porta: a marble statue in the Vatican Museums, generally treated as a copy of a lost bronze type; no single year is assigned to the copy in the lesson. Ara Pacis Augustae: conventional dates 13 BCE (voted) and 9 BCE (dedicated), labeled conventional.
  • Latin grammar for Capitulum IX follows a public syllabus (third declension; declining ūnus, duo, trēs; invariable higher numerals as taught; quot; a large number with the genitive; cēterī). Practice sentences are original. Ørberg’s story and companion-book examples are not copied.
  • Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins were omitted when the etymology section was missing, incomplete, ambiguous, or the first sense was the wrong word (function, parabola, budget, and torque were omitted for those reasons).

Week 8 (additional sources)

  • Euclid, Elements, Book III, Proposition 32 (the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment). The course’s proof uses III.20 (inscribed angle half the central angle on the same arc) and the Day 19 fact that a radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent, together with the triangle angle sum. Public-domain translations (for example Heath) may be used; the course uses the theorems, not a copyrighted commentary.
  • Vieta’s formulas for a quadratic are taught from the expansion of (x − r)(x − s), and named after François Viète (Vieta). The inverse of a linear function f(x) = mx + b (m ≠ 0) is f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m, checked by composition; the graph as a reflection in y = x is a first look. This “inverse” is distinguished from the logic inverse of a conditional (already taught) and from the reciprocal 1/f(x).
  • Newton’s law of gravitation, F = G Mm / r², as a first look, with the inverse-square pattern. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is used as a standard value of the constant, not as a classroom measurement (BIPM / standard CODATA-style school rounding). g = 9.8 N/kg near the Earth remains the first-model gravitational field. Lunar g is given as about one sixth of Earth’s, labeled as a commonly taught approximate first figure, not as a classroom measurement. Newton’s sideways-throw / mountain thought-picture is used qualitatively (a picture associated with his System of the World); no invented orbital speed; orbits are not all circles.
  • Accommodation of the lens, and two slightly different views as a first cue for judging distance, without invented focal lengths, diopter numbers, or interocular measurements.
  • Augustus’s death and the succession of Tiberius, 14 CE; a conventional Julio-Claudian list (Augustus, Tiberius 14–37, Gaius/Caligula 37–41, Claudius 41–54, Nero 54–68). Later writers (Tacitus, Suetonius, early second century CE) remain later; anecdotes are not treated as simple facts. 14 CE is a succession, not a second founding; 68 CE is the end of this line and a year of civil war in the conventional narrative, not a “fall of the Republic” day.
  • The Pantheon in Rome: the fabric we have (rotunda, concrete dome, oculus) generally treated as a Hadrianic rebuild of the early second century CE, with a conventional range around 118–125 CE used here and labeled conventional. The Agrippa inscription is treated as belonging to an earlier Agrippan temple, conventionally associated with 27 BCE, and is not used to date the present dome. No invented measurements of the dome.
  • Sophocles, Antigone, short attributed quotation from R. C. Jebb’s translation in The Tragedies of Sophocles (Cambridge, 1917), public domain, via Wikisource. Opening: “Ismene, sister, mine own dear sister, knowest thou what ill there is, of all bequeathed by Oedipus, that Zeus fulfils not for us twain while we live?” Close: “Wisdom is the supreme part of happiness; and reverence towards the gods must be inviolate. Great words of prideful men are ever punished with great blows, and, in old age, teach the chastened to be wise.”
  • Percy Bysshe Shelley, “Ozymandias,” first published 1818, public domain, short attributed extracts only (“I met a traveller from an antique land…”; “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”).
  • Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins used include tangent (Latin tangēns, “touching”), inverse (Latin inversus / invertere), infinitive (Late Latin īnfīnītīvus, from īnfīnītus), octave (Latin octavus, “eighth”), polygon (Greek polus + gōnia), Pantheon (Greek Pantheion, “temple of all gods”), and weight (Old English wiht / ġewiht, related to weigh). Origins were omitted when the etymology section was tree-only, incomplete, or the wrong sense (gravity, gravitation, mass, accommodation, refraction).

Week 9 (additional sources)

  • Composition of functions is taught as (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), with order mattering, and then as composition with an inverse: (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x = (f⁻¹ ∘ f)(x), the identity. The inverse of a linear function remains as already taught, and is still distinguished from the logic inverse. The equation of a circle, (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², is taught as the coordinate form of the already-taught locus. A line and a circle are solved as a system by substitution; a line may meet a circle twice, once (a tangent), or not at all.
  • Etymology of composition (the English word): Middle English composicioun, from Old French composicion, from Latin compositiō, as on English Wiktionary (entry “composition”). Other new week-9 terms were left without an origin rather than a guessed root.
  • Tacitus, Histories Book I, opening, in the Loeb Classical Library translation (1925), public domain as presented on LacusCurtius (Bill Thayer). The opening begins with Galba’s second consulship and names Galba, Otho, and Vitellius, and then Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian, including the career line that Tacitus’s political career owed its beginning to Vespasian. “Year of the Four Emperors” and “Flavian” are taught as later names, not as Tacitus’s titles. 69 CE is a civil-war year after 68 CE, not a fall day of Rome.
  • John Dryden, trans., opening of Aeneid Book I, in The Works of Virgil containing his Pastorals, Georgics and Æneis, first published 1697 (Wikisource, The Works of Virgil (Dryden)/Aeneid/Book I; the work page notes the 1697 first publication). Set extract only: “Arms, and the Man I sing, who forc'd by Fate, And haughty Juno's unrelenting Hate; Expell'd and exil'd, left the Trojan Shoar.” The spelling Shoar is Dryden’s. Later books are reserved.
  • National Eye Institute, “How the eyes work”: photoreceptors in the retina turn light into electrical signals, which travel by the optic nerve; the brain turns the signals into images. National Eye Institute, “Color blindness”: color vision deficiency as seeing colors differently than most people; most often, trouble telling certain colors apart; complete color vision deficiency rare; common types often genetic; men have a much higher risk than women. No invented rod-or-cone count. Afterimages are taught as a careful, optional observation without an invented mechanism, time, or wavelength.
  • Circular motion and a centripetal (center-seeking) force are taught qualitatively: velocity along the tangent; a change of direction is a change of velocity; if the inward force stops, the motion continues along the tangent in this first look. No invented orbital speed, string length, or force number.
