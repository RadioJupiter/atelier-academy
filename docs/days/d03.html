Key terms

neuter plural
Definition
The plural of a neuter noun (often ending in -a, like oppida). It takes a neuter plural adjective and the verb sunt.
Example
Oppida magna sunt in Italiā: towns are large in Italy.
Not this
A neuter plural in -a is not a feminine singular in -a. Oppida is not ‘a town’.
Connects to
Day 2’s agreement now has to cover this trap: the ending -a can be feminine singular or neuter plural, so you also look at the verb.
review (of a chapter)
Definition
A check that you can still read a sentence, hear est and sunt, and make an adjective agree, before the next chapter adds case.

Mathematics · Algebra I: identities and factoring

60 min · Method: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra (identities proved and used, not memorized as tricks)

Learn

Key terms

identity
Definition
An equality that is true for every allowed value of its letters, and that must be proved by expanding or by using a known identity.
Origin
From Late Latin identitās, from idem (‘the same’).
Example
(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b² holds for all a and b.
Not this
(a + b)² = a² + b² is not an identity: the middle term 2ab is missing, so it fails as soon as a and b are not zero.
Connects to
Used forwards, an identity computes (51 × 49 = 50² − 1). Used backwards, it factors.
factor
Definition
To write an expression as a product; or one of those pieces. Identities used backwards are the usual method.
Origin
From Latin factor (‘doer, maker’), from facere (‘to make’).
Example
x³ − 8 = (x − 2)(x² + 2x + 4).
Not this
Factoring is not ‘guessing a number that divides the constant term and hoping’ without a reason that the product matches.
Connects to
This is the same identity as in the definition, read from right to left.

A rectangular diagram (a geometric 'area model') can show why (a+b)² splits into a², b² and two rectangles ab. You must be able to prove each identity by multiplying out:

  • (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
  • a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
  • a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) and a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
  • (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³

Used forwards, they compute; used backwards, they factor. 51 × 49 = (50 + 1)(50 − 1) = 2500 − 1 = 2499. 103² = 10000 + 600 + 9 = 10609. Conversely, x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)².

Worked example (Gelfand style). Prove that the product of four consecutive integers, plus 1, is a perfect square. Pair the outer and inner factors: n(n + 3) = n² + 3n and (n + 1)(n + 2) = n² + 3n + 2. Let m = n² + 3n + 1. The product is (m − 1)(m + 1) = m² − 1, so adding 1 gives m² = (n² + 3n + 1)². ∎ (Check: 1·2·3·4 + 1 = 25 = 5², and n = 1 gives m = 5.)

Remember

An identity is true for all allowed values. Expand carefully. (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²; (a−b)² = a² − 2ab + b²; (a+b)(a−b) = a² − b².

Key terms: identity, factor.

Writing & Rhetoric · Appositives and subordinating conjunctions

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, sentence activities

Learn

Key terms

appositive
Definition
A noun phrase set beside another noun to rename it: ‘Leonardo, the draughtsman, …’.
Origin
From the grammar sense of apposition, from Middle English, from Middle French, from Latin appositiō, from appōnere (‘to put near’).
Example
The plate, a Bargue lithograph, was copied because the eye needed training in line.
Not this
An appositive is not a subordinate clause (it has no verb of its own), and it is not a separate sentence.
Connects to
You still start from a kernel (Day 1) and may add a why-clause. The appositive renames a noun inside that sentence.
subordinate clause
Definition
A clause that cannot stand alone as a sentence; it starts with a word such as because, although, when, who, or that, and it depends on a main clause.
Example
‘Because the light was good’ is not a sentence. ‘The plate was copied because the light was good’ is.
Not this
A subordinate clause is not an appositive, and it is not a kernel. A kernel can stand alone.
Connects to
Day 1’s because-expansions were already subordinate clauses. Today they are named so you can punctuate and control them.

An appositive is a noun phrase set beside another noun to rename it: Leonardo, the draughtsman, …. A subordinate clause cannot stand alone as a sentence; it starts with a word such as because, when, although, if, or who. Both are tools for packing information into one controlled sentence rather than a string of short ones.

Appositive. A noun phrase placed beside a noun to rename it, set off by commas: Gérôme, a French painter and sculptor, collaborated with Bargue on the drawing course. Appositives pack identifying information into one sentence and are a mark of mature prose.

Subordinating conjunctions begin a dependent clause that cannot stand alone: although, because, since, when, while, if, unless, after, before, until, whereas. Starting with the dependent clause forces precision about the relationship:

Although Latin has no native speakers today, it is still read daily by scholars. Unless the block-in is accurate, shading will only make the errors more visible.

Sentence combining. Joining short sentences into one well-built sentence trains you to choose the relationship deliberately.

Remember

An appositive sits next to the noun it renames, usually between commas. A subordinate clause needs a main clause. Do not leave a subordinate clause standing alone.

Key terms: appositive, subordinate clause.

Physics · Acceleration and velocity–time graphs

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol (answer → confidence → argue another option → re-answer → key)

Learn

Key terms

acceleration aavg = Δv / Δt
Definition
The rate of change of velocity: how quickly velocity increases, decreases, or changes direction.
Origin
From Latin accelerātiō, from accelerāre (‘to hasten’); first attested in English in 1531.
Example
A cart going from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s has average acceleration 2 m/s². On a velocity–time graph, acceleration is the slope.
Not this
Acceleration is not ‘going fast’, and a negative acceleration is not a different kind of quantity from a; it is acceleration in the negative direction (or a slowing down, if velocity was positive).
Connects to
Velocity (Day 1) is already a rate: change of position. Acceleration is the next rate: change of velocity.
velocity–time graph
Definition
A graph with time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis. The slope is acceleration; the area between the graph and the time axis is displacement.
Example
A horizontal line at v = 5 m/s for 4 s means zero acceleration and a displacement of 20 m.
Not this
It is not a position–time graph. A straight line sloping down on a v–t graph is constant (nonzero) acceleration, not ‘slowing only if the line is curved’.
Connects to
Day 1 used the slope of a position–time graph as velocity. The same slope idea now reads acceleration.

Velocity is the rate of change of position. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: how quickly the velocity increases or decreases, including a change of direction. Average acceleration is Δv / Δt. On a velocity–time graph, the slope is acceleration, and the area under the graph (for the interval) is the displacement.

Pre-reading. - Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / Δt (m/s²). It is a vector. An object slowing down has acceleration opposite to its velocity. - On a velocity–time graph, the slope is the acceleration and the area between the graph and the time axis is the displacement (area below the axis counts as negative). - With constant acceleration: v = v₀ + at; x = v₀t + ½at²; v² = v₀² + 2ax. - Near Earth's surface, a freely falling object (ignoring air resistance) accelerates downward at g ≈ 9.8 m/s², whether it is going up, at the top or coming down.

ConcepTests

  1. A ball is thrown straight up. At the highest point: (A) v = 0 and a = 0 (B) v = 0 and a = 9.8 m/s² downward (C) v is upward and a = 0 (D) v = 0 and a is upward.
  2. Which is possible? (A) velocity north, acceleration south (B) zero velocity, nonzero acceleration (C) both A and B (D) neither.
  3. A velocity–time graph is a horizontal line above the time axis. The object has: (A) constant positive acceleration (B) zero acceleration and constant velocity (C) zero velocity (D) increasing displacement at an increasing rate.

Problems

  1. A car goes from rest to 27 m/s in 9.0 s with constant acceleration. Find the acceleration and the distance covered.
  2. A train at 20 m/s brakes at 2.5 m/s². How long and how far until it stops?
  3. A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from 0 to 10 m/s over 4 s, stays at 10 m/s for 6 s, then falls in a straight line to 0 over 2 s. Find the total displacement.

Remember

aavg = Δv / Δt. Slope of a v–t graph = acceleration. Area under a v–t graph = displacement (for that interval).

Key terms: acceleration, velocity–time graph.

Watch me

A cart goes from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s, speeding up steadily.

  1. Δv = 8 − 2 = 6 m/s.
  2. aavg = 6 / 3 = 2 m/s².
  3. On a v–t graph this is a straight line from 2 to 8 in 3 s. The area is a trapezium: (2+8)/2 × 3 = 15 m of displacement.

Together

Hint 1: Change

Hint 2: Divide

History · Babylon: the laws of Hammurabi

45 min · Primary source: the laws on the stele of Hammurabi (C. H. W. Johns translation, 1903, public domain)

Learn

Key terms

stele
Definition
An upright stone slab with a relief or an inscription, set up to be seen in public.
Origin
From Ancient Greek στήλη (‘upright rock; pillar; column’). A doublet of stela.
Example
Hammurabi’s laws were inscribed on a black stone stele about 2.25 m tall, with a relief of the king before the god Shamash.
Not this
A stele is not a book and not a clay tablet, though the same laws may also survive on tablets.
Connects to
As a primary source, the stele is both a legal text and a public claim about the king.
lex talionis
Definition
The rule of retaliation in kind: the penalty matches the injury, ‘an eye for an eye’. In Hammurabi’s code it applies fully only to a victim of full status.
Origin
From Latin lex (‘a proposed law, a bill’) + tāliōnis, genitive of tāliō (‘retaliation’), from tālis (‘of such kind’). The Latin phrase is a conventional name for the principle, not a Babylonian title.
Example
Section 196: if a man has caused the loss of a gentleman’s eye, his own eye is to be lost. Sections 198–199 replace that with a payment when the victim is of lower status.
Not this
It is not automatically ‘the harshest possible law’. A limit to one eye also restrains a feud from taking more than an eye. It is also not applied equally to every rank in this code.
Connects to
Day 1’s sourcing questions apply: who set this up, for whom, and why a king would publish it in stone.

Context. Hammurabi ruled Babylon in the 18th century BCE (the Louvre dates his reign 1792–1750 BCE). Near the end of his reign his laws were inscribed on a black stone stele about 2.25 m tall, crowned by a relief of the king standing before the sun god Shamash, god of justice. Some 600 years later the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte carried the stele off to Susa as war booty. French excavators found it there in 1901–02, and it is now in the Louvre. Several columns near the bottom had been erased in antiquity; some of the missing laws are known from other copies.

The source (sections 196–199, Johns's translation):

  1. If a man has caused the loss of a gentleman's eye, his eye one shall cause to be lost.
  2. If he has shattered a gentleman's limb, one shall shatter his limb.
  3. If he has caused a poor man to lose his eye or shattered a poor man's limb, he shall pay one mina of silver.
  4. If he has caused the loss of the eye of a gentleman's servant or has shattered the limb of a gentleman's servant, he shall pay half his price.

(Modern translators often render Johns's "gentleman" as awīlum, a free man of full status, and "poor man" as muškēnum, a lower class of free person. "Servant" here means a slave, whose "price" is his value.)

Remember

Hammurabi's stele is a public primary source: a law code and a royal self-portrait. Sections 196–199 apply lex talionis in full only to a victim of full status; lower status brings a payment instead. When two reliable sources disagree (Johns: 'diorite'; the Louvre: basalt), prefer the one with better access to the object and note that knowledge can change.

Key terms: stele, lex talionis.

Watch me

Sourcing the stele, as a model.

  1. Who and why: made for Hammurabi, displayed in a temple context, addressed to subjects, gods, and future kings — to present the king as a just ruler, to publish standards, and to preserve his name in stone.
  2. Close reading of 196–199: retaliation in kind for a man of full status; a fixed payment for a lower-status free person; half a slave's value for a slave, payable (it is implied) to the owner. Punishment depends on the victim's rank.
  3. So the stele is both a legal text and a claim about a hierarchical society. We do not yet treat 'an eye for an eye' as simply 'harsh' or simply 'fair' without that context.

Atelier · Bargue plate: refining the contour and mapping shadow

90 min

Learn

Key terms

contour
Definition
The edge of a shape as you see it from the viewing point: its angles, tilts, and changes of direction, drawn after the block-in is right.
Origin
Borrowed from French contour, from contourner (‘to turn around’).
Example
The slope of a shoulder, compared with a pencil held at arm’s length, is a contour you correct before any shading.
Not this
Contour is not a decorative outline added at the end, and serious shading does not begin while the contour is still wrong.
Connects to
Day 2 placed the large shapes. Today you refine their edges. Value comes next.
angle (in a drawing)
Definition
The tilt of a line or an edge, compared with a horizontal, a vertical, or a neighboring edge.
Example
The slope of a shoulder can be compared with a pencil and then with the same tilt on the paper.
Not this
An angle here is not a geometry proof, and it is not ‘how dark the edge is’.
Connects to
A plumb line (Day 1) is a vertical to compare with. Contour is made of such comparisons.

After the block-in, you refine the contour: correct angles, compare neighboring lengths, and tighten the large shapes before any shading. Work from large to small. A wrong small curve on a right big shape is easier to fix than a pretty contour on a wrong proportion.

Begin by correcting the largest error you marked yesterday. Never build on an uncorrected block-in.

Refining. Break each long straight line into shorter straight lines that follow the contour more closely. Only at the end let the shortest segments become curves. Keep checking angles against the plumb line and against each other.

The shadow shape. On the finished half of the plate, find the boundary between light and shadow, the terminator (also called the core edge). Draw it as a precise shape, as carefully as the outline. In academic drawing, shadow shapes are designed as flat shapes first and modeled later.

Remember

Check big angles again. Compare neighboring lengths. Refine the contour lightly. Still no serious shading.

Key terms: contour, angle (in a drawing).

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. From the viewing point, pick one big angle (for example the slope of a shoulder) and compare it with your pencil to the angle on the drawing.
  2. If the drawing is steeper, lighten and reset that line; do not darken it first.
  3. Measure a smaller length as a fraction of a length you already trust, and adjust.
  4. Only when three or four main comparisons agree do you go around the contour once more, still lightly.

Hint 2: Why

Modern language · Spanish: ser and description

25 min

Learn

Key terms

ser
Definition
The Spanish verb used for what something is: identity, origin, characteristics, and time. (It will be contrasted with estar later.)
Origin
Spanish ser ‘to be’, from Latin esse (the same verb family as Latin est and sunt), with forms also influenced by Latin sedēre in the history of the language. Only the living Spanish forms are required here.
Example
La ciudad es antigua (a characteristic). Nosotros somos estudiantes (identity).
Not this
Ser is not the verb for place or temporary condition; that will be estar. Do not treat them as one English ‘is’.
Connects to
The five vowels (Day 1) still apply: es, somos must not glide. Gender and articles (Day 2) appear in the same sentences.
present tense of ser
Definition
The forms of ser used for now: soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son. Each form belongs to a subject.
Example
Nosotros somos estudiantes uses somos, not es.
Not this
Es does not go with nosotros, and soy is not a word for ‘I have’.
Connects to
Like Latin est and sunt, the form must match the subject. The two languages have different forms.

Adjectives agree in gender and number and usually follow the noun: un libro rojo, una casa roja, unos libros rojos. Adjectives ending in -e or a consonant have one form for both genders: una pintora inteligente, un examen difícil. Usted (formal "you") takes the third-person verb.

Remember

Ser for identity and lasting qualities: Soy estudiante. El museo es antiguo. The adjective often changes ending: alto / alta.

Art history · How to look; the first images

30 min · Seminar rotation: Art history (Wednesdays), chronological alongside History

Learn

Key terms

formal analysis
Definition
A description of what is visibly there in a work — line, shape, mass, value, color, texture, space, composition — before any interpretation.
Example
‘A continuous dark contour of varying weight along the back; flat areas of red-brown; a head in profile; the belly line following a ridge of rock.’
Not this
Formal analysis is not ‘this was probably hunting magic’, and it is not a biography of the artist. Interpretation comes second and must be labeled.
Connects to
Value and contour are already studio words (atelier this week). Here they are used to look at an image you did not draw.
interpretation
Definition
A claim about meaning or purpose, clearly marked as such, and paired with the evidence that would test it.
Origin
From Latin interpretātiō, from interpretārī (‘to explain, to translate’).
Example
‘This may be a seasonal record of prey animals’ is an interpretation; a test would be whether the depicted species match bones in the cave’s food debris.
Not this
An interpretation is not a formal analysis, and it is not a fact just because it is often repeated. Scholars still disagree about the purpose of cave art.
Connects to
History’s sourcing habit applies: say what you see, then say what you infer, and keep the two distinct.

Formal analysis describes what is there before interpreting it: line (contour, direction, weight); shape and mass; value (light and dark); color; texture; space (overlap, scale, perspective); composition (how parts are arranged, where the eye goes). Describe precisely, then infer.

The first images. Painted caves in France and Spain preserve some of the oldest known figurative art. The paintings at Chauvet Cave (France) are more than 30,000 years old; those at Lascaux are about 17,000 years old. Animals dominate: horses, aurochs, deer, bison. Artists used the natural bulges of the rock to suggest the body's volume, drew in charcoal and mineral pigments (iron oxides for reds and yellows, manganese or charcoal for blacks), and sometimes blew pigment through a tube to make outlines of hands.

Remember

Formal analysis describes what is there (line, shape, mass, value, color, texture, space, composition) before any interpretation. Interpretation must be labeled and paired with testable evidence. Chauvet paintings are more than 30,000 years old; Lascaux about 17,000. Animals dominate; artists used the rock's volume, charcoal, and mineral pigments. Scholars still disagree about purpose.

Key terms: formal analysis, interpretation.

Watch me

Two sentences, one description and one interpretation, as a model (your 150-word analysis of an animal must be your own, from a photograph).

  1. Description: a continuous dark contour of varying weight along the back; a flat red-brown flank; a head in profile; the belly line following a ridge of the rock. Every phrase names something visible.
  2. Interpretation, clearly marked: this may have been painted to help a hunt. Evidence that would test it: do the depicted species match the bones in the cave's food debris? If they do not, the claim is weaker.
  3. The two must not be mixed in one unmarked sentence.

Together

Why is 'this was hunting magic' not yet a formal analysis, and what would make it a responsible interpretation?

Hint 1: Analysis

Formal analysis only describes what is visible. Purpose is not visible on the wall.

Hint 2: Interpretation
