Mathematics · Algebra I: identities and factoring

60 min · Method: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra (identities proved and used, not memorised as tricks)

Learn

An **identity** is an equality that holds for every allowed value of the letter (for example (*a*+*b*)² = *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*²). You can prove an identity by expanding the left side, or by using a known identity, until it matches the right side. A rectangular diagram (a geometric 'area model') can show why (*a*+*b*)² splits into *a*², *b*² and two rectangles *ab*.

Learn

**An identity** is an equality that holds for *all* values of its letters. You must be able to prove each one by multiplying out: - (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b² - a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b) - a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) and a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²) - (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³ **Used forwards, they compute; used backwards, they factor.** 51 × 49 = (50 + 1)(50 − 1) = 2500 − 1 = 2499. 103² = 10000 + 600 + 9 = 10609. Conversely, x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)². **Worked example (Gelfand style).** Prove that the product of four consecutive integers, plus 1, is a perfect square. Pair the outer and inner factors: n(n + 3) = n² + 3n and (n + 1)(n + 2) = n² + 3n + 2. Let m = n² + 3n + 1. The product is (m − 1)(m + 1) = m² − 1, so adding 1 gives m² = (n² + 3n + 1)². ∎ (Check: 1·2·3·4 + 1 = 25 = 5², and n = 1 gives m = 5.)

Remember

An identity is true for all allowed values. Expand carefully. (*a*+*b*)² = *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*²; (*a*−*b*)² = *a*² − 2*ab* + *b*²; (*a*+*b*)(*a*−*b*) = *a*² − *b*².

Watch me

Show that (*a*+*b*)(*a*−*b*) = *a*² − *b*².

  1. Expand: *a*·*a* + *a*·(−*b*) + *b*·*a* + *b*·(−*b*) = *a*² − *ab* + *ab* − *b*².
  2. The middle terms cancel.
  3. What remains is *a*² − *b*².

Together

Expand (*a*+*b*)² using the area model or the distributive law.

Hint 1: Four parts *a*² + *ab* + *ba* + *b*².
Hint 2: Combine *ab* and *ba* are the same, so *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*².

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Is (*a*+*b*)² = *a*² + *b*² an identity? Why or why not? 2. What is (*a*+*b*)(*a*−*b*)?

Exercises

  1. Compute mentally, showing the identity used: 47 × 53; 99²; 1001 × 999; 65².
  2. Factor completely: x² − 9y²; x⁴ − 1; 8a³ − 27; x² + 6x + 9; a³ + 1.
  3. Simplify (x + 1)² − (x − 1)².
  4. If a + b = 7 and ab = 10, find a² + b² and a³ + b³ without finding a and b.
  5. Prove that 2¹⁶ − 1 is divisible by 17.
  6. Prove that the difference of the squares of two consecutive odd numbers is divisible by 8.
  7. (Stretch.) Verify that a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca), and deduce: if a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

Writing & Rhetoric · Appositives and subordinating conjunctions

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, sentence activities

Learn

An **appositive** is a noun phrase set beside another noun to rename it: *Leonardo, the draughtsman, …*. A **subordinate clause** cannot stand alone as a sentence; it starts with a word such as *because*, *when*, *although*, *if*, or *who*. Both are tools for packing information into one controlled sentence rather than a string of short ones.

Learn

**Appositive.** A noun phrase placed beside a noun to rename it, set off by commas: *Gérôme, **a French painter and sculptor**, collaborated with Bargue on the drawing course.* Appositives pack identifying information into one sentence and are a mark of mature prose. **Subordinating conjunctions** begin a dependent clause that cannot stand alone: *although, because, since, when, while, if, unless, after, before, until, whereas.* Starting with the dependent clause forces precision about the relationship: > **Although** Latin has no native speakers today, it is still read daily by scholars. > **Unless** the block-in is accurate, shading will only make the errors more visible. **Sentence combining.** Joining short sentences into one well-built sentence trains you to choose the relationship deliberately.

Remember

An appositive sits next to the noun it renames, usually between commas. A subordinate clause needs a main clause. Do not leave a subordinate clause standing alone.

Watch me

Kernel: The plate was copied. With an appositive and a why-clause: The plate, a Bargue lithograph, was copied because the eye needed training in line.

Together

Add an appositive to Homer tells the story.

Hint 1: Rename Homer For example: *the poet of the Iliad*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Homer, the poet of the Iliad, tells the story.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is an appositive? 2. Why is *Because the light was good.* not a complete sentence?

Exercises

  1. Add an accurate appositive to each: (a) Kiselev wrote geometry textbooks used in Russian schools for decades. (b) Erasmus wrote De Copia. (c) The Iliad begins with the anger of Achilles.
  2. Begin each with a subordinating conjunction of your choice and complete it accurately: (a) ___ the candle goes out, … (b) ___ vertical angles are equal, … (c) ___ the plate is placed beside the drawing at the same size, …
  3. Combine into one sentence each: (a) The stele was set up in Babylonia. It was taken to Susa as plunder. It was excavated there in 1901–02. (b) The runner finished a lap. The runner's displacement was zero. The runner's distance was 400 m.

Physics · Acceleration and velocity–time graphs

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol (answer → confidence → argue another option → re-answer → key)

Learn

**Velocity** is the rate of change of position. **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: how quickly the velocity increases or decreases, including a change of direction. Average acceleration is Δ*v* / Δ*t*. On a velocity–time graph, the slope is acceleration, and the area under the graph (for the interval) is the displacement.

Learn

**Pre-reading.** - **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / Δt (m/s²). It is a vector. An object slowing down has acceleration opposite to its velocity. - On a **velocity–time graph**, the slope is the acceleration and the area between the graph and the time axis is the displacement (area below the axis counts as negative). - With constant acceleration: v = v₀ + at; x = v₀t + ½at²; v² = v₀² + 2ax. - Near Earth's surface, a freely falling object (ignoring air resistance) accelerates downward at g ≈ 9.8 m/s², whether it is going up, at the top or coming down. ### ConcepTests 1. A ball is thrown straight up. At the highest point: (A) v = 0 and a = 0 (B) v = 0 and a = 9.8 m/s² downward (C) v is upward and a = 0 (D) v = 0 and a is upward. 2. Which is possible? (A) velocity north, acceleration south (B) zero velocity, nonzero acceleration (C) both A and B (D) neither. 3. A velocity–time graph is a horizontal line above the time axis. The object has: (A) constant positive acceleration (B) zero acceleration and constant velocity (C) zero velocity (D) increasing displacement at an increasing rate. ### Problems 4. A car goes from rest to 27 m/s in 9.0 s with constant acceleration. Find the acceleration and the distance covered. 5. A train at 20 m/s brakes at 2.5 m/s². How long and how far until it stops? 6. A velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from 0 to 10 m/s over 4 s, stays at 10 m/s for 6 s, then falls in a straight line to 0 over 2 s. Find the total displacement.

Remember

*a*avg = Δ*v* / Δ*t*. Slope of a *v*–*t* graph = acceleration. Area under a *v*–*t* graph = displacement (for that interval).

Watch me

A cart goes from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s, speeding up steadily.

  1. Δ*v* = 8 − 2 = 6 m/s.
  2. *a*avg = 6 / 3 = 2 m/s².
  3. On a *v*–*t* graph this is a straight line from 2 to 8 in 3 s. The area is a trapezium: (2+8)/2 × 3 = 15 m of displacement.

Together

A bicycle slows from 10 m/s to 4 m/s in 2 s. What is its average acceleration?

Hint 1: Change 4 − 10 = −6 m/s.
Hint 2: Divide −6 / 2 = **−3 m/s²** (it is decelerating).

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does the slope of a velocity–time graph represent? 2. A body keeps a steady 5 m/s for 4 s. What is its acceleration, and how far does it go?

History · Babylon: the laws of Hammurabi

45 min · Primary source: the laws on the stele of Hammurabi (C. H. W. Johns translation, 1903, public domain)

Context. Hammurabi ruled Babylon in the 18th century BCE (the Louvre dates his reign 1792–1750 BCE). Near the end of his reign his laws were inscribed on a black stone stele about 2.25 m tall, crowned by a relief of the king standing before the sun god Shamash, god of justice. Some 600 years later the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte carried the stele off to Susa as war booty. French excavators found it there in 1901–02, and it is now in the Louvre. Several columns near the bottom had been erased in antiquity; some of the missing laws are known from other copies.

The source (sections 196–199, Johns's translation):

  1. If a man has caused the loss of a gentleman's eye, his eye one shall cause to be lost.
  2. If he has shattered a gentleman's limb, one shall shatter his limb.
  3. If he has caused a poor man to lose his eye or shattered a poor man's limb, he shall pay one mina of silver.
  4. If he has caused the loss of the eye of a gentleman's servant or has shattered the limb of a gentleman's servant, he shall pay half his price.

(Modern translators often render Johns's "gentleman" as awīlum, a free man of full status, and "poor man" as muškēnum, a lower class of free person. "Servant" here means a slave, whose "price" is his value.)

Exercises

  1. Sourcing. Who made the stele, for what audience and for what purpose? Why might a king set up his laws in public stone?
  2. Close reading. What principle governs section 196? How do sections 198 and 199 change it? What does that tell you about Babylonian society?
  3. Corroboration. Johns, writing in 1903, describes the stele as "black diorite"; the Louvre today describes it as basalt. How should a historian handle a disagreement like this between two otherwise reliable sources?
  4. Interpretation. Is "an eye for an eye" a harsh law or a limiting one? Argue in one paragraph, using the source.

Atelier · Bargue plate: refining the contour and mapping shadow

90 min

Learn

After the block-in, you **refine the contour**: correct angles, compare neighbouring lengths, and tighten the large shapes before any shading. Work from large to small. A wrong small curve on a right big shape is easier to fix than a pretty contour on a wrong proportion.

Learn

**Begin** by correcting the largest error you marked yesterday. Never build on an uncorrected block-in. **Refining.** Break each long straight line into shorter straight lines that follow the contour more closely. Only at the end let the shortest segments become curves. Keep checking angles against the plumb line and against each other. **The shadow shape.** On the finished half of the plate, find the boundary between light and shadow, the **terminator** (also called the core edge). Draw it as a precise *shape*, as carefully as the outline. In academic drawing, shadow shapes are designed as flat shapes first and modelled later.

Remember

Check big angles again. Compare neighbouring lengths. Refine the contour lightly. Still no serious shading.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. From the viewing point, pick one big angle (for example the slope of a shoulder) and compare it with your pencil to the angle on the drawing.
  2. If the drawing is steeper, lighten and reset that line; do not darken it first.
  3. Measure a smaller length as a fraction of a length you already trust, and adjust.
  4. Only when three or four main comparisons agree do you go around the contour once more, still lightly.

Together

If a small bump on the outline looks 'interesting' but the height of the whole form is still uncertain, what do you do?

Hint 1: Order Leave the bump. Fix the height first.
Hint 2: Why Small contour is meaningless on a wrong proportion.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is the aim of this stage, in one sentence? 2. When does serious shading begin?

Exercises

  1. (50 min) Refine the contour of your plate to its final accuracy.
  2. (25 min) Draw the shadow shapes lightly as outlines. Fill them with one flat, even tone of HB. Do not model them.
  3. (15 min) Sight-size and mirror check. List three remaining differences.

Art history · How to look; the first images

30 min · Seminar rotation: Art history (Wednesdays), chronological alongside History

Formal analysis describes what is there before interpreting it: line (contour, direction, weight); shape and mass; value (light and dark); colour; texture; space (overlap, scale, perspective); composition (how parts are arranged, where the eye goes). Describe precisely, then infer.

The first images. Painted caves in France and Spain preserve some of the oldest known figurative art. The paintings at Chauvet Cave (France) are more than 30,000 years old; those at Lascaux are about 17,000 years old. Animals dominate: horses, aurochs, deer, bison. Artists used the natural bulges of the rock to suggest the body's volume, drew in charcoal and mineral pigments (iron oxides for reds and yellows, manganese or charcoal for blacks), and sometimes blew pigment through a tube to make outlines of hands.

Exercises

  1. Find a good photograph of the "Hall of the Bulls" at Lascaux (the French Ministry of Culture's Lascaux site has them). Write a formal analysis of one animal in 150 words: line, shape, value, colour, use of the rock surface. No interpretation yet.
  2. Then write two sentences of interpretation, clearly marked as interpretation, and state what evidence would be needed to test them.
