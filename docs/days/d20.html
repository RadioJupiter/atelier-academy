Latin · Capitula V–VI: two-week check

30 min · Two-week check on the ablative, both lists of prepositions, town names, the locative, and the first passives

Learn

Key terms

case after a preposition
Definition
The rule that the preposition, not a translation from English, chooses the case: one closed list takes the ablative (in, ex, ab, cum, sine), and one takes the accusative (ad, ante, post, inter, prope, circum, apud, per). Town names are the exception that drops the preposition.
Example
in hortō, ad hortum, ab hortō; but Rōmae, Rōmam, Rōmā.
Not this
It is not ‘use the accusative for motion and the ablative for rest’ in every sentence: cum and sine take the ablative even though they are not places, and prope takes the accusative even though it can describe a rest.
Connects to
This is the two-week summary of Capitula V and VI, to be used next week rather than re-taught from zero.
three place questions
Definition
The trio ubi? (where?), quō? (where to?), unde? (where from?), each with its own construction for common nouns and for town names.
Example
Ubi habitat Mārcus? Rōmae. Quō it? Rōmam. Unde venit? Rōmā. Ubi est puer? in hortō. Quō ambulat? ad vīllam. Unde venit? ex hortō.
Not this
English ‘where’ is not one Latin word. Mixing the three questions is the usual error, and it produces the wrong case.
Connects to
The locative belongs only to the ubi? column, and only for town names (and a few other words not added today).

A short mixed check. Close the book first; then check the key; then re-read any section of Capitula V–VI that was weak.

Work in writing. Use macrons. Answer questions in full Latin sentences.

Remember

Ablative list: in, ex, ab, cum, sine. Accusative list: ad, ante, post, inter, prope, circum, apud, per. Towns: Rōmam / Rōmā / Rōmae. Passive: portātur, ā + agent. Three questions: ubi, quō, unde.

Key terms: case after a preposition, three place questions.

Watch me

  3. The two forms look related but they are different cases, answering different questions.

Mathematics · Two-week problem set: constructions to tangents, and a first look at progressions

60 min · Method: mixed practice at the level of this week's worked examples; a short introduction to progressions

Learn

Key terms

arithmetic progression
Definition
A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by adding the same fixed number, the common difference d. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d, where a is the first term.
Origin
Arithmetic is from Middle English arsmetike, from Old French, from Latin arithmētica, from Ancient Greek ἀριθμητική (‘(art of) counting’), from ἀριθμός (‘number’). Progression is from Middle English progressioun, from Old French and Latin prōgressiō; in English, progress + -ion.
Example
5, 9, 13, 17, … has d = 4. The 8th term is 5 + 7 × 4 = 33.
Not this
An arithmetic progression is not a list that merely ‘goes up’. If the difference changes, it is not arithmetic. It is also not a geometric progression, where you multiply by a constant each time.
Connects to
The same idea as a linear function (Day 9): the terms sit on a straight line when you plot them against the term number.
geometric progression
Definition
A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by multiplying by the same fixed number, the common ratio r. The nth term is a rⁿ⁻¹, where a is the first term.
Origin
From Late Latin geometricus, from Ancient Greek γεωμετρικός, from γεωμετρία (‘geometry’). Here the name is historical: such a sequence was studied alongside geometric figures.
Example
3, 6, 12, 24, … has r = 2. The 5th term is 3 × 2⁴ = 48.
Not this
A geometric progression is not a list of geometry measurements, and it is not arithmetic: 3, 6, 12 does not have a constant difference (the differences are 3, then 6).
Connects to
The common ratio is the same habit as yesterday's exponents: multiplying by r, n − 1 times, is multiplication by rⁿ⁻¹.

This lesson is a mixed set on the two weeks just taught, together with one new tool.

Arithmetic progression (AP). Add the same d each time. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d.

Geometric progression (GP). Multiply by the same r each time. The nth term is a rⁿ⁻¹.

The rest of the hour is problems at the same difficulty as the worked examples: a construction reason, a system, a special parallelogram, an exponent or radical, a chord or a tangent. Draw first. Name the theorem you use. Check.

Remember

AP: nth term a + (n − 1)d. GP: nth term a rⁿ⁻¹. For the mixed problems, use the theorem or law from the lesson that taught it, and check.

Key terms: arithmetic progression, geometric progression.

Watch me

  1. The AP has first term 5 and common difference 4, so the 8th term is 5 + 7 × 4 = 33.
  2. The GP has first term 3 and common ratio 2, so the 5th term is 3 × 2⁴ = 48.
  3. Check: AP 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, 25, 29, 33. GP 3, 6, 12, 24, 48. ✓

Hint 1: Half

Writing & Rhetoric · Two-week check: a two-paragraph essay

45 min · Write from the revised outline; then check the list

Learn

Key terms

two-paragraph essay
Definition
A complete short argument with one thesis, two paragraphs (each with a topic sentence and support), at least one introduced and explained quotation or a precise précis of a source, a real concession or a limit, and a concluding sentence that returns to the thesis in new words.
Example
An essay on town names versus common nouns in Latin, using one short attributed example (Rōmam / ad vīllam) and conceding that a few other words also have a locative, still fits in two paragraphs.
Not this
It is not two unconnected paragraphs, not a summary of a lesson, and not a page of quotations. Length is not the test; the jobs on the list are the test.
Connects to
The piece uses the week’s skills: combining and transitions (Day 11), a legal semicolon if needed (Day 13), quotation or précis (Days 15 and 18), and the outline with a concession (Days 16 and 19).
revision pass
Definition
A second writing, after the draft is complete, in which you check a short list — thesis, topic sentences, evidence, attribution, concession or limit, concluding sentence, and punctuation of joins — and change the sentences that fail, rather than recopying the whole for neatness.
Example
If a quotation has no explanation, the revision adds one. If a semicolon splices a fragment, the revision repairs the mark. Neat handwriting alone is not a revision pass.
Not this
A revision pass is not a first draft written more slowly, and it is not a hunt for a better vocabulary word in a sentence that already works.
Connects to
Friday’s check is the essay plus this pass, not the essay written once and left.

Write the two-paragraph essay from your revised outline. Then run the list.

The list. (1) An arguable thesis in the first or second sentence. (2) Two topic sentences, with a reason for the order. (3) Support in each paragraph (examples, a short attributed quotation, or a précis of a source). (4) Every quotation introduced and explained. (5) A real concession or a precise limit. (6) A concluding sentence in new words. (7) Legal joins: no comma splices; semicolons used only between complete sentences.

Subject. Use the history, literature, science, or Latin question you outlined on Day 16, or this prompt: A primary source is not self-interpreting. Show this from two documents you have actually read in the last four weeks.

Work in writing. Then revise. Only then look at the key, which is a checklist, not a model essay to copy.

Remember

Write two paragraphs from the outline. Then run the seven-point list and revise. The key is a checklist, not a model to imitate.

Key terms: two-paragraph essay, revision pass.

Watch me

  2. A revision would add at least one concrete case (a short attributed quotation, or a specific form such as Rōmam versus ad vīllam, or a named document).
  3. The topic sentences can stay if the new support actually serves them.

Physics · Two-week check: friction to momentum

45 min · Mixed check at the level of this week’s worked examples

Learn

Key terms

impulse J = Δp
Definition
The product of a net force and the time it acts, equal to the change in momentum: J = F_net Δt = Δp, when the force is constant (or using an average force). The SI unit is N·s, which is the same as kg·m/s.
Origin
Borrowed from Middle French impulser, from Latin impulsus.
Example
A 1 kg cart going at 6 m/s is stopped in 0.3 s: Δp = −6 kg·m/s, so the average force is −20 N, and the impulse is −6 N·s.
Not this
Impulse is not the same as a force (it includes time), and it is not the same as energy. A small force over a long time can deliver the same impulse as a large force over a short time.
Connects to
Yesterday’s F = Δp/Δt is this definition, rearranged. Today it is named so that a mixed check can use the word exactly.
independence of components
Definition
The rule, used for projectiles, that the horizontal motion (constant v_x, if air is neglected) and the vertical motion (acceleration g) can be solved separately, and that a change in one component does not by itself change the other.
Example
From a given height, a faster horizontal throw has a larger range but the same time of flight.
Not this
Independence is not a claim that the body is two bodies, and it fails once air resistance (or another force with both components) matters.
Connects to
This is the idea that tied Days 13 and 15 together, and it is one of the checks today.

A mixed check on the two weeks: friction and the normal force, components, projectiles, circular motion, and momentum (including impulse).

Draw a diagram. Name the principle. Check the units. Take g = 9.8 m/s² unless a problem says otherwise.

Remember

Draw first. Use N = mg on the level; f = μN; components and Pythagoras; projectile time from the vertical, range = v_x t; a = v²/r; p = mv, with conservation when the system is closed; J = Δp.

Key terms: impulse, independence of components.

  3. Check: 19.8 − 0.2 × 5 × 9.8 = 19.8 − 9.8 = 10, and 10/5 = 2. ✓

History · Two-week synthesis: sources for kingship, from Khufu to the Ptolemies

45 min · A short argued piece, using documents from this week and earlier, not a tour of one reign after another

Learn

Key terms

synthesis (this week)
Definition
A short argued piece that puts more than one source, from more than one period, under a single thesis, with sourcing sentences and a limit. It is not a timeline in paragraph form.
Example
A thesis such as: ‘Royal inscriptions and later narratives both present kings, but they do not do the same work; a pyramid text, a treaty, and Arrian have to be asked different questions.’
Not this
A synthesis is not a retelling of every lesson this week, and a quotation left to speak for itself is not evidence (Day 18 of writing).
Connects to
Day 10 already asked for a synthesis of law and citizenship. Today the theme is kingship and how we know, from the Old Kingdom to the Ptolemies.
periodization
Definition
The decision to cut a long past into named periods (Old Kingdom, New Kingdom, Hellenistic) so that change can be talked about. The cuts are tools, not walls in the ground, and the years attached to them may be firmer in one period than in another.
Example
‘Old Kingdom’ is useful for the pyramid age; it is not a word Unas necessarily used, and its BCE dates are softer than 323 BCE.
Not this
Periodization is not a claim that nothing continued, and it is not a license to use one year-number as if all periods were equally exact.
Connects to
This week’s sequence only works if you can say which period a source belongs to, and how firm its dates are.

Write a short synthesis (two paragraphs are enough, if they are argued; a third may carry a limit) on this question:

How do the sources of this week — a royal tomb and its later texts, a two-language treaty, and a late narrative of Alexander — each show a king, and what can a historian not do with any one of them alone?

Rules. An arguable thesis. At least two periods. A sourcing sentence for each main document. No invented years for Khufu. No invented clauses of the treaty. Arrian named as late. A concluding limit.

You may use Hammurabi from Week 1 as a fourth comparison, if he is sourced and if the comparison has a limit.

The key is a rubric, not a model essay to copy.

Remember

A short argued synthesis, not a timeline in prose. Thesis; sourced documents from more than one period; no invented wording or fake exact years; a limit at the close.

Key terms: synthesis (this week), periodization.

Watch me

A draft begins: ‘First Egypt had pyramids. Then there was a treaty. Then Alexander came.’ What is missing, and how would you start instead?

  1. The draft is a tour, not a thesis. It does not ask a question of the sources.
  2. A revision would open with an arguable claim about how royal sources work, then use the pyramid, the treaty, and Arrian as cases.
  3. The order of the cases can still be chronological, if the thesis, not the calendar, is in charge.

Together

Hint 1: Firm

Hint 2: Soft

A single year for Khufu’s pyramid, or a single conversion of ‘year 21’, should carry the chronology warning.

Atelier · Critique and a two-week plan

45 min · Method: a structured critique of this fortnight’s drawings, then a short written plan · After Day 10’s critique, now including planes, hatch, a feature, and edges

Learn

Key terms

two-week critique
Definition
A structured look at the fortnight’s work (cube, cylinder, sphere, Bargue feature, still life, plaster feature), naming what is constructed, what is still a symbol or a wire, and what the next two or three sittings should fix. It is specific, and it uses the terms taught, not a vague ‘good’ or ‘bad’.
Example
A sound critique might say: the cylinder’s ellipses still come to a point; the group’s overlap is true, but the relative values of the cube and the sphere are too close; the plaster nose’s wedge is clear, and the edges are still evenly found.
Not this
A critique is not a score, and it is not a general comment on talent. It is a reading of particular drawings against particular skills.
Connects to
Day 10 already taught critique and a next-work plan. Today that habit is applied to the new skills: planes, foreshortening, ellipses, hatching, a feature, overlap, edges, accents.
plan for the next sittings
Definition
A short, written plan that names one or two skills to practice, the set-up that will make that practice possible, and how you will know the sittings have done their work. It is small enough to keep.
Example
Example: ‘Two sittings on a standing cylinder, sight-size, checking the short axis of each ellipse against a plumb; success is two end ellipses with no points and a shared axis, before any finish.’
Not this
A plan is not a list of ten ambitions, and it is not ‘draw better’. If it cannot be checked, it is not yet a plan.
Connects to
The Check includes writing this plan. The drawings stay; the plan is for the next week, not a promise to finish a portfolio in a day.

Lay out the fortnight’s drawings (or the ones you have): the cube, the cylinder, the sphere, the Bargue feature, the group, the plaster feature, and any edge or accent pass.

A structured critique (use this order, in writing): 1. Construction. Where are planes, axes, ellipses, and enclosing shapes true? Where does a symbol or a guessed shape remain? 2. Light. Do the families read? Is there one light in the groups? Did relative value across objects get compared? 3. Surface tools. Did hatch follow the form? Were crosses local and controlled? Are edges varied, or still a wire? Are accents few? 4. One strongest passage and one clearest weakness, named in sentences, not in scores.

Then a plan for the next sittings, one or two skills only, with a set-up and a check. Examples of a checkable plan: ellipses without points; a feature block-in that starts from a wedge; a group with one honest overlap and a shared table-line; a shadow contour that is actually lost where the values meet.

What you do not do. You do not rework every drawing in one hour. You may spend a short sitting on a single correction (an ellipse, a halo) if it will teach more than another full start. You do not throw out a drawing that is useful as evidence of a habit you intend to change.

English is the language of the critique. Be specific.

Remember

A two-week critique is specific and uses the terms taught: construction, light families, hatch, edges, accents. A plan for the next sittings names one or two skills, a set-up, and a check. No scores, no talent talk, no ten-item wish list.

Key terms: two-week critique, plan for the next sittings.

Watch me

Here is a vague sentence: ‘My drawings are messy and I will try harder.’ Rewrite it as one critique sentence and one plan sentence, in the language of this fortnight.

  1. A critique sentence names a particular fault in a particular drawing, e.g. ‘The cylinder’s top ellipse is pointed, and the short axis leans off the vertical.’
  2. A plan sentence names a set-up and a check, e.g. ‘Two sight-size sittings on a standing cylinder; I will not hatch until both ellipses have a shared vertical short axis and no points.’
  3. Neither sentence mentions talent or a score.

Together

Hint 1: Checkable

A plan is checkable if, at the end of the sitting, you can say yes or no.

Elective · Poetry 2: caesura, enjambment, and the volta

30 min · Method: terms taught, then close reading of a public-domain sonnet · After Day 10 (meter and iambic pentameter)

Learn

Key terms

caesura and enjambment
Definition
A caesura is a pause inside a line of verse, often (but not only) in the middle, and often marked by punctuation. Enjambment is the running-on of a sentence or clause across the end of a line, without a pause at the line break.
Origin
Caesura is from Latin caesūra (‘a cutting’), from caesus, the perfect participle of caedere (‘to cut’). Enjambment is borrowed from French enjambement.
Example
In Shakespeare’s Sonnet 73, the thought of the hanging leaves runs from ‘When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang / Upon those boughs…’ across the line end: that is enjambment. ‘Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang’ can show a mid-line pause after ‘choirs’ if the text is punctuated that way — a caesura.
Not this
A caesura is not a stanza break, and enjambment is not ‘the poet forgot to punctuate’. Both are tools for controlling breath and sense against the metrical line you already scanned on Day 10.
Connects to
Iambic pentameter is still the meter of the sonnet you will read. These terms describe how the sentence sits on that meter.
volta
Definition
The turn in a sonnet: the place where the argument or the figure shifts. In a Petrarchan sonnet this often comes after the first eight lines. In a Shakespearean sonnet (abab cdcd efef gg) it is often felt at the closing couplet, and sometimes earlier, around line 9. Look for a ‘but’, a ‘yet’, or a change of thought; do not treat a line number as a superstition.
Origin
From Italian volta (‘turn’). The English poetic sense is a turning point in a poem, most often a sonnet.
Example
In Sonnet 18, many readers feel a turn at ‘But thy eternal summer shall not fade’ (line 9), when the poem stops listing summer’s faults and starts claiming that the beloved’s summer will not fade, in verse.
Not this
A volta is not a change of typography, and it is not automatically ‘line 9 in every sonnet’. If you cannot hear a turn, say so, and point to what the poem does instead.
Connects to
Day 10’s meter tells you how the line is built. The volta tells you how the sonnet’s thought is built.

Day 10 taught iambic pentameter (five iambs, a weak-strong pattern, with license). Today adds how a sentence and an argument sit on those lines.

Caesura. A pause inside the line. Read the line aloud and mark where you have to stop, even briefly. Punctuation often helps; it is not the only clue.

Enjambment. The sense runs over the line end. The line is still a line (the meter still turns there); the sentence does not.

The sonnet and the volta. A Shakespearean sonnet has fourteen lines, usually iambic pentameter, rhyming abab cdcd efef gg. The volta is the turn of thought. In Sonnet 18 (public domain), the first lines compare the beloved to a summer’s day and then list summer’s faults. Line 9 begins: “But thy eternal summer shall not fade.” That but is a candidate for the turn: the poem moves from comparison and decay to a claim that verse will keep the summer from fading.

A note on Sonnet 73. If you also look at 73 (‘That time of year thou mayst in me behold’), you can hunt caesura and enjambment there as well. The Check can be answered from Sonnet 18 alone.

Remember

Caesura: a pause inside the line. Enjambment: the sentence runs over the line end. Volta: the turn in a sonnet, often at line 9 or at the couplet in a Shakespearean sonnet — find it by the thought, not by a superstition. Sonnet 18’s ‘But thy eternal summer shall not fade’ is a standard place to hear the turn.
