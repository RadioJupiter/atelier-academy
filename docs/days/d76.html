Latin · Capitulum XIV: uter, neuter, alter, and uterque

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XIV, Novus Diēs, begins. Original sentences only; the book’s story is read in the book, not copied here.

Learn

Key terms

uterque
Definition
Uterque (utraque, utrumque) means each of the two, or both taken as a pair. It refers to a pair, and it often takes a singular verb: uterque puer dormit, each of the two boys is asleep.
Example
Uterque discipulus legit. — Each of the two students is reading.
Not this
Not ambo used as a first look here, and not a word for a group of three or more. Uter, by itself, asks ‘which of the two?’; uterque answers ‘each of the two.’
Connects to
Capitulum XIV, Novus Diēs, begins with these pair words. Grammar follows the usual Latin facts (compare Allen and Greenough on uter and uterque). Sentences here are original, not copied from Ørberg.
alter
Definition
Alter (altera, alterum) means the one, or the other, of two.
Example
Alter puer legit, alter scribit. — One boy reads, the other writes.
Not this
Not alius, which is ‘another’ when more than two are in view. Alter stays with a pair.
Connects to
Uter asks which of the two; alter picks one of the two; uterque takes each of the two.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXIV: the area of a parallelogram

60 min · Geometry

Learn

Key terms

area of a parallelogram
Definition
The area of a parallelogram is the base times the corresponding perpendicular height: A = b × h. The height is perpendicular to the chosen base, not a slanted side.
Example
A parallelogram with base 8 and height 5 has area 8 × 5 = 40.
Not this
Not the product of two slanted sides, and not a first look at the shape itself (the parallelogram as a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel was taught on Day 14). The new job is the area.
Connects to
Day 14 named the parallelogram; Day 15 used the rectangle as a special parallelogram. Today’s area is the same product b × h that a rectangle uses, once the height is perpendicular to the base.

A parallelogram is already known from Day 14: both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. Today’s job is its area.

You can slide the slanted end across and rearrange a parallelogram into a rectangle with the same base and the same perpendicular height. The area does not change under that slide. A rectangle’s area is base times height, so a parallelogram’s area is the same product.

A = b × h, where h is perpendicular to the chosen base.

Classroom lengths, labeled as a diagram: base 8, height 5, area 8 × 5 = 40. The slanted side is longer than 5; multiplying base by a slanted side would not be the area.

This is the concrete–pictorial–abstract path already used for the triangle last week: move or imagine the pieces, draw the height, then write A = b × h.

Remember

A = b × h for a parallelogram, with h perpendicular to the chosen base. A slanted side is not the height. The shape itself is not new; the area formula is the new work.

Key terms: area of a parallelogram.

  3. A slanted side is longer than the height, so that product would be larger than the actual area.

Writing & Rhetoric · Circular reasoning

45 min · Writing

Learn

Key terms

circular reasoning
Definition
Circular reasoning is a flaw in which a claim is supported by a restatement of the same claim, or by a premise that already assumes what was supposed to be shown. It is a kind of informal fallacy, not merely a false statement.
Example
Invented (labeled invented): ‘This rule is fair because it is a fair rule.’ The because-clause restates the claim instead of giving a new ground.
Not this
Not a merely false claim (a claim can be false and still be argued in a straight line). Not last week’s straw man, false dilemma, or ad hominem, which are different flaws.
Connects to
Last week a fallacy was distinguished from a merely false claim, and three named flaws were taught. Today adds a fourth: the argument goes in a circle instead of offering independent grounds (Toulmin’s grounds, already taught).

Last week a fallacy was a flaw in reasoning, not merely a false claim. Straw man, false dilemma, and ad hominem remain those three jobs; they are not retaught as first looks.

Today’s flaw is circular reasoning: the support for a claim is only the claim again, or a premise that already assumes the conclusion.

Invented classroom examples, labeled invented:

  • ‘This rule is fair because it is a fair rule.’ The because-clause is the same claim in other words. There is no new ground.
  • ‘She is reliable, because you can always rely on her.’ Same circle.

A straight argument would offer a ground that can be inspected apart from the wording of the claim (the Toulmin job from week 13): a fact, an example, a sourced line.

A circle is still a fallacy even if the claim happens to be true. The flaw is in the support, not only in the truth-value of the sentence.

Remember

Circular reasoning supports a claim with a restatement of the claim, or with a premise that already assumes the conclusion. It is a fallacy, not merely a false claim. Invented examples are labeled invented.

Key terms: circular reasoning.

Physics · Oscillation, period, and frequency: a first look

45 min · Physics · oscillations begin; the simple-pendulum formula is tomorrow’s job

Learn

Key terms

oscillation
Definition
An oscillation is a repeating back-and-forth motion about a central position, such as a hanging bob swinging to and fro, or a mass on a spring moving in and out, once the motion repeats.
Example
A hanging bob that swings left, through the middle, to the right, and back, and then does the same again, is oscillating.
Not this
Not a single shove that does not repeat, and not a trip that goes forward without returning. Not tomorrow’s formula for a simple pendulum, which is not yet today’s job.
Connects to
Term 2’s physics plan includes oscillations after rotation. Last week’s equilibrium is a related but different job: a balance or a tip, not a repeating swing.
period (oscillation)
Definition
The period, T, is the time for one complete cycle of an oscillation — for example, the time from a leftmost position out and back to that same leftmost position.
Example
If a hanging bob takes 2 seconds to go out and return to the same point in the same direction, its period is 2 s. A particular classroom timing, if measured, is invented and labeled invented unless a source is named.
Not this
Not frequency, which is how many cycles happen in one second. Not a calendar period, such as a month, from another subject.
Connects to
Frequency f and period T are reciprocals: f = 1/T, when T is in seconds and f is in cycles per second. That relation is taught today; the pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g) waits until Wednesday, with its OpenStax source.

An oscillation is a motion that goes back and forth and repeats. A hanging bob that swings, or a quiet mass on a spring, is a first picture. A single shove that does not repeat is not an oscillation.

The period, T, is the time for one complete cycle. The frequency, f, is how many cycles happen in one second. They are reciprocals: f = 1/T. If T is 2 s, f is ½ cycle per second.

Classroom timings, labeled invented: a bob that takes 2 s for one cycle has period 2 s. That 2 s is not an official laboratory certificate, and it is not yet taken from a formula.

Tomorrow will use OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.4, for a simple pendulum: the period depends on length and on g, not on mass, for small swings. Today, do not use that formula yet. Today the job is the words oscillation, period, and frequency, and the relation f = 1/T.

The simple-pendulum facts used this week follow OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.4, “The Simple Pendulum”: for amplitudes less than about 15°, T = 2π√(L/g); the period depends on the length and on the acceleration due to gravity, and is independent of mass. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum (CC BY). Classroom lengths and timings, if given as numbers of a particular hanging bob, are invented and labeled invented. They are not official laboratory certificates. The value of g is the already-taught Earth figure, about 9.8 m/s², not a new official measurement.

Remember

An oscillation is a repeating back-and-forth motion. Period T is the time for one cycle; frequency f is cycles per second; f = 1/T. The simple-pendulum formula is Wednesday’s job. Invented timings are labeled invented.

half-cylinder (Dewing)
Definition

Atelier · The light family and the shadow family, on the head

90 min · Atelier · apply already-taught value families to the head; not a first look at core shadow

Learn

Key terms

light family and shadow family
Definition
The light family is the set of values on the surfaces that still face the light (highlight and midtone). The shadow family is the set of values on the surfaces that have turned away (core shadow, reflected light, and, if it is present, the cast shadow). The two families are kept distinct, rather than averaged into one middle gray.
Example
On the head, the forehead plane that faces the lamp stays in the light family; the side of the cheek that has turned away stays in the shadow family, even if a little reflected light lifts it.
Not this
Not a first look at highlight, midtone, core shadow, or reflected light, which were taught on the egg and the sphere (Days 6 and 41–43). Not a new lamp position mid-sitting. Not last week’s feature-by-feature value pass treated as if the families themselves were new words then; last week placed value on features already sited. Today the new job is to group those values into two families on the whole head.
Connects to
Week 15 ran a value pass across already-placed features. Today those values are read as two families, so the head still turns in one light, as the sphere did, rather than becoming a set of separate little studies.

Highlight, midtone, core shadow, and reflected light are already taught (the egg, the sphere). They are not first looks today.

Today those already-taught values are grouped into two families on the head:

  • The light family: surfaces that still face the lamp — highlight and midtone.
  • The shadow family: surfaces that have turned away — core shadow, any reflected light, and the cast shadow if the lamp and the stand give one.

The families are kept distinct. A midtone in the light is not painted as if it were a core shadow; a reflected light in the shadow is not lifted until it joins the light family. Averaging both sides into one middle gray flattens the head.

Last week’s value pass on the sockets, the nose, the mouth, and the ear remains; today you look across them and ask which family each plane belongs to, in one held light (Day 42’s habit). Do not move the lamp mid-sitting. No living, named sitter is required: the same cast, plate, or careful self-study already in use is enough. The site holds no identifying personal information.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of tener

25 min · Spanish · a first look at the uv-stem preterite of tener. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

preterite of tener
Definition
The preterite of tener is tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvisteis, tuvieron: a uv-stem, not the regular -er pattern of comer, and not the fui set of ser and ir already taught last week.
Example
Ayer tuve una clase. — Yesterday I had a class. (Invented classroom sentence, labeled invented if it is only a drill.)
Not this
Not the present tengo, and not hice from last week’s hacer. Not a first look at the preterite as a tense (regular -ar, -er, and -ir, and ser, ir, and hacer, were already taught).
Connects to
Last week’s fui and hice remain those verbs. Today begins a uv-stem set with tener. Estar and andar, which share the same uv pattern, wait until tomorrow.

The preterite tense is already in use. Last week ser and ir shared fui…fueron, and hacer had hice…hizo. Today is the preterite of tener.

Word How to say it Meaning
tuve TOO-beh I had (preterite of tener)
tuviste too-BEES-teh you had (sing., informal)
tuvo TOO-boh he/she/you formal had
tuvimos too-BEE-mohs we had
tuvisteis too-BEES-teys you had (plural, informal)
tuvieron too-BYE-rohn they/you formal plural had

The stem is tuv-: tuve, tuviste, tuvo, tuvimos, tuvisteis, tuvieron. That is not the regular -er pattern (comí), and it is not fui.

Original drill sentences, invented and labeled invented as drills:

  • Ella tuvo tiempo.

Elective · Logic: Cesare, a second-figure EAE syllogism

30 min · Logic · a first look at the second figure; Ferio and the first figure remain already taught

Learn

Key terms

second figure
Definition
In a second-figure syllogism, the middle term is the predicate of both premises. That is a different placement from the first figure (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio), already taught, in which the middle term is the subject of one premise and the predicate of the other.
Example
No fish are mammals; all whales are mammals; therefore no whales are fish. The middle term, mammals, is the predicate of both premises.
Not this
Not a first look at Celarent, which is first-figure EAE. Cesare uses the same vowel pattern EAE, but the middle term sits in a different place. Not a new look at informal fallacies, which belong to writing this week and last week.
Connects to
Week 13–15 taught the first-figure moods Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio. Today opens the second figure with Cesare. The invented classroom terms (fish, mammals, whales) are labeled invented as a logic example, not as a biology essay.
Cesare
Definition
Cesare is a second-figure syllogism of the form EAE: a universal negative premise, a universal affirmative premise, and a universal negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises.
Example
No fish are mammals (E); all whales are mammals (A); therefore no whales are fish (E). Invented as a classroom form, labeled invented as an example.
Not this
Not Celarent, which is also EAE but first figure (middle term as subject of the first premise and predicate of the second, in the usual school order). The name Cesare is a medieval mnemonic; the vowels E-A-E are the mood.
Connects to
Celarent (week 13) already used EAE in the first figure. Today’s job is to hear the same vowels in a new figure, and to check the middle term’s place.

The first figure is already complete in this course: Barbara, Celarent, Darii, and Ferio (through last week). Those moods are not first looks today.

The second figure places the middle term as the predicate of both premises. Cesare is the EAE mood in that figure:

  • E: No fish are mammals.
  • A: All whales are mammals.
  • E: Therefore no whales are fish.

The middle term is mammals, the predicate of both premises. The conclusion is universal negative. The fish–whale wording is an invented classroom example, labeled invented as a form; it is not a new biology source.

Celarent is also EAE, but first figure: the middle term is not in this second-figure place. Same vowels, different figure. That distinction is the point of today.
