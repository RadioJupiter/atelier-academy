Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 3: capiō, accipiō, faciō in the present

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VIII · Method: a listed set, then original sentences

Learn

Key terms

third-conjugation -iō verbs
Definition
A small group of third-conjugation verbs whose first principal part ends in -iō (today: capiō, accipiō, faciō). In the present they show an i in several forms (capiō, capis, capit, capimus, capitis, capiunt) and -iunt in the third-person plural, not *capunt.
Example
Mārcus rosam capat is wrong; Mārcus rosam capit is ‘Marcus takes / seizes the rose.’ Puerī rosam accipiunt — they receive the rose.
Not this
Not first-conjugation -at (dat, amat), and not a regular third without the extra i (legit, legunt — no *legiunt). Learn this set as a listed set.
Connects to
Capitulum VIII introduces this type. Original sentences only. The imperative and further tenses can wait; today is the present indicative.
Word How to say it Meaning
capiō KAH-pee-oh I take, I seize
capit KAH-pit takes
capiunt KAH-pee-unt they take
accipiō ahk-KIP-pee-oh I receive, I accept
accipit ahk-KIP-pit receives
accipiunt ahk-KIP-pee-unt they receive
faciō FAH-kee-oh I make, I do
facit FAH-kit makes, does
faciunt FAH-kee-unt they make, they do

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

A listed present. capiō, capis, capit, capimus, capitis, capiunt. The same pattern: accipiō… accipiunt; faciō… faciunt.

What to notice. The third-person plural is capiunt, not capunt and not capent. The third-person singular is capit, not capat.

Original sentences: Mārcus rosam capit. Iūlia dōnum accipit. Puerī labōrem faciunt. Quis hunc librum accipit?

Read the section of Capitulum VIII that uses these verbs, then practice on the original sentences.

Remember

capiō, accipiō, faciō are third-conjugation -iō verbs. Present: capiō, capis, capit, capimus, capitis, capiunt. capit, not capat; capiunt, not capunt.

Mathematics · Geometry XIV: mixed circle problems

60 min · Method: name the hypothesis, then the theorem; figures that combine Days 21–24

Learn

Key terms

mixed circle problems
Definition
Problems that use more than one circle fact from these two weeks — inscribed and central angles, the angle in a semicircle, cyclic quadrilaterals, or intersecting chords — in a single figure, with the reason named at each step.
Example
If AB is a diameter and C, D lie on the circle, then ∠ACB = ∠ADB = 90°, and if chords AC and BD meet at E, the intersecting-chords theorem still applies in the same figure.
Not this
Not a license to use every theorem at once without checking its hypothesis (a diameter, the same arc, a cyclic quad, a meeting point inside the circle).
Connects to
Yesterday chose AP or GP by a test. Today you choose a circle theorem by a test of the figure.

A habit for a mixed figure. Label what is given: a diameter? a center? a cyclic quadrilateral? a crossing inside the circle? Then name the theorem before you compute.

A worked mix. AB is a diameter. C is on the circle, ∠BAC = 30°. Then ∠ACB = 90°, so ∠ABC = 60°. The central angle on arc BC is twice the inscribed angle ∠BAC, so ∠BOC = 60° if O is the center — actually ∠BAC stands on arc BC, yes, central = 60°. (Check: ∠ABC = 60° stands on arc AC, central on arc AC is 120°, and 60°+120°+180° at the diameter? The angle at O on the diameter is a straight line. The two central angles on arcs AC and BC should sum to 180°: 120° + 60° = 180°, consistent.)

Chords in the same figure. If another chord through a point E on AC is given with segments, use products only at that interior point.

Remember

Name what the figure gives (diameter, same arc, cyclic quad, interior crossing), then name the theorem. Combined figures are allowed; unchecked theorems are not.

Key terms: mixed circle problems.

Writing & Rhetoric · Evidence and attribution, in three paragraphs

45 min · Method: Day 18’s attribution rule, reviewed inside the new outline; précis as an alternative to a long quote

Learn

Key terms

attribution (review)
Definition
Naming a source when you quote or précis it, so a reader can tell whose words or whose claim you are using. In this course that means a short introduction and a precise pointer (author, work, book or year as you have them) — not a decorative name-drop, and not a long academic bibliography unless one is asked for.
Example
Butler (1898), Odyssey Book 9, in a clause before a short quotation; or “Livy, writing centuries later, reports that…” before a tradition.
Not this
Not a quotation left raw, and not “science says” with no source. A theorem can be attributed too: Euclid, Elements III.20.
Connects to
Day 18 taught quotations and attribution. Today is a review in the three-paragraph setting, including a précis of a source as an alternative to a long quote.

The rule, restated. If the words or the claim come from a source, say so before or as you use them. Then explain, in your own sentences, why the excerpt or précis is there.

Prefer a short quotation or a précis. A long paste is not support; it is unpaid labor for the reader. Public-domain lines from Butler or from the Declaration may be used sparingly, as before.

A caution for this week’s sources. Livy, Polybius, Caesar, and a later marble copy all need a date or a class-of-source tag, not only a name. “Polybius says Rome was a mixed constitution” is weaker than “Polybius, in the second century BCE, analyzes Rome as a mixed constitution.”

Remember

Introduce and point to a source; explain why it is there. Prefer a short quotation or a précis. Tag late or copied sources with a date or a class of source.

Key terms: attribution (review).

  2. A repair: “Polybius, writing in the second century BCE, analyzes Roman government as a mixed constitution, with magistrates, Senate, and people each holding a share.”

Physics · Mechanical energy, conserved when friction does no work

50 min · Method: KE + PE constant in the no-friction model; v = √(2gh) from rest, after it is shown

Learn

Key terms

mechanical energy (no friction)
Definition
In this course’s first conservation model, mechanical energy is the sum KE + PE, with PE = m g h near the Earth. When no friction (and no other force that takes energy out of this account) does work, the sum is constant: ½ m v² + m g h stays the same along the path.
Example
A 2 kg object starts from rest at h = 2.5 m: PE = 49 J, KE = 0. At the floor (h = 0), PE = 0, so KE = 49 J, and v = 7 m/s, because ½ × 2 × v² = 49, v² = 49, v = 7.
Not this
Not a claim that energy is “used up” when the object stops in your hand, and not a license to ignore friction when friction is actually present. That case is Friday’s caution and is already previewed: friction’s work decreases the mechanical-energy sum as this course is keeping it.
Connects to
Mass cancels when you go from m g h to ½ v² (if you start from rest): v = √(2 g h). You may use that once you have shown it.
conservation (mechanical, first model)
Definition
The statement that KE + PE is constant when only gravity (in the PE = m g h model) and forces that do no work (such as a normal force perpendicular to the motion) act. It is a first model, not a complete account of every form of energy.
Example
From a height of 2.5 m, starting at rest, speed at the bottom is 7 m/s in the no-friction model, independent of mass.
Not this
Not “energy is always the same number on every line of every problem,” and not a refusal of the work–energy theorem: conservation of mechanical energy is that theorem, plus PE, when gravity’s work is already stored as ΔPE.
Connects to
Wednesday’s sitting is this model. Friday’s two-week check will include a problem where friction does work, so the sum is not constant.

The bookkeeping. Write KE + PE at two points, with one reference level. If the model’s conditions hold, the sums are equal.

The clean example. m = 2 kg, h = 2.5 m, at rest: 0 + 49 = 49 J. At h = 0: ½(2)v² + 0 = 49, v = 7 m/s.

Mass cancels, when it should. From rest, m g h = ½ m v², so v = √(2 g h) = √(2 × 9.8 × 2.5) = √49 = 7 m/s, for any mass in this model.

A mid-height check. At h = 0.5 m, PE = 2 × 9.8 × 0.5 = 9.8 J, so KE = 39.2 J, v = √(39.2) = √(196/5) = 14/√5 m/s (leave exact, or about 6.3 m/s, labeled approximate). Prefer a height that stays clean when you can: at h = 0, v = 7 m/s is the check to know by heart for these numbers.

Remember

In the no-friction model, KE + PE is constant. From rest, m g h = ½ m v², so v = √(2 g h). Pick one zero of height. Friday will treat friction as a case where this sum is not constant.

Key terms: mechanical energy (no friction), conservation (mechanical, first model).

History · Late Republic sources: Caesar as a participant, and Cicero’s letters

45 min · Method: primary and interested; closeness does not cancel advocacy; no full civil-war narrative

Learn

Key terms

Caesar as a participant source
Definition
Julius Caesar’s Commentaries (including the Gallic War) are a primary source in the strict sense that the author was a commander writing about campaigns he led — and they are also advocacy: the writer has a stake in how he is seen in Rome. Both facts must be in the sentence, not only the first.
Example
A crossing of a river in the Gallic War is evidence of what Caesar wrote for a Roman public, and it may be evidence of a campaign, but it is not a neutral staff log.
Not this
Not a late antiquarian only (Caesar is closer to his own wars than Livy is to the kings), and not “therefore false.” A participant source can be close and still shaped.
Connects to
Cicero’s letters are another late-Republican window: also primary, also written from a position. This lesson uses the two as a class of source, not as a full life of either man.
late Republic (sources)
Definition
The first-century BCE crisis of the Republic, for which we have unusually rich literary sources — speeches, letters, commentaries — written by participants and contemporaries, alongside later narrative. The wealth of sources does not remove the need to date and to place each voice.
Example
Cicero’s letters can date an alliance or a fear to a year; they cannot be treated as a complete, impartial record of the Roman people.
Not this
Not “the Republic was always this well documented” (early Rome was not), and not a claim that this lesson has taught the whole civil-war narrative.
Connects to
Friday’s synthesis will ask how we know the Republic, from early law through these late, louder voices.

A change in the evidence, not only in the politics. For the late Republic we have writings by people who were there: Caesar on his campaigns, Cicero in letters and speeches, and others. That is a different situation from Livy’s early books, or even from a fragmentary legal tradition.

The habit does not retire. A participant is close, and a participant is interested. Caesar’s Commentaries explain a war and also defend a career. Cicero’s letters think, persuade, and sometimes posture. Later narratives (Plutarch, Appian, and others — named only as later, not assigned in full today) still need dates.

What this lesson will not do. It will not narrate every campaign or every civil war. It will not pretend to have settled Caesar’s motives. The skill is to tag a source as participant-and-advocacy, or as a letter from a position, and to use it accordingly.

Remember

Caesar’s Commentaries are primary and also advocacy. Cicero’s letters are primary and also written from a position. Late-Republican sources are unusually rich; they still need to be dated and placed. This is not a complete narrative of the civil wars.

Key terms: Caesar as a participant source, late Republic (sources).

Watch me

  2. The second clause goes too far: the same text is also advocacy, written for a Roman public and a career.
  3. A repair: “Caesar’s Gallic War is a participant source, and it is also a defense of his conduct; both facts belong in any use of it.”

Hint 2: Position

Atelier · Lost-and-found edges on the head

90 min · Method: week-4 lost and found, applied to the same head; a few named decisions, then a look back

Learn

Key terms

lost-and-found on the head
Definition
The week-4 skill of losing an edge where values meet softly or nearly match, and finding an edge where a plane cuts clearly, now applied to the head: hair against a temple, jaw into neck, a lid into a socket, the far cheek into shadow.
Example
A jaw that is found at the chin’s corner and lost as it turns toward a close-valued neck is using the tool.
Not this
Not a smudge of the whole head, and not an outline equally hard all the way around the egg.
Connects to
Yesterday’s features remain. Today you choose a few edges on purpose, after a look at the subject, not from a rule that “portraits should have lost edges.”

Stand back. Half-close the eyes. Where does an edge actually disappear, or nearly? Where is it cut?

A few decisions, named. You might lose the far hairline into the shadow, find the near brow, lose the far jaw, find an accent at the inner lid. Write the decisions in a margin, then make them.

Do not lose structure. A lost edge is not an erased plane. The turn can still be there in a close value shift.

Remember

Lose an edge where values nearly match or the turn is slow; find an edge where the cut is clear. Name a few decisions. A lost edge is not an erased plane, and not a uniform outline.

Key terms: lost-and-found on the head.

Modern language · Spanish: telling the time

40 min · Method: Es la una / Son las dos, then cuarto, media, menos cuarto; present only

Learn

Key terms

telling time (present)
Definition
The present-tense pattern for the hour: ¿Qué hora es? Es la una (1:00), Son las dos (2:00 and the other hours). Minutes: y cuarto (15), y media (30), menos cuarto (15 before the next hour). Official 24-hour announcements can wait; today is the everyday pattern taught here.
Example
Son las tres y cuarto (3:15). Son las cinco y media (5:30). Son las siete menos cuarto (6:45). Es la una y media (1:30).
Not this
Not *Es las dos, and not *Son la una. The verb agrees with la una (singular) or las dos, las tres… (plural). This is still the present, not a past narrative tense.
Connects to

Art history · Hellenistic sculpture: the Nike of Samothrace, and the Laocoön with a date caution

40 min · Method: two type examples, looking first; dates as centuries or debated; fragments and copies named

Learn

Key terms

Nike of Samothrace
Definition
The marble “Winged Victory” now in the Louvre, a Hellenistic statue of the goddess Nike, usually dated to the second century BCE (often around the early second century; exact year is not settled, and this course will not invent one). It is a type example of Hellenistic movement and drapery, not a High Classical still stance like the Doryphoros type.
Example
The figure strides, wings open, drapery pulled against a body in motion — a contrast with the more contained High Classical athlete you met last week.
Not this
Not a complete statue in its first condition: the sculpture is fragmentary (the head is missing, among other losses), and it has a modern museum setting that is not the original terrace on Samothrace.
Connects to
Dates for Hellenistic works are often less tight than “447–432 BCE” for the Parthenon. Say “second century BCE” or “often dated around the early second century BCE,” not a fake exact year.
Laocoön (date caution)
Definition
The Vatican Laocoön group, a marble of Laocoön and his sons attacked by serpents, famous since its Renaissance rediscovery. Its date and whether it is a Hellenistic original or a later (even Roman) copy or version are argued; this course will not settle the argument, and it will not assign an exact year.
Example
You can describe the spiral movement, the pathos of the faces, and the group as a designed mass, while still writing “date and status as original or copy are debated.”
Not this
Not a secure fifth-century work, and not a photograph of a named sculptor’s workshop in a known month. Vivid feeling in the marble is not itself a date.
Connects to
Pliny mentions a Laocoön; connecting that sentence to this exact marble is part of the debate, not a completed proof in this lesson.

Hellenistic, as a contrast, not as a complete survey. After the High Classical fifth century, Hellenistic art (conventionally from the later fourth century toward the first) often shows stronger motion, more elaborate drapery, and — in some famous works — a more theatrical pathos. Two type examples, both with cautions, are enough today.

Nike of Samothrace. A winged Nike in motion, now in the Louvre, usually placed in the second century BCE, fragmentary, and displayed far from its original setting. Describe the stride and the drapery; date it carefully; name the losses.

The Laocoön group. A tightly wound group, intense faces, a famous later life in the Vatican. Then the caution: date and original-or-copy status are debated. Describe what you see; do not use the emotion of the scene as a dating method.

The same habit as last week. A museum photograph is not the ancient setting. A marble may be a later version. A conventional century is not an exact year.

Remember

The Nike of Samothrace is a fragmentary Hellenistic Nike, usually dated to the second century BCE, now in the Louvre. The Laocoön is a famous Vatican group whose date and original-or-copy status are debated. Describe first; do not invent an exact year; a museum view is not the original setting.
