Latin · The deponent perfect, used in short sentences

40 min · Method: yesterday’s locūtus sum and cōnātus sum in short classroom sentences, still contrasted with the present and with the absolute

Learn

Key terms

deponent perfect in use
Definition
The deponent perfect, yesterday’s first look, is used today in short sentences. The person of sum must match the subject, and the participle must agree in number. Ego locūtus sum. Tū locūtus es. Puer locūtus est. Nōs locūtī sumus. The present, loquor, remains a different tense. The absolute puerō locūtō remains a phrase without sum.
Example
Magister locūtus est; puerī silent. Nōs cōnātī sumus; tū cōnāris etiam nunc, if a present is wanted for a contrast.

Mathematics · Geometry LX: cosine of an acute angle, a first look

45 min · Method: cosine of an acute angle in a right triangle is the adjacent side over the hypotenuse. Yesterday’s sine is a contrast, not a first look. Degrees only

Learn

Key terms

cosine of an acute angle
Definition
In a right triangle, the cosine of an acute angle A, written cos A, is the length of the side adjacent to A, but not the hypotenuse, divided by the length of the hypotenuse. The ratio is the same for a given angle, by the already-taught similarity. In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3 and adjacent to 4, cos A = 4/5, while sin A, already taught, is 3/5. Cosine is a ratio, not a length. Degrees only; the radian and the unit circle remain reserved.
Example
The same 6-8-10 similar triangle has cos A = 8/10 = 4/5, matching 4/5 from the 3-4-5 triangle.
Not this
Not a first look at sine, already taught yesterday, or at similar triangles. Not the adjacent over the opposite, and not a unit-circle definition. Classroom lengths remain invented.
Connects to
Yesterday’s sine, and the already-taught similar right triangles.

Yesterday’s sine used the opposite side. Today a second ratio uses the adjacent side.

The cosine of an acute angle A, written cos A, is the adjacent side over the hypotenuse. Adjacent here means the leg that meets A, not the hypotenuse. The ratio is again the same for a given angle, because the right triangles are similar.

In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, A is opposite 3 and adjacent to 4, so cos A = 4/5, and sin A is still 3/5. A similar 6-8-10 triangle has cos A = 8/10 = 4/5. Do not swap the sides. The complementary relation, sin(90° − A) = cos A, waits until tomorrow. Degrees only.

Science · Three methods of heat transfer, a first look

40 min · Method: OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 14.4. Heat transfer by conduction, convection, and radiation, whenever there is a temperature difference. Last week’s latent heat is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

conduction, convection, and radiation
Definition
OpenStax 14.4 teaches that whenever there is a temperature difference, heat transfer occurs, and that every such process takes place by only three methods. Conduction is heat transfer through stationary matter by physical contact; OpenStax’s example is heat transferred between an electric burner and the bottom of a pan. Convection is heat transfer by the macroscopic movement of a fluid; OpenStax’s examples include a forced-air furnace and weather systems. Heat transfer by radiation occurs when microwaves, infrared radiation, visible light, or another form of electromagnetic radiation is emitted or absorbed; OpenStax’s obvious example is the warming of the Earth by the Sun. Quantitative rates, including a conduction formula, remain reserved in the following OpenStax modules.
Example
These are kinds of transfer, not official measured rates invented here. Thursday uses OpenStax’s fireplace, in which all three methods occur, with radiation responsible for most of the heat transferred into the room.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s latent heat, or at Lv of water, already taught. Not a first look at temperature itself. Not a quantitative conduction, convection, or radiation formula, which 14.4 names as later modules. OpenStax’s brief mention of thermal radiation from the human body is not used as a measured official rate, and is not a claim about a living person in this course.
Connects to
Last week’s phase-change work, as a contrast, and today’s first look at the three methods, from OpenStax 14.4.

Last week treated the energy required for a phase change. Today OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 14.4, turns to how heat is transferred whenever there is a temperature difference.

There are only three methods. Conduction is heat transfer through stationary matter by physical contact: an electric burner to the bottom of a pan, in OpenStax’s example. Convection is heat transfer by the macroscopic movement of a fluid: a forced-air furnace, or weather systems. Radiation is heat transfer by electromagnetic radiation, such as infrared or visible light: the warming of the Earth by the Sun.

These are kinds of transfer, not official measured rates. A formula for the rate of conduction, and the later modules that quantify each method, remain reserved. Thursday will use OpenStax’s fireplace, in which all three occur together.

Modern language · Spanish: the imperfect of ser, contrasted with iba and with fui

40 min · Method: a first look at the irregular imperfect of ser (era). Yesterday’s iba is a contrast. Ir and ser share fui, already taught, but not the imperfect

Learn

Key terms

imperfect of ser
Definition
The irregular imperfect of ser is era, eras, era, éramos, erais, eran. It is not iba, yesterday’s imperfect of ir, and it is not the already-taught preterite fui, which ir and ser do share. In this first look, era is used for what someone or something was, as an ongoing or habitual past state: Ella era estudiante, in an invented classroom sentence that does not identify a living person. Ayer ella fue, the already-taught preterite, is a completed event. Instruction remains in English.
Example
Cuando yo era joven, in an invented classroom sentence, not a claim about a living person. Nosotros éramos amigos. Yo iba is “I was going”; yo era is “I was.”
Not this
Not a first look at iba, or at fui, already taught. Not Thursday’s veía. Instruction remains in English. Classroom sentences do not identify a living person.
Connects to
Yesterday’s iba, the already-taught preterite fui, and today’s era.
