Latin · Agreement, and et or sed: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught adjective agreement, and et versus sed; Capitula I–X only

Learn

Key terms

agreement and et or sed, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of two already-taught habits: an adjective agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender, and et joins while sed contrasts. They are different jobs, still in Capitula I–X.
Example
puella laeta (nominative singular feminine, both). puer et puella rīdent. puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet.
Not this
Not a new chapter, and not a licence to use et where the sense is contrast, or to leave an adjective in a different case from its noun.
Connects to
Agreement and et / sed were taught in earlier weeks, including last week’s et and sed with an accusative object. Today gathers them.
Word How to say it Meaning

puer et puella rīdent POO-ehr et PWEH-lah REE-dent The boy and the girl laugh
puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet POO-ehr REE-det sed PWEH-lah nohn REE-det The boy laughs, but the girl does not laugh

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Two already-taught habits, gathered. Agreement: an adjective matches its noun in case, number, and gender (puella laeta, not puella laetus). et joins (puer et puella). sed contrasts (puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet).

They are different jobs. et does not do sed’s work. A nominative noun does not take an accusative adjective.

Still Capitula I–X only. Instruction in English. Original sentences, not a page copied from the companion book.

Mathematics · A tangent and a secant (Euclid III.36): a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught III.36, gathered; keep circle pictures separate

Learn

Key terms

a tangent and a secant, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of Euclid III.36, already taught: if from a point P outside a circle a tangent touches at T and a secant cuts at A (nearer) and B (farther), then PT² = PA × PB. Lengths are positive. This is not the intersecting-chords theorem (III.35), and not a quadratic inequality.
Example
If PT = 6 and PA = 4, then 36 = 4 × PB, so PB = 9. AB would be 5 if A sits between P and B, and AB is not the length multiplied by PA.
Not this
Not a sum (4 + 6 = 10 is not the theorem), not a quadratic’s roots, and not III.35.
Connects to
Week 11 taught III.36 as Geometry XXIII. Earlier weeks already taught other circle theorems (inscribed angles, the alternate segment, the incircle). Today closes the tangent-secant method and keeps those pictures separate.

Week 11 taught Euclid III.36: PT² = PA × PB. Today is a close of that method, not a first look.

The picture, already taught. From P outside the circle, a tangent touches at T. A secant cuts at A (nearer) and B (farther). The product of the two lengths along the secant, PA and PB, equals the square of the tangent length.

A numerical check, already used. PT = 6, PA = 4 → PB = 9. PB is the whole secant from P to B, not the chord AB (AB = 9 − 4 = 5, if A is between P and B).

Keep the pictures separate. Intersecting chords (III.35) are a different figure. A quadratic inequality is a different figure. Equal tangents and the incircle (already taught) are a different figure again. Do not merge them into a hybrid theorem.

Remember

III.36: PT² = PA × PB. Lengths are positive. PB is P to the farther point, not the chord AB. Not a sum, not III.35, not a quadratic inequality.

Key terms: a tangent and a secant, a Term 1 close.

Writing & Rhetoric · An attributed quotation: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: quote the already-set pine extract and attribute Firth; no new extract

Learn

Key terms

an attributed quotation, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the already-taught habit of quoting only a set extract and attributing it: the words themselves, plus the source. A paraphrase is not a quotation. Firth’s English is not Pliny’s Latin, and Dryden’s English is not Virgil’s Latin.
Example
“Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else…” (Firth, 1900, Attalus text).
Not this
Not a later reserved part of the letter, not an unattributed block, and not a claim that Pliny wrote these English words.
Connects to
Week 11 already set this pine extract and practised a short attributed quotation from it. Today gathers the habit, still on that extract only.

A quotation is the words themselves, in quotation marks or a block, plus the attribution. A paraphrase is not a quotation. Today is a close of that habit, on the pine-tree extract already set in week 11. No new extract is opened.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified):

Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.

The English is Firth’s, 1900 (Attalus notes that a few words and phrases have been modified). It is not Pliny’s Latin. The set extract does not name Pompeii, and it does not give a death toll. Later of the letter remains reserved.

Remember

A quotation is the set words plus the attribution. Firth 1900, not Pliny’s Latin. No Pompeii and no death toll from this extract. No new extract.

Music · A close and a pause: authentic and half cadences, a Term 1 close

30 min · Method: already-taught V–I close and pause on V, gathered; major key; I, IV, and V only

Learn

Key terms

a close and a pause, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of two already-taught common-practice cadence types in a major key, with I, IV, and V only: an authentic cadence (V–I) can close; a half cadence ends on V and is a pause, not an end. A deceptive cadence can still wait.
Example
In C major, V–I is G–B–D to C–E–G (a close). I–V, or IV–V, stopping on G–B–D, is a half cadence (a pause).
Not this
Not a claim that every stop in any style is a half cadence, and not a first look at a deceptive cadence.
Connects to

Two cadence types have already been taught, in a major key, with I, IV, and V only. Today gathers them.

An authentic cadence, already taught on Day 27: V moves to I, a close.

A half cadence, already taught last week: the phrase ends on V, a pause, not an end. In C major, I–V or IV–V, stopping on G–B–D, is a half cadence.

A deceptive cadence can still wait. This is a common-practice school pattern, not a law for every style. Cadence as a word keeps its earlier origin card; today does not invent a new etymology.

Remember

Authentic cadence: V–I, a close. Half cadence: a pause on V, not an end. Major key; I, IV, and V only. A deceptive cadence can still wait.

Key terms: a close and a pause, a Term 1 close.

Watch me

In C major, a phrase moves I–V and stops. What kind of cadence is that, and why is it not authentic?

  1. It is a half cadence: it ends on V (G–B–D in C major).
  2. An authentic cadence would move V to I.
  3. The stop on V is a pause, not an end.
