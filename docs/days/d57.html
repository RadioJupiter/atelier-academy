Latin · Agreement, and et or sed

40 min · Review: an adjective agrees with its noun; et joins, sed contrasts · Capitula I–X only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • et and sed — Day 51
  • nominative — Day 8

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Word How to say it Meaning
puella laeta rosam habet PWEH-lah LYE-tah ROH-sahm HAH-bet The happy girl has a rose
puer et puella rīdent POO-ehr et PWEH-lah REE-dent The boy and the girl laugh
puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet POO-ehr REE-det sed PWEH-lah nohn REE-det The boy laughs, but the girl does not laugh

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

An adjective matches its noun in case, number, and gender (puella laeta, not puella laetus). et joins (puer et puella). sed contrasts (puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet). Don’t use et when the sense is contrast, and don’t leave an adjective in a different case from its noun.

Instruction is in English. Original sentences only; we stay in Capitula I–X.

Remember

An adjective agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender. et joins; sed contrasts.

Watch me

Correct the slip *puella laetus rosam habet, and say why.

  1. Puella laeta rosam habet.
  2. puella is feminine nominative singular, so the adjective is laeta, not laetus.

Together

Join, then contrast: the boy laughs; the girl does not laugh.

Hint 1: Join or contrast?

Here the two do different things, so sed is the contrast-word.

Hint 2: The contrast

Puer rīdet, sed puella nōn rīdet.

Check

Use agreement and et or sed.

  1. Write: “The happy girl has a rose.”
  2. Write: “The boy and the girl laugh.”

Exercises

  1. Why is *puer et puella nōn rīdet a problem if both are subjects of one verb?
  2. When do you choose sed rather than et?
  3. Copy two model sentences.

Mathematics · A tangent and a secant (Euclid III.36)

40 min · Review of III.36 · keep circle pictures separate

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • secant — Day 52
  • the tangent-secant theorem — Day 52

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

You already know Euclid III.36: PT² = PA × PB. Today you use that theorem again.

From P outside the circle, a tangent touches at T. A secant cuts at A (nearer) and B (farther). The product of the two lengths along the secant, PA and PB, equals the square of the tangent length.

A numerical check you have seen before. PT = 6, PA = 4 → PB = 9. PB is the whole secant from P to B, not the chord AB (AB = 9 − 4 = 5, if A is between P and B).

Keep the pictures separate. Intersecting chords (III.35), a quadratic inequality, and the incircle are different figures. Don’t turn them into one hybrid theorem.

Remember

III.36: PT² = PA × PB. Lengths are positive. PB is P to the farther point, not the chord AB. This is a product, not a sum, and not III.35.

Watch me

PT = 6, PA = 4. Find PB, and say why the answer is not 10.

  1. PT² = 36 = 4 × PB, so PB = 9.
  2. 10 would be a sum, 4 + 6, which is not the theorem.
  3. AB, if A is between P and B, is 5, and is not the length multiplied by PA.

Together

PT = 8, PB = 16. Find PA. Check that PA is nearer than PB.

Hint 1: The product

64 = PA × 16.

Hint 2: Nearer

PA = 4, and 4 < 16, so A is still nearer than B.

Check

Use III.36 only.

  1. PA = 3, PB = 12. Find PT.
  2. PT = 10, PA = 5. Find PB.

Exercises

  1. PT = 5, PA = 4. Find PB, and then AB if A is between P and B.
  2. Why is the quantity PT², not PT, the one that matches PA × PB?
  3. Why must you not use this theorem on the roots of (x − 4)(x − 9) = 0 just because 4 and 9 appear?

Writing & Rhetoric · An attributed quotation

40 min · Quote the set pine extract and attribute Firth · no new extract

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a short attributed quotation from Firth’s Pliny — Day 52
  • quotation — Day 18
  • the pine-tree cloud — Day 53

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

A quotation is the words themselves, in quotation marks or a block, plus the attribution. A paraphrase is not a quotation. Today you practice that habit on the pine-tree extract already set in week 11. No new extract is opened.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified):

Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.

The English is Firth’s, 1900 (Attalus notes that a few words and phrases have been modified). It is not Pliny’s Latin. The set extract does not name Pompeii, and it does not give a death toll. Later of the letter remains reserved.

Remember

A quotation is the set words plus the attribution. Firth 1900, not Pliny’s Latin. No Pompeii and no death toll from this extract.

Watch me

Quote the likeness of the cloud to a pine-tree, and attribute the translation.

  1. Quote: the cloud “more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.”
  2. Attribution: J. B. Firth, 1900 (Attalus text, which notes some wording has been modified).
  3. Do not add a later reserved scene as if it were in the quotation.

Together

Why is “Pliny wrote ‘pine-tree’” a miss, even if the Latin letter is real?

Hint 1: The English

These English words are Firth’s, 1900.

Hint 2: The habit

A quotation of this extract must attribute the translation, not treat the English as Pliny’s own wording.

Check

Stay with the set pine extract.

  1. Quote the discovery that the mountain was Vesuvius, and attribute Firth.
  2. Name two things this extract does not give.

Exercises

  1. Write the attribution in a form you could place after a block quotation.
  2. How is a paraphrase of the pine likeness different from a quotation?
  3. Why must a quotation of this extract not add Stabiae?

Science · How we see

40 min · Review of the seeing module · NEI wording · no diopters and no classroom diagnosis

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • myopia — Day 47
  • hyperopia — Day 47
  • astigmatism — Day 49
  • presbyopia — Day 49
  • accommodation (eye) — Day 37
  • corrective lenses, as a tool — Day 52

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

How we see was taught across Term 1. Today you review the method; Thursday is the examination.

The eye has been taught as a kind of camera, with accommodation as a change of the lens. Refractive errors, from the National Eye Institute page already used: the shape of the eye keeps light from focusing correctly on the retina. Four common types, in the NEI wording: myopia, far-away objects look blurry; hyperopia, nearby objects look blurry; astigmatism, far-away and nearby objects look blurry or distorted; presbyopia, hard for middle-aged and older adults to see things up close, common in adults 45 and older. Causes named by the NEI include eyeball length, corneal shape, and aging of the lens.

Eyeglasses or contact lenses are named as a treatment. Surgery is listed by the NEI and is not taught here. This course invents no diopter numbers and makes no classroom diagnosis.

We will not invent cell counts, and we will not turn a classmate into an example diagnosis.

Remember

How we see: the eye as a kind of camera; accommodation; NEI refractive errors in the NEI wording; lenses as a tool. No invented diopters, no classroom diagnosis. Thursday is the examination.

Watch me

In the NEI wording, which error is distant blur, and which is near blur?

  1. Myopia: far-away objects look blurry.
  2. Hyperopia: nearby objects look blurry.
  3. Neither is a classroom diagnosis in this course.

Together

What is a corrective lens in this module, and what will this course not do with it?

Hint 1: The tool

Eyeglasses or contact lenses, named by the NEI as a treatment.

Hint 2: The limit

No invented diopter numbers, no prescription, and no classroom diagnosis. Surgery is listed by the NEI and is not taught.

Check

Use the seeing module from this lesson.

  1. What is astigmatism, in the NEI wording taught in this course?
  2. What is presbyopia, in that same wording, and what age-group does the NEI name as common?

Exercises

Literature · The set Dryden extracts

40 min · The three set Dryden 1697 extracts, gathered · later of Book II still reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Dryden's Book II opening — Day 47
  • a short attributed steed passage — Day 49
  • a short attributed hollow-side passage — Day 52

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Weeks 10–11 set three short extracts from Dryden’s Book II, and reserved the rest. Today you gather those three, not a fourth.

The opening:

All were attentive to the God-like Man; When from his lofty couch he thus began. Great Queen, what you command me to relate, Renews the sad Remembrance of our Fate.

The steed passage:

The Greeks grew weary of the tedious War: And by Minerva's Aid a Fabrick rear'd, Which like a Steed of monstrous height appear'd; The Sides were plank'd with Pine, they feign'd it made For their Return, and this the Vow they paid.

The hollow-side lines:

Thus they pretend, but in the hollow Side, Selected Numbers of their Souldiers hide: With inward Arms the dire Machine they load, And Iron Bowels stuff the dark Abode.

John Dryden, trans., Aeneid Book II, first published 1697 (Wikisource, The Works of Virgil (Dryden)/Aeneid/Book II). Public domain. Spelling and capitals follow Dryden (Souldiers, Fabrick, rear’d).

What they give, together. Hearers are attentive; a man begins, from a couch, at a queen’s command, a fate that hurts to remember. The Greeks, weary of the war, raise a pine fabric like a huge steed, as a pretended vow for a return. Soldiers are hidden in the hollow side, and the machine is loaded with inward arms. That is all that has been set.

What remains reserved. Tenedos, the gates, Sinon, Priam, later night scenes, the departure, Creusa, and later books. Thursday’s examination will not open them.

Remember

Three set Dryden 1697 extracts: the opening, the steed, the hollow side. The English is Dryden’s, not Virgil’s Latin. Tenedos, the gates, Sinon, Priam, and later books remain reserved. Thursday is the examination.

Together

What do the steed passage and the hollow-side lines give, still without opening a reserved later scene?

A weary war; a pine fabric like a huge steed; a pretended vow for a return.

Hint 2: The hollow side

  2. Name two later Book II scenes that remain reserved.

Word How to say it Meaning
también tahm-BYEHN also / too
tampoco tahm-POH-koh neither / not either
Yo también yoh tahm-BYEHN I also / me too
Yo tampoco yoh tahm-POH-koh Neither do I / me neither

también and tampoco were taught last week. Today you practice them again. también agrees with a positive; tampoco agrees with a negative. Instruction stays in English. Present only.

Agreements: Está aquí. Yo también. No está aquí. Yo tampoco.

This course does not use también no for “neither” in this pattern.

Remember

también with a positive; tampoco with a negative. Present only. Instruction in English. Not también no.

Watch me

Music · A close and a pause: authentic and half cadences

30 min · Review: V–I close and a pause on V · major key · I, IV, and V only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • authentic cadence — Day 27
  • half cadence — Day 52

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Two cadence types have already been taught, in a major key, with I, IV, and V only. Today you gather them.

An authentic cadence, taught on Day 27: V moves to I, a close.

A half cadence, taught last week: the phrase ends on V, a pause, not an end. In C major, I–V or IV–V, stopping on G–B–D, is a half cadence.

A deceptive cadence can still wait. This is a common-practice school pattern, not a law for every style.

Remember

Authentic cadence: V–I, a close. Half cadence: a pause on V, not an end. Major key; I, IV, and V only. A deceptive cadence can still wait.

Watch me

In C major, a phrase moves I–V and stops. What kind of cadence is that, and why is it not authentic?

  1. It is a half cadence: it ends on V (G–B–D in C major).
  2. An authentic cadence would move V to I.
  3. The stop on V is a pause, not an end.

Together

A phrase in C major moves V–I. What kind of cadence is that?

Hint 1: Where does it end?

It ends on I.

Hint 2: The close

V to I is an authentic cadence, and can close.

Check

Use only these two cadence types.

  1. In your own words, how is a half cadence different from an authentic cadence?
  2. Write I–V and V–I in C major, and label which is the pause and which can close.

Exercises

  1. Why is “every stop in a song is a half cadence” a miss?
  2. Why does a deceptive cadence still wait?
  3. A short because / but / so about a pause on V.
