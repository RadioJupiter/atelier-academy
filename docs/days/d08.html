Latin · Capitulum III, part 3 and review

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. III

Learn

A short review of Capitulum III: subjects in the nominative, objects in the accusative, and verbs that agree in number. Read aloud, then say who is doing the action and who or what is receiving it.

Read aloud the rest of Capitulum III, then the whole chapter once for meaning.

Consolidation: the three cases so far (singular).

masculine feminine neuter
nominative servus, puer ancilla oppidum
genitive servī, puerī ancillae oppidī
accusative servum, puerum ancillam oppidum

(Neuter nouns always have the same form in the nominative and accusative.)

Remember

Nominative = the doer. Accusative = the object (often -m). Videt with one doer, vident with more than one.

Watch me

Puerī puellam vident. The boys (nominative plural) see the girl (accusative singular). The verb is vident because the doer is plural.

Together

In puella fluvium videt, which word is the object, and how do you know?

Hint 1: Object

fluvium.

Hint 2: Why

The -um / object form, and the girl is the one who sees.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What does the accusative mark in these sentences?
  2. Which verb form goes with puerī?

Exercises

  1. Write a ten-sentence Latin scene of your own (not from the book) with at least three people, using each case, at least three different verbs and one cūr … quia exchange.
  2. Identify the case of every noun in your scene.
  3. Translate into Latin: (a) The master's slave calls the girl. (b) Why does the boy laugh? Because the girl sings. (c) The girl does not see him.

Mathematics · Geometry IV: inequalities in triangles

60 min · Kiselev, Planimetry, relations between sides and angles

Learn

In a triangle, larger sides sit opposite larger angles, and the converse. The triangle inequality says any two sides add to more than the third (or the third side lies between the difference and the sum of the other two). These are inequalities in geometry: they constrain what can be built, and they appear in proofs about distances.

Theorems (each is proved in Kiselev from congruence and the exterior-angle property): 1. In a triangle, the larger side lies opposite the larger angle, and conversely. 2. Triangle inequality: each side is less than the sum of the other two (and greater than their difference). 3. The perpendicular from a point to a line is shorter than any oblique segment from that point to the line.

Locus. The set of all points equidistant from two points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Proving a locus takes two directions: every point on the line has the property, and every point with the property is on the line.

Remember

Larger side ↔ opposite larger angle. For sides a, b, c: a + b > c, and the two similar inequalities. A 'triangle' with sides 2, 3, 6 is impossible.

Watch me

Can 6, 7 and 14 be the sides of a triangle? Give a reason from the triangle inequality.

  1. Check each pair against the third side: 6 + 7 = 13, which is not greater than 14.
  2. So these lengths cannot be the sides of a triangle (they would lie flat, or fail to close).
  3. The other two checks, 6 + 14 > 7 and 7 + 14 > 6, are true, but all three must hold.

Together

A triangle has sides 7 and 15. The third side is x. What is the range of x?

Hint 1: Difference and sum

The third side must be greater than the difference and less than the sum: 15 − 7 < x < 15 + 7.

Hint 2: Range

8 < x < 22.

Check

These questions use the triangle inequality and the locus theorem from the lesson.

  1. Can 5, 12 and 17 be the sides of a triangle? Why or why not?
  2. What is the locus of points equidistant from two points A and B? (Name the line.)

Exercises

  1. Can a triangle have sides (a) 3, 4, 8; (b) 5, 7, 11; (c) 6, 6, 12?
  2. Two sides of a triangle are 4 and 9. What lengths can the third side have?
  3. A triangle has angles 50°, 60° and 70°. Order its sides from shortest to longest, naming them by their opposite angles.
  4. Prove theorem 3 using theorem 1.
  5. Prove both directions of the perpendicular-bisector locus.
  6. Prove that each diagonal of a quadrilateral is shorter than half its perimeter.
  7. (Stretch.) P is a point inside triangle ABC. Prove that PB + PC < AB + AC.

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium II, logic: claim, evidence, warrant

45 min · Method: the logic stage of the Trivium, using Stephen Toulmin's model of argument (The Uses of Argument, 1958)

Learn

Logic in the trivium asks whether a claim follows from its reasons. A valid argument has a conclusion that must be true if the premises are true. A sound argument is valid and has true premises. Today's writing work is to lay out a short argument so that the premises and the conclusion are visible, and then to test them.

Every argument has parts. Claim: what is being argued. Grounds (evidence): the facts offered. Warrant: the principle that connects the grounds to the claim, often unstated. Backing supports the warrant; a qualifier limits the claim ("usually", "in most cases"); a rebuttal names exceptions.

Example. Claim: Athens was a model for other states. Grounds: its laws gave equal justice in private disputes and opened office to merit. Warrant: a state that does these things deserves imitation. Rebuttal: unless large groups are excluded from citizenship altogether.

Finding the unstated warrant is often where an argument is weakest, and where you should attack or defend it.

Remember

Write the premises and the conclusion on separate lines first. Ask: if the premises were true, must the conclusion be true? Then ask whether the premises are in fact true.

Watch me

Premise 1: Every congruent triangle pair has equal corresponding angles. Premise 2: △ABC ≅ △DEF. Conclusion: angle A equals angle D. This is valid. Whether it is sound depends on whether premise 2 is actually true in the case at hand.

Together

Premises: 'All Bargue plates are lithographs. This plate is a lithograph.' Conclusion: 'This plate is a Bargue plate.' Valid or not?

Hint 1: Test

The premises could be true and the conclusion false: other lithographs exist.

Hint 2: Verdict

Not valid.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What is the difference between validity and soundness?
  2. If an argument is valid and one premise is false, can the conclusion still happen to be true?

Exercises

  1. Analyse Pericles' passage (Day 6) into claim, grounds and warrant. Which part is weakest?
  2. Identify the unstated warrant: (a) She should get the role; she practiced more than anyone. (b) This painting is valuable; it is very old. (c) The candle explanation must be right; it is in every textbook.
  3. Write a short argument (4–5 sentences) for the claim Copying master drawings is the best way to begin learning to draw, labelling claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier and rebuttal.

Physics · Newton's second law

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Newton's second law: the net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration, Fnet = ma. Force and acceleration are in the same direction. For a given force, a larger mass accelerates less. Units: force in newtons (N), with 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Pre-reading. The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass: Fnet = ma. Acceleration points in the direction of the net force. 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². Weight is the gravitational force: W = mg.

ConcepTests

  1. The same net force acts on a 2 kg cart and on a 4 kg cart. Compared with the 2 kg cart, the 4 kg cart's acceleration is: (A) twice as large (B) the same (C) half as large (D) a quarter as large.
  2. A box slides at constant velocity across the floor while you push it. Your push compared with friction is: (A) larger (B) equal (C) smaller.
  3. An elevator moves upward and slows down as it approaches the top floor. The tension in its cable compared with the elevator's weight is: (A) larger (B) equal (C) smaller.

Problems

  1. You push a 10 kg box with 30 N; friction is 10 N. Find its acceleration.
  2. What net force gives a 1,200 kg car an acceleration of 2.5 m/s²?
  3. A 60 kg person stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 1.0 m/s². What does the scale read, in newtons? (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)

Remember

Fnet = ma*. If several forces act, add them as vectors (or as signed numbers in one dimension) first, then divide by m to get a.

Watch me

A 2 kg cart is pulled by a 10 N force, and a 2 N friction force acts the other way.

  1. Fnet = 10 − 2 = 8 N in the direction of the pull.
  2. a = Fnet / m = 8 / 2 = 4 m/s² in that direction.

Together

The same 2 kg cart, now with a 6 N pull and 2 N friction the other way. What is a?

Hint 1: Net force

6 − 2 = 4 N.

Hint 2: Acceleration

4 / 2 = 2 m/s².

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. A 5 kg body has a net force of 20 N. What is its acceleration?
  2. If the net force is doubled and the mass stays the same, what happens to the acceleration?

History · The trial of Socrates

45 min · Primary source: Plato, Apology (Benjamin Jowett translation, public domain)

Context. In 399 BCE, five years after Athens lost the Peloponnesian War, the philosopher Socrates was tried before a jury of several hundred Athenian citizens, charged with not recognising the city's gods and introducing new divinities, and with corrupting the young. He was convicted and sentenced to death, and he died by drinking hemlock. Socrates wrote nothing himself; the Apology (Greek apologia, "defence speech") is Plato's version of his speech. Plato was his student and was present at the trial.

The source (two passages):

… if you kill me you will not easily find a successor to me, who, if I may use such a ludicrous figure of speech, am a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God; and the state is a great and noble steed who is tardy in his motions owing to his very size, and requires to be stirred into life.

… if I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me.

Exercises

  1. Sourcing. Plato was Socrates' devoted student. How should that affect your reading? What other evidence would help?
  2. Close reading. Explain the gadfly metaphor: who is the horse, what is the gadfly, and what does it imply about Athens?
  3. Context. The city had recently suffered defeat, plague and a brief, violent oligarchy. Why might citizens in 399 BCE be suspicious of a man who questioned everything?
  4. Compare with Pericles' praise of Athens (Day 6): how does Socrates' view of the city differ?

Atelier · Cast drawing 2: separating light and shadow

90 min

Learn

Once the block-in of the cast is stable, separate light from shadow. As on the egg, squint until you see two families, map the terminator, and lay in a single shadow gray. Do not model small bumps in the light until the large shadow is in the right place and the right value.

Today: two values only. Fill every shadow shape (form shadows and cast shadows) with one flat, even middle-dark tone, and leave every light shape as paper. Then compare with the cast while squinting hard. At this stage the drawing should already read as a solid form under light. If it does not, the shapes are wrong, and no amount of modeling will fix them.

Remember

Two families first. Terminator next. One shadow gray from the scale. No small modeling in the light yet.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. From the viewing point, squint at the cast until mid-grays in the light collapse toward white and the shadow becomes one field.
  2. Draw the terminator lightly over the block-in, then fill the shadow family with one even gray, leaving only a true reflected light if you see one.
  3. Compare that gray to your scale and to the cast, by squinting at both from the same spot.
  4. Stop. Do not 'improve' the light side today except to correct a shape error.

Together

If the shadow you have laid is much darker than the cast when you squint, what do you do?

Hint 1: Adjust

Lift some charcoal or graphite until the family matches, as a family.

Hint 2: Don't

Do not fix it by adding random mid-grays inside the shadow.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What are you separating at this stage?
  2. When do you start modeling small forms in the light?

Exercises

  1. (10 min) Re-check yesterday's block-in from the marked spot; correct anything off.
  2. (60 min) Lay the flat shadow tone. Keep the edges of shadow shapes accurate: sharp where the form turns abruptly, slightly soft where it turns gradually.
  3. (20 min) Squint test and mirror test; adjust shapes.

Modern language · Spanish: -er and -ir verbs

25 min

Learn

Regular -er and -ir verbs share most of the present endings, and differ in the nosotros and vosotros forms. Model: comer (to eat) and vivir (to live).

Word How to say it Meaning
comer koh-MEHR to eat
como KOH-moh I eat
comes KOH-mehs you eat
come KOH-meh he/she eats
comemos koh-MEH-mohs we eat
comen KOH-men they eat
vivir bee-BEER to live
vivo BEE-boh I live
vivimos bee-BEE-mohs we live
viven BEE-ben they live
escribir es-kree-BEER to write

comer (to eat): como, comes, come, comemos, coméis, comen. vivir (to live): vivo, vives, vive, vivimos, vivís, viven. The two sets differ only in the nosotros and vosotros forms. Common verbs: aprender, leer, beber, comprender, escribir, abrir, recibir, decidir.

Remember

-er and -ir: -o, -es, -e, -emos/-imos, -éis/-ís, -en. Escribimos (we write) uses -imos because escribir is -ir.

Watch me

Vivo en la ciudad. Escribimos mucho. I live in the city. We write a lot. Comen en la casa — they eat in the house.

Together

How do you say 'we live' and 'they write'?

Hint 1: We live

vivimos

Hint 2: They write

escriben

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What does comes mean?
  2. Why is it vivimos and not vivemos?

Exercises

  1. Conjugate aprender and escribir.
  2. Translate: (a) We read every day. (b) Do you (tú) understand? (c) They write letters. (d) I open the window. (e) We live in a big city.

Art history · Egypt: the art of permanence

30 min · Seminar rotation: Art history

Conventions. Egyptian relief and painting show the human figure in a composite view: head in profile, but the eye drawn as if seen from the front; shoulders from the front; hips, legs and feet in profile. Artists laid out figures on grids of squares to keep proportions consistent. Hierarchical scale: the most important figure (a pharaoh or god) is largest. These conventions stayed remarkably stable for some three thousand years, with one striking exception: the period of Akhenaten (14th century BCE), when the royal family was shown with elongated, curving bodies in more informal scenes.

Why a composite view? These images were not meant as snapshots from one viewpoint; they aimed to show each part in its most characteristic, complete form, appropriate to tomb and temple images meant to last forever.

Exercises

  1. Find a good reproduction of an Egyptian tomb painting or relief (for example, from the Metropolitan Museum of Art's online collection). Write a formal analysis (150 words): line, shape, composition, scale, color.
  2. Draw one figure from it using the composite view, then redraw the same figure as it would actually appear from a single viewpoint. What is lost and what is gained?
  3. In one paragraph: how do Egyptian conventions compare with the atelier's goal of sight-size accuracy?
