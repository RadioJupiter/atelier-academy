Latin · Capitulum III, part 3 and review

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. III

Learn

Key terms

nominative
Definition
The case of the subject: the form of the noun (and of its adjective) that you have used from the first chapter for who or what the sentence is about.
Origin
From Latin nōminātīvus, from nōmināre (‘to name’).
Example
Puer est in hortō: puer is nominative, the subject.
Not this
The nominative is not the only case, and a noun you have memorized in one form is not finished until you can also show the accusative and genitive when needed.
Connects to
This review puts nominative, genitive, and accusative side by side, with agreement still required.
case
Definition
The role a noun or adjective is playing in the sentence, shown by its ending: subject, possessor, object, and others still to come.
Origin
From Old French cas, from Latin cāsus (‘a falling, a grammatical case’), a calque of Greek πτῶσις (‘a falling’).
Example
The same noun can be puer in the nominative and puerum in the accusative, depending on the role.
Not this
Case is not gender, and it is not tense (tense belongs to the verb).
Connects to
Gender, agreement, and case are three different systems. This lesson checks that they are not being mixed up.

A short review of Capitulum III: subjects in the nominative, objects in the accusative, and verbs that agree in number. Read aloud, then say who is doing the action and who or what is receiving it.

Read aloud the rest of Capitulum III, then the whole chapter once for meaning.

Remember

Nominative = the doer. Accusative = the object (often -m). Videt with one doer, vident with more than one.

Mathematics · Geometry IV: inequalities in triangles

60 min · Kiselev, Planimetry, relations between sides and angles

Learn

Key terms

triangle inequality
Definition
In a triangle, each side is less than the sum of the other two (and greater than their difference). If this fails, the three lengths do not form a triangle.
Example
Sides 6, 7 and 14 cannot form a triangle, because 6 + 7 = 13, which is not greater than 14.
Not this
Equality (5 + 12 = 17) is not a triangle either: the lengths lie flat in a straight segment (a degenerate case).
Connects to
This is an inequality in geometry, in the same family as Day 7, but proved from congruence and the exterior-angle property.
locus
Definition
The set of all points that satisfy a given condition. A locus proof has two directions: every point on the figure has the property, and every point with the property lies on the figure.
Origin
Learned borrowing from Latin locus (a doublet of lieu).
Example
The locus of points equidistant from A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB.
Not this
A locus is not ‘a random collection of points that happen to work in one example’, and one direction of the proof is not enough.
Connects to
The proof uses congruence (Day 4) and the isosceles triangle facts already proved.

In a triangle, larger sides sit opposite larger angles, and the converse. The triangle inequality says any two sides add to more than the third (or the third side lies between the difference and the sum of the other two). These are inequalities in geometry: they constrain what can be built, and they appear in proofs about distances.

Theorems (each is proved in Kiselev from congruence and the exterior-angle property): 1. In a triangle, the larger side lies opposite the larger angle, and conversely. 2. Triangle inequality: each side is less than the sum of the other two (and greater than their difference). 3. The perpendicular from a point to a line is shorter than any oblique segment from that point to the line.

Locus. The set of all points equidistant from two points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Proving a locus takes two directions: every point on the line has the property, and every point with the property is on the line.

Remember

Larger side ↔ opposite larger angle. For sides a, b, c: a + b > c, and the two similar inequalities. A 'triangle' with sides 2, 3, 6 is impossible.

Key terms: triangle inequality, locus.

Watch me

Can 6, 7 and 14 be the sides of a triangle? Give a reason from the triangle inequality.

  1. Check each pair against the third side: 6 + 7 = 13, which is not greater than 14.
  2. So these lengths cannot be the sides of a triangle (they would lie flat, or fail to close).
  3. The other two checks, 6 + 14 > 7 and 7 + 14 > 6, are true, but all three must hold.

Hint 1: Difference and sum

The third side must be greater than the difference and less than the sum: 15 − 7 < x < 15 + 7.

Hint 2: Range

8 < x < 22.

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium II, logic: claim, evidence, warrant

45 min · Method: the logic stage of the Trivium, using Stephen Toulmin's model of argument (The Uses of Argument, 1958)

Learn

Key terms

claim
Definition
A sentence that can be argued with, which the rest of the paragraph or essay must support.
Origin
From Old French clamer, from Latin clāmāre (‘to cry out, declare’).
Example
‘Thersites is treated differently from Achilles because of rank, not because his complaints are false.’
Not this
A claim is not a topic, and it is not a quotation. Evidence supports a claim; it does not replace one.
Connects to
The topic sentence (Day 6) is a claim at the head of a paragraph. Today you also name the evidence and the warrant.
evidence
Definition
The material that is supposed to support a claim: a quotation, a fact, a detail from a source, given accurately.
Origin
From Old French, from Late Latin ēvidentia, from ēvidēns (‘obvious, visible’), from ē- + vidēre (‘to see’).
Example
A line number and a short quotation from Book 1, introduced and then explained.
Not this
Evidence is not a feeling, and a quotation that is not explained is not yet doing any work.
Connects to
In history, evidence is what a sourced document actually says. In writing, you present that material in support of a claim.
warrant
Definition
The reason that the evidence counts as support for the claim: the general rule or principle that joins the two.
Origin
From Old French warant, garant (‘guarantor, protector’). (The legal and argument sense grows from that idea of a guarantee.)
Example
Claim: Agamemnon is at fault. Evidence: he refuses a priest’s ransom. Warrant: a commander who insults a priest and brings a plague on his own army has failed in his duty.
Not this
A warrant is not a second quotation, and it is not ‘because I think so’. If the warrant is doubtful, the argument is weak.
Connects to
Claim and evidence were already in use. The warrant is the missing link you must now write down.

Logic in the trivium asks whether a claim follows from its reasons. A valid argument has a conclusion that must be true if the premises are true. A sound argument is valid and has true premises. Today's writing work is to lay out a short argument so that the premises and the conclusion are visible, and then to test them.

Every argument has parts. Claim: what is being argued. Grounds (evidence): the facts offered. Warrant: the principle that connects the grounds to the claim, often unstated. Backing supports the warrant; a qualifier limits the claim ("usually", "in most cases"); a rebuttal names exceptions.

Example. Claim: Athens was a model for other states. Grounds: its laws gave equal justice in private disputes and opened office to merit. Warrant: a state that does these things deserves imitation. Rebuttal: unless large groups are excluded from citizenship altogether.

Finding the unstated warrant is often where an argument is weakest, and where you should attack or defend it.

Remember

Write the premises and the conclusion on separate lines first. Ask: if the premises were true, must the conclusion be true? Then ask whether the premises are in fact true.

Key terms: claim, evidence, warrant.

Watch me

Premise 1: Every congruent triangle pair has equal corresponding angles. Premise 2: △ABC ≅ △DEF. Conclusion: angle A equals angle D. This is valid. Whether it is sound depends on whether premise 2 is actually true in the case at hand.

Together

Premises: 'All Bargue plates are lithographs. This plate is a lithograph.' Conclusion: 'This plate is a Bargue plate.' Valid or not?

Hint 1: Test

The premises could be true and the conclusion false: other lithographs exist.

Hint 2: Verdict

Not valid.

Physics · Newton's second law

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Key terms

Newton's second law Fnet = m a
Definition
The net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration is in the direction of the net force.
Example
A 2 kg cart with Fnet = 4 N has a = 2 m/s² in the direction of that force.
Not this
The law is not F = m v, and mass is not the same as weight. A larger mass gives a smaller acceleration for the same net force.
Connects to
Day 6 treated the special case Fnet = 0, which forces a = 0. This is the general relation.
mass m
Definition
A measure of how much a body resists acceleration (inertial mass). In SI it is measured in kilograms.
Origin
From Middle English masse (‘lump, quantity of matter’), from Anglo-Norman and Old French, from Latin massa, from Ancient Greek μᾶζα (‘barley-cake, lump’).
Example
The same 4 N net force gives a 1 kg object a = 4 m/s² and a 2 kg object a = 2 m/s².
Not this
Mass is not weight, and it is not ‘how much space the object takes up’.
Connects to
Inertia (Day 6) is this resistance, named as a quantity. Weight is a force and will not be treated as a synonym.

Newton's second law: the net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration, Fnet = ma. Force and acceleration are in the same direction. For a given force, a larger mass accelerates less. Units: force in newtons (N), with 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Pre-reading. The acceleration of an object is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass: Fnet = ma. Acceleration points in the direction of the net force. 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². Weight is the gravitational force: W = mg.

ConcepTests

  1. The same net force acts on a 2 kg cart and on a 4 kg cart. Compared with the 2 kg cart, the 4 kg cart's acceleration is: (A) twice as large (B) the same (C) half as large (D) a quarter as large.
  2. A box slides at constant velocity across the floor while you push it. Your push compared with friction is: (A) larger (B) equal (C) smaller.
  3. An elevator moves upward and slows down as it approaches the top floor. The tension in its cable compared with the elevator's weight is: (A) larger (B) equal (C) smaller.

Problems

  1. You push a 10 kg box with 30 N; friction is 10 N. Find its acceleration.
  2. What net force gives a 1,200 kg car an acceleration of 2.5 m/s²?
  3. A 60 kg person stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 1.0 m/s². What does the scale read, in newtons? (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)

Remember

Fnet = ma*. If several forces act, add them as vectors (or as signed numbers in one dimension) first, then divide by m to get a.

Key terms: Newton's second law, mass.

Watch me

A 2 kg cart is pulled by a 10 N force, and a 2 N friction force acts the other way.

  1. Fnet = 10 − 2 = 8 N in the direction of the pull.
  2. a = Fnet / m = 8 / 2 = 4 m/s² in that direction.

Together

The same 2 kg cart, now with a 6 N pull and 2 N friction the other way. What is a?

Hint 1: Net force

6 − 2 = 4 N.

Hint 2: Acceleration

4 / 2 = 2 m/s².

History · The trial of Socrates

45 min · Primary source: Plato, Apology (Benjamin Jowett translation, public domain)

Learn

Key terms

apology
Definition
A defense speech. Plato’s Apology is not an admission of fault; the Greek apologia means a defense.
Origin
From French apologie, from Latin apologia, from Ancient Greek ἀπολογία, from ἀπό (‘away from’) + λόγος (‘speech, account’).
Example
Socrates, as Plato writes him, defends himself before an Athenian jury in 399 BCE and calls himself a gadfly to the city.
Not this
It is not ‘sorry’. Reading the title as a modern apology will invert the whole work.
Connects to
Sourcing: Plato was a devoted student, present at the trial. The speech is a literary work, not a transcript. Compare Xenophon and the political context.
gadfly
Definition
A biting fly; in Socrates’ metaphor, the stinging critic who keeps a large, sluggish horse — the city — awake.
Example
‘If you kill me you will not easily find a successor to me… a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God.’
Not this
The gadfly is not a compliment meaning ‘popular preacher’, and it is not a claim that Athens is worthless. The horse is ‘great and noble’ as well as tardy.
Connects to
Compare Day 6: Pericles praises Athens as a model. Socrates (as Plato presents him) says the city still needs to be examined.

Context. In 399 BCE, five years after Athens lost the Peloponnesian War, the philosopher Socrates was tried before a jury of several hundred Athenian citizens, charged with not recognizing the city's gods and introducing new divinities, and with corrupting the young. He was convicted and sentenced to death, and he died by drinking hemlock. Socrates wrote nothing himself; the Apology (Greek apologia, "defense speech") is Plato's version of his speech. Plato was his student and was present at the trial.

The source (two passages):

… if you kill me you will not easily find a successor to me, who, if I may use such a ludicrous figure of speech, am a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God; and the state is a great and noble steed who is tardy in his motions owing to his very size, and requires to be stirred into life.

… if I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me.

Remember

In 399 BCE Socrates was tried in Athens, charged with not recognizing the city's gods and with corrupting the young, and was sentenced to death. The Apology is a defense speech (apologia), written by Plato, a devoted student who was present. Socrates' gadfly metaphor: Athens is a large, noble, sluggish horse; he is the sting that wakes it. 'The unexamined life is not worth living.'

Key terms: apology, gadfly.

Watch me

Sourcing Plato, then reading the gadfly metaphor.

  1. Plato would present Socrates favorably; the speech is a literary work, not a transcript. Other evidence: Xenophon's Apology, Aristophanes' Clouds, and the political context after defeat and the Thirty.
  2. The horse is Athens — great, noble, tardy because of its size. The gadfly is Socrates, stinging it awake by questioning. Killing the critic, on this view, harms the city.
  3. The metaphor is not an insult meaning the city is worthless; the horse is 'great and noble' as well as sluggish.

Together

The city had recently suffered defeat, plague, and a brief, violent oligarchy. Why might citizens in 399 BCE be suspicious of a man who questioned everything?

Hint 1: Associates

Some men associated with Socrates (for example Critias, one of the Thirty, and Alcibiades) had harmed the city.

Hint 2: Mood

After defeat and civil strife, questioning traditional beliefs could look like undermining the community, even if the questions were philosophical.

Atelier · Cast drawing 2: separating light and shadow

90 min

Learn

Key terms

terminator
Definition
The line (often soft) where light on a form gives way to shadow: the boundary along which the surface turns away from the light.
Origin
From Latin terminātor, from termināre (‘to bound, to limit’), from terminus (‘boundary’).
Example
On a cast of a face, the terminator runs where the cheek turns from the light into the core shadow.
Not this
The terminator is not a hard ink outline, and it is not the edge of the cast shadow on the wall.
Connects to
On the egg (Day 6) you already found this turn. Today you separate the whole light family from the whole shadow family before refining.
light and shadow families
Definition
The rule that values on the lit side stay in the light range, and values on the shadow side stay in the shadow range, so the two sides do not get mixed.
Example
Reflected light on the shadow side of a cast is still a shadow value; it must not be as light as a halftone on the lit side.
Not this
‘Cleaning up’ a shadow by adding pale patches usually destroys the family, and the form goes flat.
Connects to
Core shadow, reflected light, and cast shadow (Day 6) all belong to the shadow family.

Once the block-in of the cast is stable, separate light from shadow. As on the egg, squint until you see two families, map the terminator, and lay in a single shadow gray. Do not model small bumps in the light until the large shadow is in the right place and the right value.

Today: two values only. Fill every shadow shape (form shadows and cast shadows) with one flat, even middle-dark tone, and leave every light shape as paper. Then compare with the cast while squinting hard. At this stage the drawing should already read as a solid form under light. If it does not, the shapes are wrong, and no amount of modeling will fix them.

Remember

Two families first. Terminator next. One shadow gray from the scale. No small modeling in the light yet.

Key terms: terminator, light and shadow families.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. From the viewing point, squint at the cast until mid-grays in the light collapse toward white and the shadow becomes one field.
  2. Draw the terminator lightly over the block-in, then fill the shadow family with one even gray, leaving only a true reflected light if you see one.
  3. Compare that gray to your scale and to the cast, by squinting at both from the same spot.
  4. Stop. Do not 'improve' the light side today except to correct a shape error.

Together

If the shadow you have laid is much darker than the cast when you squint, what do you do?

Hint 1: Adjust

Lift some charcoal or graphite until the family matches, as a family.

Hint 2: Don't

Do not fix it by adding random mid-grays inside the shadow.

Modern language · Spanish: -er and -ir verbs

25 min

Learn

Key terms

-er and -ir verbs
Definition
The two other regular infinitive classes. In the present they share many endings; a few forms differ (nosotros and vosotros).
Example
Comer: como, comes, come, comemos. Vivir: vivo, vives, vive, vivimos.
Not this
They are not all identical to -ar verbs: hablo ends in -o like como, but hablamos and comemos do not use the same vowel before -mos.
Connects to
The infinitive (Day 5) tells you which pattern to use. Regularity is still the point; only the vowel of the class changes.
stem
Definition
The part of the verb that remains when the infinitive ending -ar, -er, or -ir is removed, to which the present endings are added.
Origin
From Old English stefn, stæfn (‘stem of a plant, tree-trunk’); the grammar sense is a later use of the same word.
Example
From hablar the stem is habl-; from comer, com-; from vivir, viv-.
Not this
The stem is not the infinitive, and in an irregular verb (tengo) the stem may change.
Connects to
Regular -ar, -er, and -ir verbs are exactly ‘stem + ending’. Irregular verbs are the ones that break this.

Regular -er and -ir verbs share most of the present endings, and differ in the nosotros and vosotros forms. Model: comer (to eat) and vivir (to live).

Art history · Egypt: the art of permanence

30 min · Seminar rotation: Art history

Learn

Key terms

composite view
Definition
The Egyptian convention of showing each part of a figure in its most characteristic aspect: head in profile, eye as if from the front, shoulders from the front, legs and feet in profile.
Example
A tomb figure can show a full shoulder and a full eye at the same time, which no single viewpoint would allow.
Not this
A composite view is not a mistake in drawing, and it is not the same as sight-size, which records one viewpoint faithfully.
Connects to
Formal analysis (Day 3) is how you describe it. The atelier’s sight-size is the contrast: one viewpoint, not the most complete form of each part.
hierarchical scale
Definition
The convention of making the most important figure (a pharaoh or a god) larger than the others, not because they stand closer, but because of rank.
Origin
Hierarchical is from hierarchy, from Late Latin, from Ancient Greek ἱεραρχία (‘rule of a high priest’), later ‘ranked order’. Scale here means relative size.
Example
In a relief, the king may be twice the height of attendants who stand on the same ground line.
Not this
Hierarchical scale is not perspective, and it is not ‘the artist could not measure’.
Connects to
A formal analysis should name this when it is present, along with registers (horizontal bands) and the composite view.

Conventions. Egyptian relief and painting show the human figure in a composite view: head in profile, but the eye drawn as if seen from the front; shoulders from the front; hips, legs and feet in profile. Artists laid out figures on grids of squares to keep proportions consistent. Hierarchical scale: the most important figure (a pharaoh or god) is largest. These conventions stayed remarkably stable for some three thousand years, with one striking exception: the period of Akhenaten (14th century BCE), when the royal family was shown with elongated, curving bodies in more informal scenes.

Why a composite view? These images were not meant as snapshots from one viewpoint; they aimed to show each part in its most characteristic, complete form, appropriate to tomb and temple images meant to last forever.

Remember

Egyptian relief and painting: composite view (head in profile, eye from the front, shoulders from the front, legs in profile); grids for proportion; hierarchical scale; registers (horizontal bands). These conventions were stable for some three thousand years, with a striking exception under Akhenaten (14th century BCE). The aim was the most complete, lasting form of each part, not a snapshot from one viewpoint.

Key terms: composite view, hierarchical scale.

Watch me

What a composite view gives and costs, as a model (your own copy of a relief still has to be drawn from a reproduction).

  1. The eye and the shoulder are shown in their most characteristic shapes, so the figure is complete as a record of a person, appropriate to a tomb or temple.
  2. A single-viewpoint drawing would foreshorten those parts and could not show them all fully at once. That is what sight-size trains, and it is the opposite aim.
  3. Neither system is 'wrong'; they use measurement (grids, or a viewing point) for different goals. A formal analysis should name the convention actually used.
