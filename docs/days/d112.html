Latin · The deponent imperfect, used in short sentences

40 min · Method: short original sentences with loquēbar and cōnābar, contrasted with the perfect and the present

Learn

Key terms

deponent imperfect in use
Definition
The imperfect of a deponent, already named yesterday, is used here in short original sentences: loquēbar and cōnābar for the ongoing or habitual past, still looking passive and translated as active. The participle of last week’s perfect is not used in these forms.
Example
Dum magister recitat, puer loquēbātur. Heri agricolae cōnābantur.
Not this
Not a first look at yesterday’s table, and not a first look at locūtus sum. Not an absolute. Not a claim that every past sentence in Latin must be imperfect.
Connects to
Yesterday’s six forms, last week’s perfect, and the present table.

Yesterday’s table is now used, not retaught as a first look. The imperfect is the right tense when the past action is ongoing or habitual in the sentence you are writing. The perfect, already taught, is the right tense when the sentence presents the action as completed.

Word How to say it Meaning
loquēbar loh-KWAY-bar I was speaking, or I used to speak
loquēbātur loh-kway-BAH-toor he or she was speaking, or used to speak
loquēbantur loh-kway-BAHN-toor they were speaking, or used to speak
cōnābar koh-NAH-bar I was trying, or I used to try
loquor LOH-kwor I speak, or I am speaking (present, already taught)
locūtus sum lo-KOO-toos soom I spoke, or I have spoken (perfect, already taught)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

Use loquēbar when the past action is ongoing or habitual. Use locūtus sum when it is presented as completed. The present loquor is for action in the present. The sentences remain original.

Watch me

Watch a habitual sentence, a completed sentence, and a present contrast.

  1. Heri puer loquēbātur: yesterday the boy was speaking, or used to speak — ongoing or habitual.
  2. Heri puer locūtus est: yesterday the boy spoke, or has spoken — completed.
  3. Hodiē puer loquitur: today the boy is speaking — present.

Together

Hint 1: The imperfect

Loquēbāmur: we were speaking, or we used to speak.

Hint 2: The perfect

Locūtī sumus: we spoke, or we have spoken. The action is now presented as completed.

Mathematics · Geometry LXIV: tangent as sine over cosine

40 min · Method: tan A = sin A / cos A, rewritten from the already-taught right-triangle ratios; degrees only

Learn

Key terms

tangent as sine over cosine
Definition
From the already-taught definitions, tan A = sin A / cos A, because (opposite over hypotenuse) divided by (adjacent over hypotenuse) is opposite over adjacent. No new triangle is required; the identity is a rewriting of the three ratios in the same right triangle. Degrees only.
Example
In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5, cos A = 4/5, and (3/5) / (4/5) = 3/4, which is tan A.
Not this
Not a first look at sine, cosine, or tangent as separate ratios. Not a claim about a unit circle, which remains reserved. Not a reciprocal such as cotangent, which remains reserved.
Connects to
Yesterday’s tangent, and last week’s sine and cosine.

Yesterday tan A was named as opposite over adjacent. Last week, sin A was opposite over hypotenuse, and cos A was adjacent over hypotenuse. Those three definitions already sit in the same right triangle, so one ratio can be written in terms of the other two.

Sin A / cos A = (opp/hyp) ÷ (adj/hyp) = (opp/hyp) × (hyp/adj) = opp/adj, which is tan A. In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, (3/5) / (4/5) = 3/4, matching tan A from yesterday. The same check holds in a 5-12-13 triangle. Degrees only. The unit circle remains reserved.

Remember

Tan A = sin A / cos A, because those three ratios use the same three sides of one right triangle. In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, (3/5) / (4/5) = 3/4 = tan A. Degrees only.

Watch me

Watch the rewriting, then the 3-4-5 check, then a 5-12-13 check.

  1. (Opp/hyp) ÷ (adj/hyp) = opp/adj, so sin A / cos A = tan A.
  2. In the 3-4-5 triangle, (3/5) / (4/5) = 3/4, which is tan A.
  3. In a classroom 5-12-13 triangle, if B is opposite 5, (5/13) / (12/13) = 5/12, which is tan B.

Science · The rate of conductive heat transfer, a first look

40 min · Method: a first look at Q/t = kA(T2 − T1)/d, from OpenStax 14.5, with k from Table 14.3; invented slabs labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

rate of conductive heat transfer
Definition
In OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 14.5, the rate of conductive heat transfer through a slab is Q/t = kA(T2 − T1)/d, where Q/t is in watts (joules per second), k is the thermal conductivity of the material, A is the surface area, d is the thickness, and (T2 − T1) is the temperature difference across the slab. The rate increases with k, with A, and with the temperature difference, and it decreases as the thickness d increases. When the two temperatures are the same, the net rate falls to zero. Values of k are taken from OpenStax Table 14.3, and are not invented.
Example
Table 14.3 gives, among other figures, copper 390, aluminum 220, ice 2.2, glass (average) 0.84, air 0.023, and Styrofoam 0.010, each in J/s·m·°C, at temperatures near 0 °C, as the table footnote states.
Not this
Not last week’s qualitative naming of conduction, convection, and radiation. Not a first look at convection or radiation rates, which remain reserved. Official household R-factor calculations in non-metric units remain reserved. Table k values are not to be replaced with invented ones.
Connects to
Last week’s three methods of heat transfer, OpenStax 14.4, and today’s first quantitative look at conduction alone.

Last week named conduction as heat transfer through stationary matter by physical contact. Today, from OpenStax 14.5, that method is given a rate. The rate depends on four factors: the material, through its thermal conductivity k; the cross-sectional area A; the temperature difference (T2 − T1); and the thickness d. Q/t = kA(T2 − T1)/d. OpenStax’s tile-and-carpet illustration is used only as it is written: both floors may be at the same temperature, yet heat leaves the skin faster into the tile than into the carpet, so the sensation differs because the rates differ, not because today’s job is a measured temperature of a living person.

Table 14.3, footnote: values at temperatures near 0 °C. Useful entries for this week, quoted from that table, include silver 420, copper 390, gold 318, aluminum 220, steel iron 80, steel (stainless) 14, ice 2.2, glass (average) 0.84, concrete brick 0.84, water 0.6, wood 0.08–0.16, wool 0.04, air 0.023, and Styrofoam 0.010, in J/s·m·°C. Good electrical conductors are, in the table, also good heat conductors; materials that trap air, such as Styrofoam, wool, and glass wool, are poor conductors and so are good insulators. The R factor, d/k, is named by OpenStax as a measure of insulation, but the non-metric household figures given there are not required calculations this week and are reserved. A worked official example from the same section waits until Thursday.

Remember

Q/t = kA(T2 − T1)/d. The rate grows with k, A, and the temperature difference, and shrinks as d grows. When the temperatures are equal, the net rate is zero. Take k from Table 14.3; do not invent a conductivity. Classroom slabs, other than official table values, are invented and labeled invented.

Watch me

Watch the four factors, then a classroom calculation that uses a table k and invented dimensions, then a warning.

  1. Larger k, larger A, or a larger temperature difference increases Q/t; a larger d decreases it.
  2. An invented copper slab, k = 390 J/s·m·°C from Table 14.3, has A = 0.010 m², d = 0.020 m, and a 20 °C difference: Q/t = 390 × 0.010 × 20 / 0.020 = 3900 W.
  3. The same invented dimensions in Styrofoam, k = 0.010 from the same table, give Q/t = 0.10 W. Do not invent a k that is not in the table.

Together

For the invented copper slab just worked, recompute Q/t, and say what happens to the rate if d is doubled and everything else stays the same.

Hint 1: The product

390 × 0.010 × 20 = 78, and 78 / 0.020 = 3900 W.

Hint 2: Doubling d

The rate is inversely proportional to d, so it falls to 1950 W.

Literature · Inferno VI.1–57: the third circle, Cerberus, and Ciacco

40 min · Method: Longfellow, Inferno VI.1–57, as the public-domain poem states it; no official identification beyond the text

Learn

Key terms

the third circle, Cerberus, and Ciacco, Inferno VI
Definition
Longfellow’s Inferno VI, lines 1–57, places the speaker in the third circle of rain “Eternal, maledict, and cold, and heavy,” with hail, sombre-hued water, and snow. Cerberus, “monster cruel and uncouth,” barks with three gullets, and the Conductor throws earth into those “rapacious gullets.” One spirit sits upright: “You citizens were wont to call me Ciacco”; “For the pernicious sin of gluttony I, as thou seest, am battered by this rain.” The passage is taught as the public-domain poem states it. No official historical identification of Ciacco, beyond that name, is added, and no official shade count is invented.
Example
A fair account keeps “maledict,” names Cerberus and the earth in his gullets, and lets Ciacco name himself and his sin.
Not this

Connects to

The set text is Longfellow 1867, Inferno VI, lines 1–57, from Wikisource: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_6. Keep the typography and wording of the set text, including “maledict,” “sombre-hued,” “unctuous,” and “wont.”

Consciousness returns after the swoon of Canto V. The speaker is in the third circle of “the rain / Eternal, maledict, and cold, and heavy; / Its law and quality are never new.” Huge hail, sombre-hued water, and snow pour down; “Noisome the earth is, that receiveth this.” Cerberus, “with his three gullets like a dog,” has red eyes, an unctuous black beard, a large belly, and clawed hands, and “rends the spirits, flays, and quarters them.” The Conductor fills his fists with earth and throws it “into those rapacious gullets,” and the muzzles grow quiet. background and a completed event
Definition
The imperfect can set a background, already ongoing, while the preterite names a completed event against that background, using only already-taught forms. The new skill is the pairing, not a new paradigm.
Example

Not this
Not a first look at yesterday’s choice in a single-tense sentence. Not a new verb. Not an identifying invented person.
Connects to

Ayer, mientras comía, vi el cielo. Cada mañana iba, y un día fui temprano. Do not invent a verb that has not been taught, and do not invent identifying characters. Veía, not vía.

Remember

The imperfect can hold a background; the preterite can name a completed event against that background. Ayer, mientras comía, vi el cielo. Use only already-taught forms.

Watch me

Watch a background, then a completed event, then the pair.

  1. Mientras comía: while I was eating — imperfect, already ongoing.
  2. Vi el cielo: I saw the sky — preterite, completed.
  3. Together: Ayer, mientras comía, vi el cielo. A miss would be Ayer, mientras comí, veía el cielo, which swaps the jobs.

Key terms

a short invented sequence
Definition
A sequence, in this first look, repeats a short invented figure starting on a new degree, so the shape of the figure is kept and the starting place is moved. The figure remains invented, not a copyrighted score. Last week’s retrograde inversion, and the already-taught retrograde and inversion, remain contrasts, not first looks. Invertible counterpoint remains reserved.
Example
An invented four-note figure C–D–E–G, sequenced one step higher, becomes D–E–F–A. The shape — step, step, skip — is kept, and the starting note has moved.
Not this

Last week’s retrograde inversion turned a short invented figure backwards and upside down, still on an invented figure, not a copyrighted score. Today a first look at a sequence: the same invented figure is repeated starting on a new degree, so the intervals of the figure are kept and the starting place is moved.

Invented example: C–D–E–G has the shape step, step, skip of a third. Starting one step higher, D–E–F–A keeps that shape. Starting one step lower, B–C–D–F keeps it as well, with the same quality of steps and skip on an invented white-note figure for this first look. A miss is to copy a copyrighted tune, or to treat a retrograde as if it were a sequence. Invertible counterpoint remains reserved.

Remember

A sequence repeats a short invented figure starting on a new degree, keeping the shape and moving the starting place. C–D–E–G, sequenced one step higher, becomes D–E–F–A. Do not use a copyrighted tune. Retrograde is a contrast, not a sequence.

Watch me

Watch the invented figure, the sequence up, then a near-miss.

  1. The figure C–D–E–G has the shape step, step, skip of a third.
  2. One step higher: D–E–F–A, the same shape from a new starting note.
  3. The retrograde G–E–D–C is not a sequence: it reverses the figure instead of repeating it from a new degree.
