Latin · Capitulum VI, part 2: town names, quō and unde

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VI · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms

town names (motion)
Definition
With the names of towns, cities, and small islands, Latin usually drops the preposition: the accusative alone means ‘to’ (Rōmam it), and the ablative alone means ‘from’ (Rōmā venit). Common nouns still need ad, ab, or ex (ad vīllam, ab oppidō).
Example
Mārcus Rōmam it, but Mārcus ad vīllam it. He comes from Rome: Mārcus Rōmā venit.
Not this
Rōmam is not automatically ‘in Rome’, and ad Rōmam is the form this chapter tells you not to use for the city itself. The exception applies to town names, not to vīlla or hortus.
Connects to
The questions are still quō? (where to?) and unde? (where from?). Tomorrow adds ubi? with the locative (Rōmae).
unde
Definition
The Latin question-word meaning ‘where from?’, asking for a motion-from answer: ab or ex + ablative for common nouns, and the bare ablative for town names.
Example
Unde venit Mārcus? Mārcus Rōmā venit. Unde venit puer? Puer ex hortō venit.
Not this
unde is not ubi and not quō. English ‘where’ covers all three, which is why they have to be practiced as three different Latin questions.
Connects to
Last Friday already used unde in a mini-dialogue. Today the town-name exception is the new piece.

Town names are the exception.

Word How to say it Meaning
Rōma ROH-mah Rome (the city)
Rōmam ROH-mahm to Rome (accusative, no preposition)
Rōmā ROH-mah from Rome (ablative, no preposition)
Tusculum TUS-ku-lum Tusculum (a town near Rome, in the book)
it it goes (from īre)
eunt eh-unt they go
unde UN-deh where from?
quō kwoh where to?

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The rule, in three lines. For a common noun: ad vīllam, ab oppidō, in hortō. For a town name: Rōmam (to), Rōmā (from). The preposition is dropped.

Original sentences: Mārcus Rōmam it. Iūlia Tusculō venit. Puerī nōn ad Rōmam, sed Rōmam eunt — actually just teach Puerī Rōmam eunt.

Read the section of Capitulum VI that uses the town names. Notice quō? and unde? in the margin or the text, then practice on the original sentences above.

Remember

Town names: accusative alone = to (Rōmam), ablative alone = from (Rōmā). Common nouns still need ad / ab / ex. quō? = where to?; unde? = where from?

Key terms: town names (motion), unde.

Mathematics · Algebra V: the laws of exponents

60 min · Method: Gelfand, Algebra, exponents as repeated multiplication, then the laws

Learn

Key terms

exponent an
Definition
The small number that says how many times a base is used as a factor: in aⁿ, a is the base and n is the exponent. For a positive integer n, aⁿ = a · a · … · a (n factors).
Origin
From Latin expōnēns, the present participle of expōnō (‘to set out, to explain’), from ex- (‘out’) + pōnō (‘to place’).
Example
2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. The exponent is 4; the base is 2.
Not this
The exponent is not the same as the base, and a² is not ‘a times 2’. Also, (ab)ⁿ is not a·bⁿ unless a = 1 or n = 1.
Connects to
Tomorrow's radicals undo even exponents: if x ≥ 0, (√x)² = x and √(x²) = x.
power
Definition
The result of raising a base to an exponent, or the expression aⁿ itself. The laws of powers are: aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0), (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ, (ab)ⁿ = aⁿ bⁿ, and a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0), a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0).
Example
2³ · 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128, and 2⁷ / 2³ = 2⁴ = 16. Also 5⁰ = 1 and 2⁻³ = 1/8.
Not this
A power is not addition: 2³ + 2⁴ is not 2⁷. You can add exponents only when you multiply powers of the *same* base.
Connects to
The laws are identities (Day 3): they hold for every allowed base, and they must be proved from the meaning, not memorized as tricks.

For a positive integer n, the power aⁿ means n factors of a. The laws then follow by counting factors.

  • aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ (m factors, then n more, is m + n factors).
  • (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ (n groups of m factors).
  • aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ when a ≠ 0 and m ≥ n (n factors cancel). The same pattern suggests the definitions a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0) and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0), so the law still holds when the exponent is zero or negative.
  • (ab)ⁿ = aⁿ bⁿ.

Do not add the exponents when you add the powers: 2³ + 2⁴ = 8 + 16 = 24, not 2⁷. The laws apply when you multiply or divide powers of the same base, or raise a power to a power.

Remember

Same base, multiply → add exponents. Divide → subtract. Power of a power → multiply exponents. a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ for a ≠ 0. Do not add exponents when you add the powers themselves.

Key terms: exponent, power.

Writing & Rhetoric · From one paragraph to two: the two-paragraph outline

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, expanding the single-paragraph outline

Learn

Key terms

two-paragraph outline
Definition
A short plan for a piece that has two paragraphs, each with its own topic sentence and supporting points, under one overall thesis. It is the single-paragraph outline (Day 6) used twice, plus a sentence that says how the two paragraphs work together.
Example
Thesis: the Egyptian–Hittite treaty should be read as a pair of royal self-presentations, not as a neutral record. ¶1: the Hattusa tablet. ¶2: the Karnak inscription. Each paragraph lists two or three points, not new theses.
Not this
It is not two separate short essays, and it is not one long paragraph cut in half. If the second paragraph could be first without any loss, the outline has not yet said why the order exists.
Connects to
Day 6’s SPO is still the unit inside each paragraph. The new skill is the job of the second paragraph relative to the first.
concluding sentence
Definition
The last sentence of a paragraph or of a short essay that does not merely repeat the topic sentence, but closes the thought — often by stating a consequence, a limit, or a return to the thesis in new words.
Example
After evidence about both copies of the treaty: ‘Read together, the two versions show what each court needed the other to accept.’
Not this
A concluding sentence is not ‘In conclusion, as I said…’ and it is not a new claim that would need its own paragraph.
Connects to
In a two-paragraph piece, the last sentence of paragraph two should close the thesis, not only the second topic sentence.

A two-paragraph piece has one thesis, two topic sentences, and a final concluding sentence.

The outline. Write: (1) the thesis, in one sentence that can be argued with; (2) the topic sentence of paragraph 1, plus two or three supporting points as phrases; (3) the topic sentence of paragraph 2, plus its points; (4) a concluding sentence that touches the thesis again in new words.

The test of order. Paragraph 2 should do work that paragraph 1 cannot do alone: a second source, a contrast, a consequence, or a limit. If you can swap the two paragraphs and nothing changes, rewrite the topic sentences until the order has a reason.

Today, build an outline only. The essay itself can wait until Day 20; a first draft may be written later in the week as a Franklin or quotation exercise needs it.

Remember

One thesis; two topic sentences, each with supporting points; a concluding sentence in new words. The second paragraph must do work the first cannot do alone. Do not write ‘In conclusion’ as a substitute for a close.

Key terms: two-paragraph outline, concluding sentence.

Physics · Uniform circular motion and centripetal force

45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student

Learn

Key terms

centripetal acceleration a = v²/r
Definition
The inward, center-seeking acceleration of a body that moves at constant speed on a circle, of size a = v²/r (or a = 4π²r/T², where T is the period). The velocity’s direction is changing even when the speed is not, so there is an acceleration.
Origin
From New Latin centripetālis, coined by Isaac Newton, from Latin centrum (‘center’) + petere (‘to seek, to aim at’).
Example
A body moving at 10 m/s on a circle of radius 5 m has a = 10²/5 = 20 m/s², toward the center.
Not this
Centripetal acceleration is not ‘a force that appears in circular motion’, and it is not outward. The word names the acceleration; the force that produces it has a separate name and a real source (tension, friction, gravity).
Connects to
Day 1: velocity can change by changing direction. Day 3: acceleration is the change of velocity. Today that change points toward the center.
centripetal force F = mv²/r
Definition
The net force toward the center that produces the centripetal acceleration: F_net, inward = m v²/r. It is not a new kind of force; it is the net inward force, supplied by whatever is actually pushing or pulling inward.
Example
If m = 2 kg, v = 10 m/s, r = 5 m, then F_net inward = 2 × 20 = 40 N. That 40 N might be the tension in a string, or friction, or a component of a normal force.
Not this
There is no outward ‘centrifugal force’ acting on the moving body in an inertial frame. The feeling of being ‘thrown out’ is the body’s inertia (Day 6) trying to go in a straight line.
Connects to
Newton’s second law still reads F_net = ma, with a = v²/r toward the center. Do not add an extra force to ‘balance’ the motion.

A body can move at constant speed on a circle. Its direction is always changing, so it is accelerating. The acceleration points toward the center and has size

a = v²/r.

If T is the time of one lap (the period), then v = 2πr/T and a = 4π²r/T².

The force is not a new type. Fnet toward the center is m v²/r, and it is supplied by a real force or a sum of real forces: a string’s tension, friction at the tires, gravity in an orbit. In an inertial frame there is no outward force on the body; inertia would have carried it in a straight line (Day 6).

A clean example. v = 10 m/s, r = 5 m, m = 2 kg: a = 20 m/s², Fnet = 40 N inward.

Remember

For constant speed on a circle, a = v²/r toward the center, and F_net inward = m v²/r. That net force is supplied by a real inward force, not by a new ‘centrifugal’ force on the body.

Key terms: centripetal acceleration, centripetal force.

Watch me

  3. The 40 N is the size of whatever real force (or sum) points inward — for example, the tension if the body is on a string.

History · Alexander and the end of the Achaemenid empire

45 min · Method: a narrative primary source (Arrian) judged against its own claims

Learn

Key terms

Alexander of Macedon
Definition
The Macedonian king who, from 336 BCE (the death of his father, Philip II) until his own death at Babylon in 323 BCE, conquered the Achaemenid Persian empire. The main campaign in Asia ran from 334 BCE to his death. These dates, unlike Old Kingdom years, are comparatively secure.
Example
Major battles include the Granicus (334), Issus (333), and Gaugamela (331). He also took Tyre (332) and entered Egypt, where the city of Alexandria was founded.
Not this
Alexander is not ‘the first king of Egypt’ (Egypt had had kings for many centuries), and he is not a Roman. The world he entered was the Late Achaemenid empire, already itself a successor of earlier Near Eastern states you have met.
Connects to
Day 5’s Herodotus described an earlier Persia. This lesson is two centuries later, and the sources are different.
Arrian
Definition
A Greek historian (2nd century CE) whose Anabasis of Alexander is a main surviving narrative of the campaign. Arrian says he relied especially on the accounts of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, companions of Alexander. He is therefore a later writer using earlier, now mostly lost, eyewitness narratives.
Example
When you use a public-domain translation (for example E. J. Chinnock, 1884), you are reading Arrian, not Ptolemy himself. Attribute what you quote to Arrian, and remember the gap of centuries.
Not this
Arrian is not a journalist at Issus, and he is not a Persian source. A complete picture would also need other voices (for example, later the Babylonian astronomical diaries, and Persian evidence), which this lesson only flags.
Connects to
Thucydides (Week 2) told you to ask how a historian knows. Ask that of Arrian’s preface: he names his preferred sources and claims to choose the more trustworthy.

Alexander became king in 336 BCE and died at Babylon in 323 BCE. The Asian campaign began in 334. The dates are those of a well-attested Hellenistic chronology, much firmer than Old Kingdom year-numbers.

A source you can actually read. Arrian, Anabasis of Alexander, written in the 2nd century CE, says he followed Ptolemy and Aristobulus, who had been with Alexander. That is a claim about sources, not a proof that every speech in the book was taken down at the time. Read Arrian’s opening remarks on method in a public-domain translation (Chinnock, 1884, is one), and then a short passage on one battle (Issus or Gaugamela). Ask: what does Arrian say happened, and how does he say he knows?

What not to do. Do not treat later legends (the more novelistic Alexander Romance) as the same kind of source. Do not invent numbers of dead. Do not call Alexander Egypt’s first king.

A link forward. Alexander’s death without a secure adult heir is the start of the Hellenistic successor kingdoms (next lesson), including Ptolemaic Egypt.

Remember

Alexander: 336–323 BCE; Asian campaign from 334; Issus 333, Gaugamela 331; death at Babylon. Arrian (2nd century CE) used Ptolemy and Aristobulus; attribute quotations to Arrian, and ask how he knows. These dates are much firmer than Old Kingdom years.

Key terms: Alexander of Macedon, Arrian.

Atelier · Combining solids: a small still life

45 min · Method: cube, cylinder, and sphere together, one light, fixed viewing point · After Days 11–14

Learn

Key terms

overlap
Definition
One form partly hiding another, which both creates a depth cue and forces you to measure where a contour stops and another begins. The hidden part of the back form is still constructed, lightly, so that the visible fragment sits on a whole solid.
Example
If the sphere sits partly in front of the cube, the cube’s edge stops at the sphere’s contour, and the sphere’s cast shadow may fall on the cube’s plane.
Not this
Overlap is not a smear between two objects, and it is not an excuse to leave the back object unconstructed. A broken, guessed join will flatten the group.
Connects to
Each solid still uses the construction you already know (planes, axis and ellipses, a calm contour). The new problem is their relation.
relative value
Definition
How light or dark a passage is compared with its neighbors, not only compared with a scale in the margin. In a group, the light plane of the cube, the light on the sphere, and a white cloth are not automatically the same step.
Example
The sphere’s highlight may be the highest light in the set-up; the cube’s light plane may be a step down; a cloth in shadow may be darker than a form shadow that receives bounce.
Not this
Relative value is not ‘each object gets its own private scale’, and it is not ‘everything in light is the same white’. You compare across objects.
Connects to
Control of value (Day 14) is now used between forms, including shadows that fall from one object onto another.

Put the cube, the cylinder, and the sphere together under one light. This is a small still life of solids.

Set-up. A simple grouping: some overlap, some space, one cast-shadow family on the table, and possibly a shadow from one solid onto another. Do not overcrowd. Fix the viewing point and the light.

Order of work. 1. The large group: one enclosing shape, the height of the tallest solid, the width of the group. 2. Each solid constructed (cube planes, cylinder axis and ellipses, sphere contour), including light construction of hidden parts where overlap needs it. 3. A check of alignments and of shared table-line or box they all sit on. 4. Value: first the largest families (the group in light, the group in shadow, the table), then relative value between objects, then a controlled hatch or plane tone, and the inner shadows (sphere on cube, and so on).

Cast shadows. They belong to the group: they share a direction, from the same light, and they take the shape of the surface they fall on (a shadow on the table is flatter than a shadow that climbs a cube’s face).

A common error. Three separate drawings placed side by side, each with its own light, or a smear where two forms overlap.

Remember

Modern language · Spanish: reflexive verbs

30 min · Method: explicit pronoun set, then daily-routine input · Instruction in English

Learn

Key terms

reflexive verb
Definition
A verb whose object is the same as the subject, marked by a reflexive pronoun: me, te, se, nos, os, se. The infinitive is written with -se: lavarse, levantarse, llamarse.
Origin
From Medieval Latin reflexīvus, from Latin reflexus, equivalent to reflex + -ive.
Example
Me lavo. Te levantas. Se llama Ana. Nos vamos. The pronoun stands before the conjugated verb, or attaches to an infinitive: Voy a lavarme / Me voy a lavar.
Not this
A reflexive is not automatically a daily-routine verb only, and se is not a second subject. *Se llama Ana* is ‘she is called Ana’, not ‘Ana calls herself’ in a strained English sense — but the Spanish form is still reflexive.
Connects to
The pronouns look like yesterday’s object pronouns, but they match the subject. Placement follows the same two options you already know.
the article with body parts
Definition
When the possessor is already clear from a reflexive pronoun, Spanish usually uses the definite article with a body part, not a possessive adjective.
Example
Me lavo las manos (I wash my hands). Se cepilla los dientes. Not usually *me lavo mis manos* in this course.
Not this
This is not a ban on mi/tu/su in every sentence, and it is not a new gender rule. The article still agrees with the noun: las manos, el pelo.
Connects to
Agreement of the verb and of the pronoun is with the subject; the article agrees with the body part.

A reflexive verb has an object that is the same person as the subject. The infinitive carries -se: lavarse, levantarse, vestirse, llamarse, irse, acostarse, despertarse.

The pronouns. me, te, se, nos, os, se — standing before the conjugated verb: Me lavo. Te lavas. Se lava. Nos lavamos. Os laváis. Se lavan.

Stem changes still apply. Despertarse (e → ie): me despierto, nos despertamos. Acostarse (o → ue): me acuesto, nos acostamos. Do not invent a new stem change for a reflexive; use only the patterns already taught.

Body parts. Me lavo las manos. Se lava la cara. The article, not mis/su.

Placement with an infinitive. Voy a levantarme or Me voy a levantar.

Logic · Necessary and sufficient conditions

30 min · Method: explicit definitions, then translation of ‘only if’ and ‘if and only if’ · After implication (Day 1) and De Morgan (Day 11)

Learn

Key terms

sufficient condition
Definition
A condition that is enough to guarantee the result: if P is sufficient for Q, then P → Q. Whenever P holds, Q holds.
Example
Being equilateral is sufficient for a triangle to be isosceles: if it is equilateral, then it is isosceles.
Not this
Sufficient is not the same as necessary. A condition can be enough without being the only way. A triangle can be isosceles without being equilateral.
Connects to
In symbols, ‘P is sufficient for Q’ is P → Q, the same arrow you have used since Day 1.
necessary condition
Definition
A condition that must hold if the result holds: if P is necessary for Q, then Q → P (equivalently, ¬P → ¬Q). Without P, you cannot have Q.
Example
Being a quadrilateral is necessary for being a square: if a figure is a square, then it is a quadrilateral. If it is not a quadrilateral, it is not a square.
Not this
Necessary is not the same as sufficient. Being a quadrilateral is not enough to make a square.
Connects to
‘Q only if P’ is another English form of Q → P: Q only if P means P is necessary for Q.

Implication can be read as a pair of everyday words: sufficient and necessary.

Sufficient. P is sufficient for Q when P is enough to give Q: P → Q. ‘If P, then Q.’

Necessary. P is necessary for Q when Q cannot hold without P: Q → P. Equivalently, ¬P → ¬Q. English also says ‘Q only if P’.

Both: if and only if. P is necessary and sufficient for Q when (P → Q) and (Q → P). We write P ⇔ Q, the same equivalence idea as Day 11, now in the language of conditions.

A test. Ask two questions. (1) If I have P, am I guaranteed Q? If yes, P is sufficient for Q. (2) If I lack P, am I guaranteed to lack Q? If yes, P is necessary for Q.

A trap. ‘Only if’ is not ‘if’. ‘A figure is a square only if it is a rectangle’ means: if it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It does not mean that every rectangle is a square.

Remember

P sufficient for Q means P → Q. P necessary for Q means Q → P (‘Q only if P’). Both together: P ⇔ Q. ‘Only if’ is not ‘if’.

Key terms: sufficient condition, necessary condition.

Watch me

  2. If it has four right angles, it is a rectangle (sufficient). If it is a rectangle, it has four right angles (necessary).
  3. The answer follows the course definition, not a looser everyday use of ‘rectangle’.
