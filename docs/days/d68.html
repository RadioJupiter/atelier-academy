Latin · Capitulum XII, part 3: the comparative (-ior, -ius)

40 minutes · Method: fortior / fortius beside fortis, -e. Instruction in English. Original sentences.

Learn

Key terms

comparative adjective
Definition
The degree of an adjective that compares two things (in English, often the -er form, or “more”). In Latin the first look is the ending -ior (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and -ius (neuter nominative or accusative singular): fortior, fortius, “braver” or “stronger.”
Example
Mīles fortior est. Scūtum fortius est.
Not this
Not the positive adjective fortis, -e already taught, and not an English -er added onto a Latin stem (*fortiser). The comparative is a Latin ending, not a borrowed English one.
Connects to
The comparative uses third-declension endings, but it is not an i-stem in the way fortis, -e is. The first look today is the nominative singular pair -ior / -ius. Later cases remain for a later sitting.
Word How to say it Meaning
fortior for-TEE-or braver, stronger (masculine or feminine nominative singular)
fortius for-TEE-us braver, stronger (neuter nominative or accusative singular)
Mīles fortior est MEE-les for-TEE-or est The soldier is braver

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

A comparative adjective compares. In Latin, a first look is the pair -ior (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and -ius (neuter nominative or accusative singular). From fortis you form fortior, fortius: braver, or stronger.

So Mīles fortis est describes one soldier as brave. Mīles fortior est describes a soldier as braver (than some other person or thing already in view). Scūtum fortius est uses the neuter fortius to match scūtum.

Original sentences: Mīles fortior est. Scūtum fortius est. Mīles nōn fortior est. Scūtum nōn fortius est.

The comparative uses third-declension endings, but it is not an i-stem in the way fortis, -e is. You do not need the full set of cases today; the job is to recognize -ior and -ius in the nominative singular and to keep agreement.

Remember

The comparative nominative singular is -ior (masculine or feminine) and -ius (neuter): fortior, fortius. It is not the positive fortis, -e, and it is not an English -er stuck on a Latin word.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXI: a missing side, from a² + b² = c²

40 minutes · Method: the theorem already proved, used to find a side

Learn

Key terms

a missing side in a right triangle
Definition
A leg or the hypotenuse of a right triangle, found from the Pythagorean theorem when the other two sides are known. To find a hypotenuse, add the squares of the legs and take the positive square root. To find a leg, subtract the known leg’s square from the hypotenuse’s square, then take the positive square root.
Example
Legs 6 and 8 give hypotenuse 10, because 36 + 64 = 100. Hypotenuse 13 and leg 5 give the other leg 12, because 169 − 25 = 144.
Not this
Not adding the legs to get the hypotenuse (6 + 8 is not 10’s reason), and not using the theorem on a triangle that has not been shown to be right.
Connects to
The length is the positive root; a side is not negative. A 6-8-10 triangle is a scaled 3-4-5 triangle, which you may notice after the calculation, but the theorem itself does not require that you remember a list of triples first.

Once a triangle is known to be right, the Pythagorean theorem finds a missing side.

To find the hypotenuse c from legs a and b: compute a² + b², then take the positive square root, because c² equals that sum and a length is positive.

To find a leg a from the other leg b and the hypotenuse c: compute c² − b², then take the positive square root. You subtract because a² = c² − b².

Watch the arrangement. Adding the two legs does not give the hypotenuse. The theorem adds squares.

A 6-8-10 triangle works because 36 + 64 = 100. It is also a scaled 3-4-5 triangle (scale factor 2, from week 13’s similarity work). You may notice that after the calculation. You do not have to memorize a list of triples in order to use the theorem.

If a triangle has not been shown to be right, you may not use this method to invent a third side as if it were a hypotenuse.

Remember

In a right triangle, c² = a² + b². A missing hypotenuse is the positive square root of a² + b². A missing leg is the positive square root of c² minus the other leg’s square. Do not add the legs themselves.

Physics · Stability, and the base of support

40 minutes · Method: a vertical line from the center of mass, tested against the base

Learn

Key terms

base of support
Definition
The area on the ground (or other supporting surface) within which a vertical line from the center of mass must fall, if the object is not to tip.
Example
A standing block whose center-of-mass line meets the ground inside its base stays put. If the block is tilted until that line falls outside the base, it tips.
Not this
Not the center of mass itself (which is a point in the object), and not a claim that a wider base always means a lower center of mass. A wider base makes it harder for the line to fall outside, which is a different statement.
Connects to
Stability here is a geometric test with the center of mass, not a new force law. Classroom tilts and widths are invented and labeled, unless a sourced figure is named.
stability (against tipping)
Definition
An object is stable against tipping while a vertical line from its center of mass falls inside its base of support. It begins to tip when that line reaches the edge of the base.
Example
A taller, narrower object, with a higher center of mass and a smaller base, needs a smaller tilt before the line falls outside the base.
Not this
Not “heavy things never tip,” and not a confusion with last week’s rotational equilibrium of a lever as a first look at a new law. The test is the line and the base.
Connects to
Lowering the center of mass, or widening the base, both make a larger tilt necessary before the line leaves the base.

An object resting on a surface has a base of support: the area on that surface inside which a vertical line from the center of mass must fall, if the object is not to tip.

If the line meets the ground inside the base, the object stays put. If the object is tilted until the line falls outside the base, it tips. The edge of the base is the last point at which the line can fall and still not tip.

A wider base makes it harder for the line to fall outside. A lower center of mass does the same, because a larger tilt is then needed to carry the line over the edge. A tall, narrow object is the opposite case.

The widths and tilts in today’s drawings are invented classroom figures, labeled as such. The test itself is the line and the base, not a new official measurement.

Remember

An object does not tip while a vertical line from its center of mass falls inside its base of support. A wider base, or a lower center of mass, makes tipping require a larger tilt. Invented widths stay labeled invented.

History · The Nika rising, and the church called Sophia, in Buildings I.1.20–22

40 minutes · Method: a second set extract from the same chapter, quoted exactly and read as Procopius’s account

Learn

Key terms

Nika Insurrection (as named by Procopius)
Definition
The rising in Byzantium against Justinian that Procopius, in Buildings I.1.20, calls “the Nika Insurrection,” and that he says he has already described in the Books on the Wars. In the set extract, the rising includes the burning of the church the people of Byzantium call “Sophia.”
Example
The set wording includes: “they brought about the rising called the Nika Insurrection,” and “they had the hardihood to fire the Church of the Christians, which the people of Byzantium call ‘Sophia.’”
Not this
Not a rising in the city of Rome in 410 or 476, and not a death toll, which this extract does not give. The name used here is Procopius’s (in Dewing’s English).
Connects to
Procopius writes that the old church lay “a charred mass of ruins,” and that Justinian “built not long afterwards a church so finely shaped” that, in Procopius’s opinion, people would have prayed to see the old one destroyed in order to have this one. That last comparison is Procopius’s praise, not a measured plan of the building.
the church called Sophia
Definition
The church in Byzantium which, Procopius says, “the people of Byzantium call ‘Sophia,’ an epithet which they have most appropriately invented for God, by which they call His temple.” In the set extract it is first burned in the Nika rising, then rebuilt by Justinian “so finely shaped.”

The set wording after the fire is: “So the whole church at that time lay a charred mass of ruins. But the Emperor Justinian built not long afterwards a church so finely shaped…”

Connects to

Atelier · The mouth, on the blocked-in head

40 minutes · Method: two masses and a mid-line, under the nose already placed

Learn

Key terms

the mouth, as a form between two masses
Definition
The mouth on the blocked-in head, first placed as a slit and two masses (an upper and a lower) under the base of the nose, on the mid-line, not as a lined smile copied from a photograph.
Example
A first pass finds the mid-line through the lips, the corners in from the edges of the nose’s base, and the two masses in the same light as the wedge and the cheeks.
Not this
Not a outlined heart shape, and not teeth drawn before the masses close. If the mouth is closed, there is no separate set of teeth to invent.
Connects to
The corners of the mouth usually sit inside the width of the eye sockets, but the trustworthy check is this head’s own nose-base and mid-line, not a memorized slogan used without looking.

The mouth is first two masses, an upper and a lower, with a slit between them, seated under the base of the nose, on the mid-line. The corners usually sit a little in from the sides of the nose’s base, but you check that on this head, rather than importing a memorized width and refusing to look.

Do not begin with a lined smile from a photograph, or with a row of teeth. If the mouth is closed, the teeth are not a separate drawing job. If it is a little open, the dark of the opening is a simple shape, not a dental chart.

The mouth shares the same light. An upper mass that faces the lamp a little more than a lower one will be a little lighter; a terminator that reaches the cheek beside the corner will reach that corner too.

Remember

The mouth is two masses and a slit, under the nose, on the mid-line, in the same light. It is not a copied outline or a set of teeth drawn first. Check corners against this head’s nose, not against a slogan alone.

This sitting uses the same set extract as Wednesday’s history — Procopius, The Buildings, I.1.20–22, in Dewing’s translation — now as a first look at a building described in words.

The people of Byzantium call the church “Sophia.” In the rising Procopius names the Nika Insurrection, it is fired, and “the whole church at that time lay a charred mass of ruins.” Justinian “built not long afterwards a church so finely shaped” that, Procopius says, people would have prayed to see the old one destroyed in order to have this one.

The architectural extract is Procopius, The Buildings, I.1.20–22, in H. B. Dewing’s 1940 translation, as posted at LacusCurtius (https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html). The translation is in the public domain (copyright not renewed; expired 1968). This sitting uses the same set wording as Wednesday’s history, as a description in words, not a plan or a photograph. No height or span is invented.

What you have, as evidence of the building, is this praise of its shape after a fire, not a measured height, a span, a ground plan, or a photograph. This course will not invent those figures. A later source may add them; this extract does not.

Quote Dewing when you quote, including “shewing” if that word is quoted, and “finely shaped.”
