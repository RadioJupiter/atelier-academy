Latin · Capitulum X, part 3: the present infinitive, passive

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. X · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

present infinitive (passive)
Definition
The ‘to be …-ed’ form that pairs with yesterday’s active infinitive. In this first look: vocārī (to be called), habērī (to be held / to be had), vidērī (to be seen). The ending is -rī on these verbs (the first and second conjugations as used today).
Example
Rēx vidērī potest. Homō vocārī potest.
Not this
Not the active vidēre (‘to see’), and not a finite passive (which this lesson does not teach as a full conjugation). vidērī is ‘to be seen,’ not ‘he is seen.’
Connects to
posse still takes an infinitive. Today the infinitive may be passive: someone is able to be seen, or to be called, without a new tense.
Word How to say it Meaning
vidēre / vidērī wi-DAY-reh / wi-DAY-ree to see / to be seen
vocāre / vocārī wo-KAH-reh / wo-KAH-ree to call / to be called
habēre / habērī ha-BAY-reh / ha-BAY-ree to have / to be held or had
Rēx vidērī potest rayks wi-DAY-ree PO-test The king can be seen

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Active and passive, as a pair. vidēre — to see; vidērī — to be seen. vocāre — to call; vocārī — to be called. After possum / potes / potest, either kind of infinitive may be used, but the meaning changes.

This lesson does not teach a full finite passive (laudātur, and so on). It teaches the infinitive in the passive, in original sentences.

Original sentences: Rēx vidērī potest. Homō vocārī potest. Potes vidērī. Leō habērī nōn potest is a possible classroom sentence if you mean ‘cannot be kept’; you may also write Canis vidērī potest.

Remember

Passive infinitives in this first look: vidērī, vocārī, habērī (‘to be seen / called / held’). After posse, the infinitive may be active or passive; they are not the same.

Key terms: present infinitive (passive).

Mathematics · Algebra XI: Vieta’s formulas for a quadratic

60 min · Method: expand a factored quadratic, then read the sum and the product of the roots from the coefficients

Learn

Key terms

Vieta’s formulas (quadratic)
Definition
For a quadratic equation, the sum and the product of the roots can be read from the coefficients. For x² + bx + c = 0, the sum of the roots is −b and the product is c. For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0, the sum is −b/a and the product is c/a. The name honors François Viète (Vieta).
Example
If the roots are 2 and −5, the monic quadratic is (x − 2)(x + 5) = x² + 3x − 10, so the sum is −3 (which is −b) and the product is −10 (which is c).
Not this
Not a substitute for solving when you are asked to find the roots, and not a claim that every quadratic has two real roots. If there are no real roots, today’s reading of a real sum and product from a real factored form does not apply in that way; this lesson works with quadratics that have two real roots (possibly repeated).
Connects to
Day 3 factored quadratics, Day 5 solved them, and Day 33 graphed them. The x-intercepts are the roots; when there are two, the vertex’s x-coordinate is their average, which is also half the sum.

From the factored form, first. If the roots are r and s,

(x − r)(x − s) = x² − (r + s)x + rs.

Compare with x² + bx + c. Then:

  • r + s = −b
  • rs = c

That pair is Vieta’s formulas for a monic quadratic (leading coefficient 1). If the quadratic is ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0, first divide by a, or remember:

  • r + s = −b/a
  • rs = c/a

A pictorial check. On the graph, the roots are the x-intercepts. Their midpoint is −b/(2a), which is also the x-coordinate of the vertex (Day 33). That is the same sum, halved.

A near-miss. The sum is −b/a, not b/a. The minus sign is easy to drop.

Remember

For x² + bx + c = 0, sum of roots = −b, product = c. For ax² + bx + c = 0, sum = −b/a, product = c/a. This lesson uses two real roots (possibly repeated). The minus sign on the sum matters.

Key terms: Vieta’s formulas (quadratic).

Watch me

  3. Check: 2 + 3 = 5, 2 × 3 = 6.

Writing & Rhetoric · The concession paragraph, revisited

45 min · Method: a middle paragraph that grants a real point without giving up the essay’s job

Learn

Key terms

concession paragraph
Definition
The middle paragraph of a three-paragraph essay, when its job is to grant a real point on the other side, in that side’s terms, and then return to the essay’s own job without a sneer or a sudden new subject.
Example
A paragraph that grants Creon’s claim that a city needs a public command, and then returns to the cost of that command as the essay will treat it, is a concession — if the grant is real and not a caricature.
Not this
Not a fake concession (“some may say, wrongly…”), and not a second introduction. Last week’s parenthetical interruption is a sentence-level tool; today the grant is a whole paragraph’s job.
Connects to
Yesterday’s plan may already have named this paragraph. Today you write it, with a topic sentence that names the grant.

A concession is a real grant. Write one middle paragraph:

  • A topic sentence that names the point you are granting, in fair terms.
  • Two or three sentences that make the grant specific (a reason, a short attributed quotation if you have one already taught, an example).
  • A last sentence that returns to your essay’s job without erasing the grant.

If you cannot state the other claim in words that claim’s defender would still recognize, the concession is not yet real.

Remember

A concession paragraph grants a real point, in fair terms, and then returns to the essay’s job. No fake grant, and no ending of the play.

Key terms: concession paragraph.

Physics · Mass and weight

50 min · Method: contrast a property of the body (mass) with the gravitational force on it (weight)

Learn

Key terms

mass (mechanics)
Definition
A measure of the amount of matter, and of how strongly the body resists a change of motion (inertia), in this first look. The SI unit is the kilogram (kg). Mass is not the same as weight.
Example
A 2 kg package is still 2 kg if you carry it to a place where g is smaller; its mass has not changed in this course’s first model.
Not this
Not a synonym of weight, and not a number of newtons. A mass is measured in kilograms here, not in newtons.
Connects to
The m in F = ma and the m in F = G Mm / r² is this quantity. Monday’s formula uses mass, not weight, in the symbols M and m.
weight
Definition
The gravitational force on a body. Near the Earth, in the first model already used, weight has size W = mg, with g = 9.8 N/kg, and is measured in newtons. The English word is related to Old English wiht / ġewiht, and to weigh.
Origin
Related to Old English wiht / ġewiht, and to the verb weigh. In this lesson weight is a force, in newtons.
Example
A 2 kg mass near the Earth has weight W = 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N. That is a force, not a second mass.
Not this
Not a mass in kilograms, and not a new unit. A scale that is labeled in kilograms is often reporting a mass inferred from a weight; this lesson will still keep the two words apart.
Connects to
On the Moon, the same mass has a smaller weight, because g there is smaller. This lesson uses the commonly taught first figure that lunar g is about one sixth of Earth’s, labeled as an approximate first figure, not as a classroom measurement.

Mass is a property of the body (kilograms). Weight is the gravitational force on it (newtons).

Near the Earth, in the model you already have:

W = mg, with g = 9.8 N/kg.

The same mass in a place where g is smaller has a smaller weight. A commonly taught first figure is that g on the Moon is about one sixth of g near the Earth; that is an approximate first figure, not a number this class measured.

Monday’s G is still not g. Weight near the Earth can be seen, in Newton’s formula, as the pull between the Earth and the body; this lesson does not ask you to compute that pull from G and the Earth’s mass.

Remember

Mass is in kilograms; weight is a force, W = mg near the Earth, in newtons. The same mass has a smaller weight where g is smaller (Moon: about one sixth, an approximate first figure). G is not g.

Key terms: mass (mechanics), weight.

History · A later name: the Julio-Claudian line, and the same source habit

50 min · Method: a conventional list of successors, with later writers still dated, and anecdotes not treated as simple facts

Learn

Key terms

Julio-Claudian line (conventional)
Definition
A later name for the line of principes from Augustus through Nero, conventionally: Augustus (to 14 CE), Tiberius (14–37), Gaius (“Caligula,” 37–41), Claudius (41–54), and Nero (54–68). The name and the year-ranges are conventional labels, not a claim that the men used this family title in a modern way, and not a classroom proof of every year.
Example
A careful use: “Nero is the last of the Julio-Claudian principes in this conventional list; his death is dated to 68 CE.” The list is a map, not a novel.
Not this
Not a license to treat later anecdotes (in Suetonius or in Tacitus) as straightforward fact, and not a new “fall of the Republic” date at 68 CE. 68 CE is the end of this line in the conventional list, and a year of civil war; it is not taught here as a single constitutional day.
Connects to
Monday’s 14 CE sits inside this list. The source habit of last week still applies: date the writer, name the kind of text, and do not flatten a later narrative into “what happened.”

A conventional list, to be used as a map:

  • Augustus, to 14 CE
  • Tiberius, 14–37 CE
  • Gaius (often called Caligula), 37–41 CE
  • Claudius, 41–54 CE
  • Nero, 54–68 CE

The family name Julio-Claudian is a later label for this line. This lesson does not teach a scandal-sheet of anecdotes as history. Tacitus and Suetonius remain later writers; they are evidence, and they are often moralizing or shaped. Use them with the same habit as last week: date the source, name its kind, and do not take a vivid story as a simple fact only because it is memorable.

68 CE ends this list and is a year of civil war in the conventional narrative. It is not taught here as “the day the Republic fell,” and not as a second founding.

Remember

A conventional Julio-Claudian list: Augustus, Tiberius, Gaius (Caligula), Claudius, Nero, with the year-ranges taught today. Later writers remain later; anecdotes are not straightforward facts. 68 CE is not a new fall day.

Key terms: Julio-Claudian line (conventional).

50 min · Method: edges of the hair masses, and the ear they overlap, decided from the plate — some lost, some found

Key terms

lost-and-found at the hair and ear
Definition
A second, slower look at which edges of the hair masses, and of the ear they may overlap, should stay soft (lost) and which may be a little clearer (found), as the plate or plaster shows.
Example
A far jaw lost against dark hair, and a clearer temple edge if the plate has one, is a typical pair — only if this plate actually shows it.
Not this
Not an outline around every lock, and not an ear cut out as a sticker. Week 6 already named lost-and-found; today it is applied to this week’s hair and ear.
Connects to
Yesterday’s intervals should still hold. An edge pass is not a license to move a feature that was just corrected, unless the plate now shows that the interval itself was wrong.

Squint at the edges. Where does hair meet the background, the forehead, the ear, the neck? Some of those meetings should almost disappear (lost); some may take a slightly clearer mark (found). Copy the plate, not a rule that “hair is always soft” or “the ear is always sharp.”

If a mass still overlaps the ear, keep the ear’s place and let the overlap be a shape, not a hole or a pasted outline.

Decide lost and found edges at the hair and the ear from the plate. No outline around every lock, and no ear-as-sticker. Intervals already checked should still hold.

Modern language · Spanish: acabar de + infinitive

40 min · Method: one present-tense ‘have just’ pattern, contrasted with ir a + infinitive; no past-tense conjugation is introduced

Learn

Key terms

acabar de + infinitive
Definition
A present-tense pattern that means ‘to have just done’ something: acabar (conjugated in the present) + de + infinitive. It is still the present of acabar, not a past-tense conjugation.
Example
Acabo de estudiar. Acabas de repetir la frase. Acabamos de pedir agua.
Not this
Not a past-tense form (pretérito) of the second verb, and not ir a + infinitive (that pattern is ‘going to,’ last week). And not *acabo estudiar (the de is required).
Connects to
You already have -ar presents (acabo, acabas, acaba, acabamos, acabáis, acaban). Today they are followed by de and an infinitive you already know (estudiar, pedir, practicar, leer, comer).
Word How to say it Meaning
Acabo de estudiar ah-KAH-bo deh eh-stoo-DYAR I have just studied
Acabas de repetir ah-KAH-bas deh reh-peh-TEER You have just repeated
Acaba de pedir agua ah-KAH-bah deh peh-DEER AH-gwah He/She has just asked for water
Acabamos de practicar ah-kah-BAH-mohs deh prahk-tee-KAR We have just practiced

The pattern. A present form of acabar + de + infinitive: ‘have just…’ The de stays. This is not a new past-tense conjugation; acabo is still present.

Contrast: Voy a estudiar (I am going to study) versus Acabo de estudiar (I have just studied). Original sentences: Acabo de leer. ¿Qué acabas de hacer? Acaban de servir la comida. No acabamos de empezar is not today’s meaning — stay with the ‘have just’ sense, and do not invent acabo estudiando.

Remember

acabar (present) + de + infinitive: ‘have just…’ The de is required. Still not a past-tense conjugation, and not ir a + infinitive.

Key terms: acabar de + infinitive.

Art history · The Pantheon in Rome, with a dating caution

40 min · Method: the building we have, the inscription it still carries, and two dates that must not be collapsed

Learn

Key terms

Pantheon (Hadrianic building, Agrippan inscription)
Definition
The Pantheon in Rome is a circular temple with a concrete dome and a central oculus, as we have it, generally treated as a rebuild under Hadrian in the early second century CE (a conventional range often given around 118–125 CE). The word comes from Latin Pantheon, from Greek Πάνθειον (Pantheion), “a temple of all gods,” from pan-, “all,” and theos, “god.”
Origin
From Latin Pantheon, from Greek Pantheion, “a temple of all gods” (pan-, “all,” and theos, “god”).
Example
The porch still bears an inscription naming Agrippa, consul for the third time, as the one who made it. That inscription belongs to an earlier Agrippan temple, conventionally associated with 27 BCE. It is not, in this lesson, a date for the rotunda and dome we now see.
Not this
Not a work to be dated to 27 BCE just because the inscription names Agrippa, and not a work to be dated to 27 BCE just because that year was last week’s conventional start of the principate. A later rebuild can keep an earlier name.
Connects to
Last week’s Prima Porta (a copy type) and Ara Pacis (conventional 13–9 BCE) already required a dating caution. The Pantheon is a third, different caution: an inscription and a fabric that are not the same date.

Two facts, kept apart:

  • The building we have — a circular temple, a concrete dome, an oculus (the open circular eye in the dome) — is generally treated as a Hadrianic rebuild of the early second century CE. This course uses a conventional range around 118–125 CE, and it does not invent a measured diameter or height.
  • The inscription on the porch names Agrippa, consul for the third time. That text belongs to an earlier Agrippan temple, conventionally associated with 27 BCE. Do not date the present rotunda and dome to 27 BCE because the letters are still there, or because 27 BCE is already on this course’s political timeline.

A public building can carry an older name. That is the caution, and the interest, of the Pantheon in this lesson. A later museum or tourist setting is not the Hadrianic context unchanged, but the building itself, unlike a statue moved into a museum, is still in Rome as a standing temple-turned-church; this lesson will not pretend to have described every later use.

Remember

The Pantheon we have is a Hadrianic rebuild, conventionally early second century CE (around 118–125). The Agrippa inscription is older, conventionally tied to 27 BCE, and does not date the present dome. Oculus named; no invented measurements.
