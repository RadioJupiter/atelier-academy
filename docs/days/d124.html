Latin · The deponent pluperfect, a second mixed practice

40 min · Method: more short original sentences, still choosing from the time of the action

Learn

Another mixed practice, not a new tense. Keep locūtus eram for a completed action already done before another past moment, loquēbar for ongoing or habitual past action, and locūtus sum for a completed action not marked as prior. The absolute puerō locūtō remains a loosely attached phrase, not a fifth tense. The first persons of sequor and ēgredior remain reserved.

Remember

The pluperfect is still locūtus eram, I had spoken. Mix it with the imperfect and the perfect by the time of the action, not by a new ending.

Watch me

Write a short original pair in which someone had tried, and then spoke.

  1. The trying is already completed before the speaking.
  2. A fair sentence: Puer cōnātus erat, et tum locūtus est.
  3. Cōnātus erat is pluperfect; locūtus est is perfect, a later completed action.
  4. Do not make both verbs pluperfect unless both actions are prior to some third past moment, which is not asked today.

Together

Write two short original sentences: one with locūtī erant and a later past verb, and one with loquēbantur as a habitual past.

Hint 1: Pluperfect

They had spoken, and then something else happened.

Hint 2: Imperfect

A word such as cotīdiē, daily, or a long ongoing scene, fits loquēbantur.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Write one original sentence with locūtī erant and a later past verb.
  2. Write one original sentence with loquēbantur as a habitual past.
  3. Why is puerō locūtō still not a pluperfect of the main verb?

Exercises

  1. Translate: Puella cōnāta erat, et tum locūta est.
  2. Choose: Cotīdiē puer ___ (cōnābātur / cōnātus erat).
  3. Give the six pluperfect forms of loquor once more, from memory.

Mathematics · Geometry LXX: skew lines, a first look

40 min · Method: two lines that do not meet, are not parallel, and do not lie in one plane

Learn

Key terms

skew lines
Definition
Two lines in space are skew if they are not parallel, do not meet, and do not lie in one and the same plane. Skew lines are a space relation; two lines in a single plane are either parallel or intersecting, and cannot be skew.
Example
On the invented box, the front bottom edge AB and the top back edge (the edge of the top face farthest from AB) are skew: they are not parallel, they do not meet, and no single plane contains both.
Not this
Not two parallel edges, which never meet but do lie in one plane (or in many, if one wishes to name a plane of two parallels). Not two edges that meet at a corner. Not two lines drawn on one sheet of paper.
Connects to
A line and a plane, a line perpendicular to a plane, and two planes in space.

In a single plane, two distinct lines either meet or are parallel. In space a third relation appears: two lines may fail to meet and also fail to be parallel, because they do not lie in one plane. Such lines are called skew. On the invented box, take the front bottom edge AB and the top back edge. Those two edges do not meet, are not parallel (one runs along the front of the floor, the other along the back of the top, in a different direction of depth), and no one face of the box contains both. They are skew.

By contrast, AB and DC are parallel and lie in the front plane. AB and AD meet at A. AB and EF are parallel and lie in the floor plane. None of those pairs is skew. The unit circle and radians remain reserved.

Remember

Skew lines do not meet, are not parallel, and do not lie in one plane. Two lines in a single plane cannot be skew.

Watch me

On the invented box, why are AB and the top back edge skew, and why are AB and DC not skew?

  1. AB and the top back edge do not meet: one is on the floor at the front, the other on the top at the back.
  2. They are not parallel: they do not run in the same direction through the box.
  3. No single face contains both, and they do not lie in one plane. So they are skew.
  4. AB and DC are parallel and both lie in the front plane, so they are not skew.

Together

On the same invented box, is the vertical edge DA skew to the right-hand bottom edge BF? Why or why not?

Hint 1: Do they meet?

DA and BF are different edges; they do not share a corner.

Hint 2: One plane?

If they are not parallel and do not meet, check whether some one plane contains both, or not.

Hint 1: Own words

Start from the definition, but do not copy it. If you can only repeat the card, close it and try again from the example you invent.

Hint 2: Near-miss

The "Not this" line on the card names a common mix-up. Explain why that mix-up would change an answer in this lesson.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. What three conditions make two lines skew?
  2. On the invented box, why are AB and the top back edge skew?
  3. Why can two lines drawn on one sheet of paper not be skew?
  4. For each of today's key terms (skew lines), write one sentence in your own words and one near-miss you must not confuse it with. Then apply one of the terms to a fresh case of your own (not the example on the card).

  2. Is DA skew to BF? Explain.
  3. Why is a pair of parallel floor edges not a skew pair?

Writing · A tighter original fable

40 min · Method: shorten the action, keep the moral at the end, cut a mid-story lecture

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • fable (progymnasmata) — Day 121

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Yesterday’s original fable is now tightened. Keep the same story if it already works, or write a new one in the same shape. Cut any sentence that lectures before the action is finished. Cut a second moral. Keep the narrative short enough to be a fable, not a short story. The moral remains one sentence, after the action, and must still follow from what happens. Townsend’s crow may be a contrast, not a source to copy today.

Remember

Shorten the action, keep one moral at the end, and cut a mid-story lecture.

Watch me

A loose draft, then a tighter one, on the invented ink jar.

  1. Loose: A jar of ink stands open, and everyone knows that open ink is a risk, which is why people should always close a jar, and then a sheet slides toward the rim, and this shows that care matters, and then the sheet is pulled back and the jar is closed.
  2. The middle lectures twice before the action is finished, and a second moral (“care matters”) is inserted.
  3. Tighter: A jar of ink stands open on a desk. A loose sheet slides toward the rim, is pulled back, and the jar is then closed. Moral: A lid is cheaper than a spoiled page.
  4. The tighter version has a complete short action and one moral, stated after it.

Together

Take an original fable (yesterday’s, or a new one) and write a tighter version: short action, one moral at the end, no mid-story lecture.

Hint 1: What to cut

Advice in the middle, a second moral, or a scene that does not change the action.

Hint 2: What to keep

The events that the moral depends on, and one moral sentence at the close.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Write a tighter version of an original fable, with a short action and one moral at the end.
  2. Name one sentence you cut, and why it was a lecture or a second moral.
  3. How can you tell that the moral still follows from the tightened action?

Exercises

  1. Compare your tighter version with Townsend’s crow: what shape do they share, without sharing the story?
  2. Why is a mid-story lecture a miss even if the advice is sound?
  3. Write the moral alone, as one sentence.

Science · Hematite, and a red from a yellow ochre, a first look

40 min · Method: Fe2O3 as anhydrous hematite; USGS Bulletin 430-G on calcining yellow ocher

Learn

Key terms

hematite
Definition
Hematite is the anhydrous iron oxide Fe2O3. In the USGS pigment sources used this week, hematite is the usual red iron-oxide mineral, and earthy hematite is a red ochre. It is not a hydrated yellow ochre until that hydration is taken into account as a different mineral.
Origin
From Greek haimatites lithos, bloodlike stone, from haima, blood (Etymonline).
Example
A red earth pigment whose color comes from Fe2O3 is a hematite ochre in this sense.
Not this
Not goethite or limonite, the hydrated oxides that give yellow, buff, or brown. Not an official iron percentage invented for a classroom lump. Not lapis lazuli.
Connects to
Tuesday’s ochre. Bulletin 430-G also notes that a red ocher can be made by calcining common yellow ocher, because the color depends mainly on the degree of hydration.

Tuesday’s ochre is now named more closely. Hematite is the anhydrous iron oxide Fe2O3. The USGS 2019 Yearbook lists hematite as the usual red iron-oxide pigment mineral. Bulletin 430-G adds that, because the color of ocher depends mainly on the degree of hydration, “a red ocher can be made by calcining common yellow ocher.” Calcining here means heating so as to drive off water, not a new official industrial recipe with invented temperatures. The formula Fe2O3 is the mineral’s composition, not a percentage of iron in a classroom lump; invent no official percentage. Lapis lazuli and ultramarine remain reserved.

Remember

Hematite is Fe2O3, the usual red. A red ocher can be made by calcining a common yellow ocher, because color hangs on hydration. Invent no official percentage or temperature.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 1: Netherfield, and the conversation

40 min · Method: the 1813 dialogue after the opening; figures in the novel are the novel’s figures

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • ironic opening (Pride and Prejudice) — Day 122

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

The 1813 Chapter 1 continues. Mrs. Bennet asks whether Mr. Bennet has heard that “Netherfield Park is let at last,” and, when he will not ask, tells him that “Netherfield is taken by a young man of large fortune from the north of England,” that he “agreed with Mr. Morris immediately,” that he is “to take possession before Michaelmas,” and that his name is Bingley. Asked whether he is married or single, she says, “Oh! single, my dear, to be sure! A single man of large fortune; four or five thousand a year. What a fine thing for our girls!” Mr. Bennet asks how that can affect them; she answers that she is “thinking of his marrying one of them,” and that therefore “you must visit him as soon as he comes.” Later in the same chapter he says that “Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters,” and she replies that he has “no compassion on my poor nerves.” He answers, “I have a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you mention them with consideration these twenty years at least.”

Keep figures inside the novel. “Four or five thousand a year” is Mrs. Bennet’s report in the book, not an official salary. “Twenty years at least” is Mr. Bennet’s wording, not an official biography. The words “girls,” “daughters,” and “children,” when they appear, are the novel’s, and stay inside quotation or clearly in the book’s household. Do not add an official identification of a character with a living person. The rest of Chapter 1, and Chapter 2, remain for later close reading if a sentence is not set today.

Remember

Netherfield is let, in the novel, to a single man named Bingley, “four or five thousand a year” in Mrs. Bennet’s report. Mr. Bennet prefers Lizzy’s “quickness,” and treats “nerves” as “old friends.” These are the book’s figures and jokes, not official facts.

Seminar · Economics: a sales tax on an invented price, a first look

40 min · Method: a named percent added to an invented price; 5 percent of $80 is $4, total $84

Learn

Key terms

sales tax (invented figures)
Definition
A sales tax, in this lesson, is a named percent of an invented price, added to that price to give a total. It is not a discount, and it is not an official living tax rate. Today’s invented figures: 5 percent of $80 is $4, and the total is $84.
Example
An invented $80, with an invented 5 percent sales tax: the tax is 0.05 × 80 = 4, and the total is 80 + 4 = 84, or 80 × 1.05 = 84.
Not this
Not a 5 percent discount, which would leave $76. Not last term’s markup-then-discount. Not an official tax rate of a real place, and not a form to be filed.
Connects to
Term 3 economics begins with taxes on invented figures. Insurance and later macroeconomics remain reserved. Last term’s discounts and markups remain a contrast.

Term 3 economics begins with a first look at a tax, on invented figures only. A sales tax here is a named percent of an invented price, added to that price. It is not a discount, and it is not an official rate of a real place. Today’s invented figures: a price of $80, and a sales tax of 5 percent. The tax is 0.05 × 80 = $4. The total is 80 + 4 = $84, which is also 80 × 1.05 = $84. A miss is to treat the 5 percent as a discount and leave $76. Another miss is to treat last term’s markup-then-discount as if it were this tax. Invent no official living tax rate, and do not treat the $80 as an official price of a real good.

Remember

A sales tax is added to the price. Invented: 5 percent of $80 is $4, total $84. A discount leaving $76 is a miss. This is not an official rate.
