Remember

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday mix, not a new relation. Use the invented classroom box. A line may lie in a plane, meet it at one point, or be parallel to it. A line is perpendicular to a plane when it meets the plane at a foot and is perpendicular to two intersecting lines in the plane through that foot. Two distinct planes intersect in a line or are parallel. Skew lines do not meet, are not parallel, and do not lie in one plane. The unit circle, radians, and the law of sines remain reserved. Invent no official room measurement.

Remember

Keep the four relations separate: a line with a plane, a perpendicular line, two planes, and a skew pair.

Watch me

On the invented box, classify DA with the floor, the front plane with the floor, and AB with the top back edge.

  1. DA meets the floor at A and is perpendicular to AB and AE, so DA is perpendicular to the floor.
  2. The front plane and the floor intersect in the line AB.
  3. AB and the top back edge are skew: they do not meet, are not parallel, and do not lie in one plane.

Hint 1: DC and CB

Hint 2: AB and DC

They are parallel and both lie in the front plane.

Physics · Friday mix: interference, standing waves, and beat frequency, a first look at beats

40 min · Friday mix of 16.10, with a first look at f_B = |f1 − f2|

Learn

Key terms

beat frequency
Definition
The beat frequency, in OpenStax 16.10, is f_B = |f1 − f2|, the frequency at which two waves of similar but not identical frequencies fluctuate in amplitude, from constructive to destructive interference. The resulting wave has the average frequency f_ave of the two.
Example
OpenStax: if a tuning fork is 256 Hz and two beats per second are heard, the other frequency is either 254 or 258 Hz. An invented pair, 440 Hz and 444 Hz, has f_B = 4 Hz and f_ave = 442 Hz.
Not this
Not the average frequency itself, which is what you hear as the pitch when the two frequencies are close. Not an official pitch standard claimed for a living piano today. 256 Hz is the book’s example, not a classroom invention.
Connects to
Superposition and constructive and destructive interference. Standing waves remain in the Friday mix as already taught.

OpenStax 16.10 closes this week with beats. Two waves of similar but not identical frequencies superpose and fluctuate in amplitude, alternately constructive and destructive. The beat frequency is f_B = |f1 − f2|. The resulting wave has the average frequency f_ave of the two. The book’s official example: a tuning fork of 256 Hz, with two beats per second, means the other frequency is either 254 or 258 Hz. An invented pair used for practice: 440 Hz and 444 Hz, so f_B = 4 Hz and f_ave = 442 Hz. Invent no official living pitch standard. Standing waves, already taught, return in the mix.

Remember

Beats: f_B = |f1 − f2|, and the heard pitch is near f_ave. The official 256 Hz example gives 254 or 258 Hz if two beats per second are heard.

Watch me

If a 256 Hz fork gives two beats per second, what are the two possible frequencies of the other source? Then, for 440 Hz and 444 Hz, invented, find f_B and f_ave.

  1. f_B = 2 Hz, so the other frequency is 256 − 2 = 254 Hz, or 256 + 2 = 258 Hz.
  2. For the invented pair, f_B = |444 − 440| = 4 Hz.
  3. f_ave = (440 + 444) / 2 = 442 Hz.
  4. 256 Hz is the book’s figure. 440 and 444 are invented. Do not call either an official living pitch standard today.

Together

Recompute λ1 and f1 for the invented string L = 2.00 m, vw = 80.0 m/s, and say what pure destructive interference does to two identical waves.

Hint 1: The string

λ1 = 2L, f1 = vw / (2L).

Hint 2: Destructive

Crest with trough; the resulting amplitude is zero.

Hint 1: Own words

Hint 2: Near-miss

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: a cervical vertebra, and the foramen transversarium, from Gray 1918

40 min · Method: Gray’s distinguishing mark of a cervical vertebra; atlas, axis, and C7 reserved

Learn

Key terms

foramen transversarium
Definition
In Henry Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 20th ed., 1918, “The Cervical Vertebrae,” as posted at theodora.com, the cervical vertebrae “are the smallest of the true vertebrae, and can be readily distinguished from those of the thoracic or lumbar regions by the presence of a foramen in each transverse process.” That opening is the foramen transversarium. In the upper six vertebrae it “gives passage to the vertebral artery and vein and a plexus of sympathetic nerves.”
Example
A typical cervical vertebra (one of the remaining four, after Gray sets the first, second, and seventh aside as exceptional) is recognized by a foramen in each transverse process.
Not this
Not the already-taught typical vertebra of last term as if it had no regional mark, and not the mental foramen of the mandible. Not the atlas, the axis, or the vertebra prominens, which Gray says “must be separately described” and which remain reserved. Official lengths remain reserved.
Connects to
Last term’s typical vertebra, from Gray. The first, second, and seventh cervical vertebrae remain reserved as separate first looks.

Friday’s elective continues Henry Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 20th ed., ed. Warren H. Lewis, 1918, now “The Cervical Vertebrae,” as posted at https://www.theodora.com/anatomy/the_cervical_vertebrae.html. Last term’s typical vertebra is the starting point; today a regional mark. Gray: the cervical vertebrae “are the smallest of the true vertebrae, and can be readily distinguished from those of the thoracic or lumbar regions by the presence of a foramen in each transverse process.” The transverse processes “are each pierced by the foramen transversarium, which, in the upper six vertebrae, gives passage to the vertebral artery and vein and a plexus of sympathetic nerves.” The first, second, and seventh “present exceptional features and must be separately described”; “the following characteristics are common to the remaining four.” Those exceptional three remain reserved. Official lengths remain reserved. Invent no official diameter of the foramen.

Gray also notes, of those remaining four, a short bifid spinous process, and a body “broader from side to side than from before backward.” Those may be noticed, but the distinguishing mark taught today is the foramen in each transverse process.

Remember

A cervical vertebra is distinguished, in Gray, by a foramen in each transverse process, the foramen transversarium. In the upper six it gives passage to the vertebral artery and vein and a plexus of sympathetic nerves. Atlas, axis, and the seventh remain reserved. Official lengths remain reserved.

Learn

Remember

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the pluperfect of a deponent, and why is locūtus sum a miss if the speaking was already done before a later past action?
  2. The pluperfect is the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum: locūtus eram, I had spoken.
  3. Locūtus sum is the perfect, I spoke or I have spoken, and does not mark the action as already completed before another past moment.
  4. A second miss is loquēbar, the imperfect, I was speaking or I used to speak, which has no participle.
