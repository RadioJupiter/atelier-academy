Latin · Deponent perfect, present, and the two absolutes, mixed

40 min · Method: a mixed practice of this week’s perfect with last week’s present table and with the two times of the ablative absolute

Learn

Key terms

deponent tenses and absolutes, mixed
Definition
Today the deponent perfect (locūtus sum) is mixed with the already-taught present (loquor) and with the two times of the ablative absolute: a present-participle absolute is contemporaneous with the main verb; a perfect-participle absolute is already complete when the main verb happens. The perfect tense uses sum; the absolute does not.
Example
Ego locūtus sum. Nunc tū loqueris. Magistrō recitante, puerī silent. Urbe captā, agricolae fugiunt.

Not a first look at any of these four, except that the perfect tense was new this week. Classroom sentences only.

Mathematics · Geometry LXI: sine and cosine of complementary angles

45 min · Method: in a right triangle the two acute angles add to 90°, and sin(90° − A) = cos A. Degrees only

Learn

Key terms

sine and cosine of complementary angles
Definition
The two acute angles in a right triangle are complementary: they add to 90°. The side opposite A is the side adjacent to the other acute angle, B, and B = 90° − A. So sin A = cos B, and sin(90° − A) = cos A. In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5 and cos A = 4/5, while sin B = 4/5 and cos B = 3/5. Degrees only; the radian remains reserved.
Example
If sin A = 5/13 in a classroom 5-12-13 triangle, then cos(90° − A) = 5/13, the same ratio, because 90° − A is the other acute angle, adjacent to 5.
Not this
Not a first look at sine or cosine, already taught, or at the fact that the acute angles of a right triangle add to 90°, which follows from the already-taught angle sum of a triangle. Not a unit-circle identity beyond this triangle fact. Classroom lengths remain invented.
Connects to
Monday’s sine, Tuesday’s cosine, and the already-taught angle sum of a triangle.

The angle sum of a triangle is already taught, 180°. In a right triangle, one angle is 90°, so the two acute angles add to 90°. They are complementary.

The side opposite A is the side adjacent to the other acute angle, B. So the sine of A, opposite over hypotenuse, is the same ratio as the cosine of B, adjacent over hypotenuse. Since B = 90° − A, sin A = cos(90° − A), and sin(90° − A) = cos A.

In the classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5 and cos A = 4/5. Then B, opposite 4, has sin B = 4/5 = cos A, and cos B = 3/5 = sin A. Degrees only.

Physics · Damping and the shape of a resonance peak, from OpenStax 16.8

40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.8 on how damping changes the resonance curve. Less damping: a higher and narrower peak. Monday’s resonance is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

damping and the resonance peak
Definition
OpenStax 16.8, with its graph of amplitude against driving frequency, shows that resonance still occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, for each amount of damping shown. The greatest response, the highest peak, is for the least damping, because less energy is removed by the damping force. The least damping also gives the narrowest peak: the system responds strongly only very near the natural frequency. Heavy damping lowers the amplitude and makes the system respond at more frequencies. These are OpenStax’s qualitative claims from that figure, not invented official peak heights.
Example
OpenStax’s contrast: piano strings and many musical instruments use little damping, so they can resonate at a very specific frequency. A car’s suspension is built with heavy damping so that the amplitude stays small, at the cost of responding at more frequencies. Those are kinds of design, not official measured periods invented here. Tacoma Narrows, 1940, is named by OpenStax as a collapse in which wind drove a bridge at a resonant frequency after damping decreased; this course invents no official wind speed.
Not this
Not a first look at Monday’s definitions of driving frequency, natural frequency, and resonance, or at last week’s three names for damped motion, except as already-taught damping now used to read the peak. Not a quantitative formula for peak width, which 16.8 does not give for use here.
Connects to
Monday’s resonance, last week’s damping, and OpenStax 16.8’s figure of amplitude against driving frequency.

Monday defined resonance: driving at the natural frequency, when the amplitude is greatest. Today OpenStax 16.8’s graph is used to see how damping changes that peak.

All the curves in the figure peak where the driving frequency equals the natural frequency. The highest peak, and the narrowest, is for the least damping, because less energy is removed by the damping force. Heavy damping lowers the amplitude and spreads the response over more frequencies.

OpenStax’s contrast: little damping if you want a very specific resonant frequency, as in many musical instruments; heavy damping if you want small amplitude, as in a car’s suspension, with the tradeoff that the system then responds at more frequencies. A named 1940 bridge collapse is cited by OpenStax as a driven oscillation at a resonant frequency after damping decreased; this course invents no official wind speed.

Definition
The irregular imperfect of ver is veía, veías, veía, veíamos, veíais, veían. It looks like last week’s regular -er imperfect (comía), and in fact uses the same -ía endings, but the stem keeps e: veía, not vía. In this first look, veía is “I was seeing,” or “I used to see,” an ongoing or habitual past action, contrasted with the already-taught preterite vi. Iba and era, already taught this week, remain different verbs. Instruction remains in English. Classroom sentences are invented.
Example
Cuando yo iba, yo veía la plaza, in an invented classroom sentence, not a real address. Todos los días veíamos. Ayer yo vi, the already-taught preterite.
Not this
Not a first look at iba or era, already taught this week, or at last week’s comía, except as the endings veía shares. Not a first look at vi. Instruction remains in English.

Seminar · Art history: Dewing’s unarmed figure, reread

40 min · Method: a reread of Dewing I.2.7–12, already taught in history this week, now as a question of what the statue is made to show, and what it is made not to show

Learn

Key terms

the unarmed figure and the cross, reread
Definition
Dewing I.2.7–12, already read in this week’s history, is reread today as a question of what the sculptor is made to signify. The figure is “habited like Achilles,” looks toward the rising sun, and holds in the left hand a globe “by which the sculptor signifies that the whole earth and sea are subject to him, yet he has neither sword nor spear nor any other weapon, but a cross stands upon the globe which he carries, the emblem by which alone he has obtained both his Empire and his victory in war.” The right hand is stretched toward the rising sun and “commands the barbarians in that quarter to remain at home.” A reread holds that combination — rule signified, and a weapon refused — without inventing an official height, and without turning Dewing’s “I suppose” about the Persians into a proven official campaign fact.
