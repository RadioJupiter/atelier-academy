Latin · Capitulum II, part 1: gender and the genitive

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. II

Learn

Key terms

genitive
Definition
The case that often marks a possessor or a ‘of’ relation: whose thing it is.
Origin
From Latin genetīvus, used to translate a Greek name for this case; from gignere (‘to beget’), though the Greek term actually meant ‘generic case’.
Example
A question such as cuius? (‘whose?’) looks forward to a genitive answer, and the ending of the noun will later show that case.
Not this
The genitive is not the same as gender, and cuius is not itself the genitive ending of every noun.
Connects to
Gender (Day 2 and today) is a class of the noun. Case (genitive, later accusative) is a role in the sentence. Both are needed for agreement.
quis and quid
Definition
Question words: quis, ‘who?’, for a person; quid, ‘what?’, for a thing.
Example
Quis est? Puer est. Quid est? Oppidum est.
Not this
Quis is not used for a town, and quid is not used for a person. Oppidum is neuter, not masculine.
Connects to
The answers still use est from Day 1, and the gender of the noun (puer, oppidum) is already in play.

Latin nouns have gender (masculine, feminine, or neuter), which you cannot always guess from the meaning. Adjectives and some pronouns follow that gender. Today's chapter work is on gender and the first patterns of Capitulum II. Learn the words here, then read the book with the pattern in mind.

Notice: three genders. Nouns are masculine (most in -us, some in -r, such as puer, vir), feminine (most in -a) or neuter (-um).

Mathematics · Geometry II: congruent triangles and the isosceles triangle

60 min · Kiselev, Planimetry, triangles

Learn

Key terms

congruence △ABC ≅ △DEF
Definition
A matching of two figures so that corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal; one can be placed exactly on the other.
Origin
From Latin congruentia, from congruere (‘to agree, come together’).
Example
SSS, SAS, or ASA, with the correspondence named, proves two triangles congruent.
Not this
AAA is not a congruence test: it shows the same shape, not necessarily the same size.
Connects to
A congruence proof uses the axioms and theorems of Day 2, with reasons written for each step.
isosceles
Definition
A triangle with two sides equal. The angles at the base (opposite those sides) are equal.
Origin
From Latin īsoscelēs, from Ancient Greek ἰσοσκελής (‘equal-legged’), from ἴσος (‘equal’) + σκέλος (‘leg’).
Example
If AB = AC and ∠A = 40°, each base angle is 70°.
Not this
Isosceles does not mean ‘all three sides equal’ (that is equilateral, which is a special isosceles triangle).
Connects to
The base-angle theorem is proved by congruence: the triangle is compared with itself with the equal sides swapped.

Two triangles are congruent when one can be fitted exactly onto the other: corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal. Standard tests (taught as axioms or early theorems, depending on the book) include SSS, SAS, ASA, and often RHS for right triangles. You must name the correspondence (A to D, B to E, C to F) or the equalities are meaningless.

Congruence means one figure can be placed exactly on the other. Kiselev proves the first criterion by superposition; the others follow:

  • SAS: two sides and the included angle equal ⇒ triangles congruent.
  • ASA: a side and the two angles adjacent to it equal ⇒ congruent.
  • SSS: three sides equal ⇒ congruent.

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal; this is how congruence proves other facts.

Theorem. In an isosceles triangle the angles at the base are equal. Proof. Let AB = AC. Compare triangle ABC with triangle ACB (the same triangle, read with B and C swapped): AB = AC, AC = AB, and the included angle A is common. By SAS they are congruent, so ∠B (in the first) = ∠C (in the second). ∎

Write proofs in two columns until the habit is automatic: statement | reason.

Remember

Congruent means same shape and same size, with a stated correspondence. SSS, SAS, ASA (and RHS for right triangles) are the usual tests. AAA is not enough (that gives only similarity).

Key terms: congruence, isosceles.

Watch me

Triangle ABC is isosceles with AB = AC and ∠A = 40°. Find the base angles, and name the reason.

  1. The base angles are equal (in an isosceles triangle the angles at the base are equal).
  2. The three angles add to 180°, so each base angle is (180° − 40°) / 2 = 70°.
  3. So ∠B = ∠C = 70°.

Hint 2: Base angles

The angles at the base are equal: ∠PQR = ∠PRQ, because they sit opposite the equal sides PR and PQ.

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 1: making hints

45 min · Method: Benjamin Franklin's reconstruction exercise (Day 2's passage describes it); model text: Addison, The Spectator No. 10, 12 March 1711 (public domain)

Learn

Key terms

Franklin's method
Definition
A practice from Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography: take a passage, jot brief hints of the ideas in order, put the passage away, and rewrite from the hints; then compare with the original.
Example
Original idea: the city was short of grain, so the council opened the stores. Hints: city — short of grain / council — opened stores. Then rewrite without looking.
Not this
The hints are not allowed to be a full copy of the sentence. If you can read the original off your notes, the method has failed.
Connects to
This is imitatio (Day 2) with the model removed. You are training memory for structure, not copying words.
hint (in Franklin's method)
Definition
A brief cue for an idea, written in order, just strong enough to recall the thought and not the original wording.
Example
‘city — short of grain / council — opened stores’ is a pair of hints. It is not a draft of the sentence.
Not this
A hint is not a quotation, and it is not an outline of a whole essay (that comes on Day 6).
Connects to
Franklin’s method uses hints as the only bridge between reading and rewriting.

Franklin's method, as he describes it in his Autobiography: take a passage, jot brief notes (hints) of the ideas in order, put the passage away, then rewrite from the hints and compare. A later step is to scramble the hints and practice finding a good order. Today you make the hints and rewrite.

Franklin's method has four steps: (1) read a model paragraph closely; (2) write a short hint for each sentence (a few words recording the idea, not the wording); (3) put it away for several days; (4) rebuild the paragraph from the hints alone, then compare with the original and correct. You will do steps 1–2 today and step 4 on Day 7.

The model (The Spectator, No. 10; the original capitals and spelling are kept):

It is with much Satisfaction that I hear this great City inquiring Day by Day after these my Papers, and receiving my Morning Lectures with a becoming Seriousness and Attention. My Publisher tells me, that there are already Three Thousand of them distributed every Day: So that if I allow Twenty Readers to every Paper, which I look upon as a modest Computation, I may reckon about Threescore thousand Disciples in London and Westminster, who I hope will take care to distinguish themselves from the thoughtless Herd of their ignorant and unattentive Brethren. Since I have raised to myself so great an Audience, I shall spare no Pains to make their Instruction agreeable, and their Diversion useful. For which Reasons I shall endeavour to enliven Morality with Wit, and to temper Wit with Morality, that my Readers may, if possible, both Ways find their account in the Speculation of the Day. And to the End that their Virtue and Discretion may not be short transient intermitting Starts of Thought, I have resolved to refresh their Memories from Day to Day, till I have recovered them out of that desperate State of Vice and Folly, into which the Age is fallen. The Mind that lies fallow but a single Day, sprouts up in Follies that are only to be killed by a constant and assiduous Culture. It was said of Socrates, that he brought Philosophy down from Heaven, to inhabit among Men; and I shall be ambitious to have it said of me, that I have brought Philosophy out of Closets and Libraries, Schools and Colleges, to dwell in Clubs and Assemblies, at Tea-tables, and in Coffee-houses.

Remember

Notes are short: nouns and verbs, not full copied sentences. Rewrite from the notes only. Then compare with the original and mark what you missed.

Science · Combustion and gases: the quantitative model

45 min · Phenomenon continued; olympiad-style problems

Learn

Key terms

combustion
Definition
The chemical burning of a fuel with oxygen, producing new gases (and often water) and releasing heat.
Origin
From Old French combustion, from Latin combustio, from combūrere (‘to burn up’), from com- + ūrere (‘to burn’).
Example
Burning wax consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water vapor. A candle goes out long before all the oxygen is gone.
Not this
Combustion is not ‘using up air and leaving an empty space of the same volume as the oxygen’. The new gases and the heat both matter.
Connects to
Day 2’s candle-under-the-jar phenomenon is a combustion experiment; the water rise is mostly heating, escape of gas, and cooling, not the oxygen fraction alone.
quantitative
Definition
Described with measured numbers and a relation, not only with words such as ‘more’ or ‘less’.
Origin
From Late Latin quantitātīvus; in English, quantity + -ative.
Example
If trapped air cools from 350 K to 293 K at constant pressure, its volume shrinks by about 16% (V is proportional to T).
Not this
A number without a unit, or a calculation that does not match an observation, is not yet a quantitative explanation.
Connects to
Day 2 asked for an estimate of the same kind. Today the measurements and the gas relations have to agree.

Combustion of wax. Paraffin wax is a mixture of alkanes, roughly C₂₅H₅₂. Complete combustion: C₂₅H₅₂ + 38 O₂ → 25 CO₂ + 26 H₂O. (Balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen: 25 × 2 + 26 = 76 O atoms = 38 O₂.)

Ideal gas law. pV = nRT, with T in kelvin (K = °C + 273.15). At constant pressure, V ∝ T; at constant volume, p ∝ T.

Olympiad habit: estimate before calculating, keep units in every line, and check the order of magnitude at the end.

Problems

  1. Balance the combustion of methane (CH₄) and of propane (C₃H₈).
  2. Per molecule of O₂ consumed, how many molecules of CO₂ does burning C₂₅H₅₂ release? If all the water condensed, by what fraction would the number of gas molecules from the reaction fall, per O₂ used?
  3. A 1.0 L jar of air at 20 °C is sealed by water. About how many moles of gas does it hold? (Use 24 L/mol at 20 °C and 1 atm.) What volume does 21% of that gas occupy?
  4. The trapped air is heated from 20 °C to 80 °C at constant pressure. What fraction of it escapes as bubbles? (Hint: volume at 80 °C compared with the jar's volume.)
  5. Estimate: how much does a typical candle's mass decrease in one hour? Explain your assumptions, then check it with a kitchen scale over an hour (with supervision).

Remember

Combustion of a hydrocarbon consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water; it also releases heat. A quantitative model must match measured volumes and temperatures, not only the word 'burning'. Day 2 already showed that the water rise in the jar is not simply the 21% oxygen fraction.

Key terms: combustion, quantitative.

Watch me

Why a quantitative check is needed, as a model.

  1. The chemical facts (oxygen in, CO₂ and water out) are true, and still do not by themselves tell you how far the water will rise.
  2. If you measured a rise, you can compare it with a calculation: for example, a temperature drop at constant pressure shrinks volume in the ratio of the Kelvin temperatures.
  3. If the calculated shrink is close to the rise you saw, heating and cooling is enough to explain most of the phenomenon. If not, the model is incomplete. That comparison is the point of today's work.

Together

A run of the jar experiment gives a water rise of 14% of the jar's height. The cooling calculation (293/350) predicted about 16%. What do you conclude, and what would make you change your mind?

Hint 1: Close, not exact

14% and 16% are the same size of effect; cooling can account for most of the rise. Measurement and the estimated 350 K are both rough.

Hint 2: What would change your mind

A rise consistently near 21% with no sign of bubbles or of a post-flame rise, or a much larger rise than any reasonable cooling, would force the model to change.

Literature · Iliad, Books 2–3

45 min · Read Books 2–3 before this lesson (the Catalogue of Ships in Book 2 may be skimmed)

Learn

Key terms

catalogue
Definition
A formal list built into a poem. The Catalogue of Ships in Iliad Book 2 is a roll-call of the Greek contingents.
Origin
From Late Latin catalogus, from Ancient Greek κατάλογος (‘a listing, a register’), from καταλέγειν (‘to recount, list’).
Example
The poet can name each contingent and its leader; listeners hear their own places in the army of the poem.
Not this
It is not a random inventory, and it is not required that you memorize every name. This course allows the Catalogue to be skimmed.
Connects to
Book 2 also tests Agamemnon’s leadership: the army nearly runs to the ships before the roll-call ends the book.
simile
Definition
A comparison that names itself with ‘like’ or ‘as’, often extended over several lines in Homer, usually from nature or ordinary work.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin simile (‘comparison, likeness’), the neuter of similis (‘like, similar’).
Example
An extended comparison of a warrior to a lion, or of a falling man to a tree cut down, is a Homeric simile.
Not this
A simile is not an epithet, and it is not a metaphor (a metaphor does not say ‘like’ or ‘as’).
Connects to
Day 2 asked you to find one simile or epithet in Book 1. Today you keep quoting with line numbers from your translation.

Book 2. Zeus sends Agamemnon a false dream promising victory. Agamemnon tests the army by proposing to sail home; the army rushes to the ships. Odysseus restores order and strikes Thersites, the one common soldier who speaks in the assembly. The book ends with the long Catalogue of Ships, a roll-call of the Greek contingents.

Book 3. Paris (Alexandros) offers to settle the war in single combat with Menelaus. On the wall of Troy, Helen names the Greek leaders for Priam. Menelaus wins the duel, but Aphrodite snatches Paris away to safety in his bedroom and summons Helen to him.

Remember

Book 2: a false dream, Agamemnon's test, the rush to the ships, Odysseus and Thersites, the Catalogue of Ships (which may be skimmed). Book 3: the duel of Paris and Menelaus, Helen on the wall, Aphrodite's rescue of Paris. Thersites says some of the same things Achilles said; the poem treats them differently because of rank.

Key terms: catalogue, simile.

Watch me

Why the army nearly runs home in Book 2, as a model.

  1. The army is exhausted after nine years; Agamemnon misjudges his men when he tests them by proposing to sail home.
  2. Only Odysseus's intervention restores order. The episode reveals a failure of leadership, not only a moment of panic.
  3. The Catalogue then puts the whole army back on the page, named and numbered, after it had almost dissolved.

Together

Hint 1: Presentation

He is a commoner and is presented as ugly and insolent; Odysseus strikes him, and the army laughs.

Hint 2: The tension

The poem treats speech against the leaders as a privilege of rank, though a careful reader may notice that his complaints are not simply wrong.

Atelier · Value: the nine-step scale

90 min

Learn

Key terms

value
Definition
How light or dark a surface is, considered separately from its color.
Origin
From Old French value, from valoir (‘to be worth’), from Latin valēre.
Example
A nine-step value scale is a row of grays from the white of the paper to the darkest mark, each step visually even.
Not this
Value is not color, and it is not ‘how important the object is’. A pale yellow and a pale blue can have the same value.
Connects to
Contour (Day 3) settled the edges. Modeling on Day 5 will use this scale to turn form.
nine-step scale
Definition
A row of nine even steps of gray from white to black, used so that values in a drawing can be chosen and compared.
Example
Box 1 is the paper; box 9 is the darkest charcoal or graphite you will use; the middle box should look halfway, not ‘a bit too light’.
Not this
The steps must be visually even, not even only in how hard you pressed if the result looks uneven.
Connects to
Value is the quality; the scale is the tool that trains the eye to judge it.

A nine-step value scale is a row of grays from white to black, each step visually even. Value is how light or dark a surface is, separate from its color. Even steps matter: if step 5 is too dark, every later judgment of mid-gray will be off. Squint to compare the steps at arm's length.

Value is lightness or darkness, independent of color. Before modeling form, the eye must judge value accurately. (The Munsell color system, a standard reference, measures value on a scale from 0 for pure black to 10 for pure white.)

Build a nine-step scale. Draw nine 2 cm squares in a row. Square 1 is the white of the paper; square 9 is the darkest graphite you can achieve (layered 2B or softer). Square 5 is the visual middle. Then fill 3 and 7 halfway between, then 2, 4, 6 and 8. Steps must look equally spaced; that is a judgment for the eye, not for the pencil pressure.

Technique. Apply graphite in even layers of light, overlapping strokes in several directions; don't press hard early (it polishes the paper and stops later layers). Squint to compare values: squinting reduces detail and makes values easier to judge.

Remember

Nine even steps, white to black. Squint to check. No sudden jump. Step 5 should look like a true middle gray.

Key terms: value, nine-step scale.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Draw a row of nine equal boxes on mid-weight paper.
  2. Leave box 1 the white of the paper. Fill box 9 with the darkest charcoal or graphite you can lay evenly.
  3. Mix a gray for box 5 that, when you squint, sits halfway between 1 and 9.
  4. Place 3 halfway between 1 and 5, and 7 halfway between 5 and 9; then fill 2, 4, 6, 8 the same way.
  5. At arm's length, squint: if a step jumps, lighten or darken it before you use the scale on a drawing.

Together

If step 5 looks closer to black than to white, what do you do?

Hint 1: Adjust

Lighten step 5 until it sits in the middle, then repair 6–8 if they have followed it down.

Hint 2: Check

Squint again from the same distance.

Economics & personal finance · Scarcity and opportunity cost

30 min · Seminar rotation: Economics (Thursdays)

Learn

Key terms

scarcity
Definition
The fact that resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up.
Origin
From Middle English scarsite, from Norman French escarsete; in English, scarce + -ity.
Example
A Saturday afternoon can be spent on a paid shift, a lecture, or painting, but not on all three.
Not this
Scarcity is not ‘poverty only’, and it is not ‘having zero of something’. A person with money still has scarce time.
Connects to
Because of scarcity, each choice has an opportunity cost: the best alternative given up.
opportunity cost
Definition
The value of the single best alternative given up when a choice is made, not the sum of every alternative.
Origin
Opportunity is from Latin opportūnitās (via French); cost is the ordinary English word. The pair is a technical term in economics.
Example
If you paint, the opportunity cost is whichever of the paid shift or the lecture you valued more — one of them, not both.
Not this
It is not ‘$60 plus the lecture’, because you could not have done both of the things you gave up at the same time.
Connects to
Scarcity forces a choice. Opportunity cost names what that choice actually costs.
sunk cost
Definition
A cost already paid that cannot be recovered, and that should not affect a forward-looking decision.
Example
A $40 workshop ticket is spent whether you go or stay home. If you would rather draw at home, the $40 should not drag you to the workshop.
Not this
A sunk cost is not an opportunity cost, and ‘I already paid’ is not a good reason to continue.
Connects to
Opportunity cost looks forward to the best alternative still open. A sunk cost looks backward and should be ignored.

Scarcity: resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up. The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative given up, not the sum of every alternative.

Thinking at the margin: decisions are about one more unit: one more hour of practice, one more item. Compare the extra benefit with the extra cost.

Sunk costs are costs already paid that cannot be recovered. They should not affect a forward-looking decision.

Remember

Scarcity: resources are limited relative to wants, so every choice gives something up. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up, not the sum of every alternative. Sunk costs are already paid and should not affect a forward-looking decision. Think at the margin: one more unit, extra benefit versus extra cost.

Key terms: scarcity, opportunity cost, sunk cost.

Watch me

Opportunity cost of choosing to paint on a Saturday, as a model.

  1. The alternatives were (a) a $60 shift, (b) a free lecture, (c) painting. You choose painting.
  2. You could only have done one of the others, so the cost is one: whichever of the shift or the lecture you valued more. It is not '$60 plus the lecture'.
  3. The $60 is the money you did not earn if the shift was the best alternative; it is not automatically the opportunity cost if you valued the lecture more.
