Latin · Capitulum XVI: fiō, and more deponents in the third person

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XVI continues. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

fiō, fierī
Definition
Fiō, fierī means to become, to be made, or to happen. In this first look the course uses the third person and the infinitive: fit, it becomes, it is made, it happens; fiunt, they become; fierī, to become.
Example
Tempestās fit. — A storm arises, or a storm happens. Discipulī tacitī fiunt. — The pupils become silent.
Not this
Not a first look at sum, es, est, and not a first look at a deponent. Fiō is an irregular verb, not a deponent, though fierī can sit near deponents in this chapter. Do not invent first- and second-person forms of fiō today; they are reserved.
Connects to
Yesterday’s deponents look passive and mean active. Fiō is a different verb, taught here in the third person, for becoming or happening.
sequitur and ēgreditur
Definition
Sequitur means he or she follows; ēgreditur means he or she goes out. Both are deponents, used today in the third person and as infinitives (sequī, ēgredī), like yesterday’s loquitur and cōnātur.
Example
Canis puerum sequitur. — The dog follows the boy. Discipulī ē lūdō ēgrediuntur. — The pupils go out from the school.
Not this
Not a first look at deponent as a kind, taught yesterday. Not an ordinary active sequit or ēgredit, which this course does not use.
Connects to
Ēgredī often takes ē or ā and the ablative for the place left: ē lūdō ēgreditur.

Two more deponents, still only in the third person and the infinitive, and one irregular verb of becoming.

Canis puerum sequitur. Magister ē lūdō ēgreditur. Tempestās fit.

Fiō is not a deponent. It is an irregular verb meaning become, be made, or happen. Today only fit, fiunt, and fierī are required. Discipulī laetī fiunt. — The pupils become glad.

Remember

Sequitur and ēgreditur are deponents, third person and infinitive only, like loquitur. Fiō, fierī is a different verb, irregular, meaning become, be made, or happen; today: fit, fiunt, fierī. First- and second-person forms remain reserved.

Key terms: fiō, fierī, sequitur and ēgreditur.

Mathematics · Geometry XLV: mixed volumes, prism and cylinder

45 min · Method: V = B × h for right prisms and right cylinders together, with last week’s areas used as B

Learn

This lesson puts the week’s volume formulas together. Every right prism or right cylinder still uses

V = B × h,

with B taken from an area already taught: a rectangle, a triangle, a circle, or a ring.

Keep areas and volumes distinct. Keep π in exact cylinder answers. If two volumes are to be compared numerically, a labeled school approximation such as π ≈ 3.14 may be used, and must be named as an approximation.

Remember

V = B × h for a right prism or a right cylinder. B may be ℓw, ½bh, πr², or π(R² − r²). Exact cylinder answers stay in terms of π. No new solid is introduced.

Watch me

Watch a classroom box and a classroom cylinder of the same height, then a tube.

  1. Box 4 × 3 × 5: V = 60 cubic units.
  2. Cylinder r = 3, h = 5: V = 45π cubic units. Leave it in terms of π unless an estimate is asked.
  3. A tube with R = 5, r = 3, h = 5: V = π(25 − 9) × 5 = 80π cubic units.

Together

A classroom cylinder of radius 4 and height 6 is compared with a classroom box 8 × 6 × 4. Give both volumes, the cylinder in terms of π.

Hint 1: The cylinder

V = π × 16 × 6 = 96π cubic units.

Hint 2: The box

V = 8 × 6 × 4 = 192 cubic units. Do not treat 96π as 96.

Check

Use this week’s volume formulas only. Classroom lengths are invented.

  1. A right triangular prism has a base of area 18 and height 7. What is V?
  2. A classroom cylinder has diameter 8 and height 3. What is V, in terms of π?
  3. Why must 45π and 60 not be compared as if both were plain whole numbers?

Exercises

  1. A classroom tube has R = 6, r = 4, and h = 3. What is the volume of the material, in terms of π?
  2. A classroom box 10 × 2 × 3 is stacked on another box of the same size, making one taller prism. What is the combined volume?
  3. A classroom cylinder has volume 100π cubic units and height 4. What is its radius?
  4. Using π ≈ 3.14, estimate the volume of a classroom cylinder of radius 2 and height 5, and label the estimate.

Science · The specific heat of ice, and a comparison with melting

40 min · Method: ice Ih cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 0 °C, from the same Harvey/NIST table, compared with ΔfusH = 333.4 J/g

Learn

Key terms

specific heat of ice
Definition
The specific heat of ice in this lesson is the Harvey/NIST figure for ice Ih at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa: cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. It is the energy, per gram per kelvin, to change the temperature of the ice at that point in the table, not the energy to melt it.
Example
Using that 0 °C figure, warming 1 g of ice by 1 °C (or 1 K) at that temperature takes 2.10 J. Melting that same gram at 0 °C takes 333.4 J, about 159 times as much (333.4 / 2.10 ≈ 158.8).
Not this
Not a first look at ΔfusH, taught Tuesday, and not last week’s densities as a first look. Not an official liquid-water cp, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2.10 holds at every temperature; the same table gives other cp values at other temperatures, which are not today’s job.
Connects to
A kelvin and a Celsius degree have the same size, so 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ may be used for a 1 °C change at this point. The comparison 333.4 / 2.10 uses both figures at 0 °C from the same table.

Tuesday’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g is the energy to melt a gram of ice at 0 °C, with no temperature change. The same Harvey/NIST table gives a specific heat for ice Ih at 0 °C:

cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.

That figure is the energy, per gram per kelvin, associated with changing the temperature of the ice at that row of the table. Warming 1 g by 1 K (which is also 1 °C in size) at that point takes 2.10 J. Melting that gram at 0 °C takes 333.4 J.

333.4 / 2.10 ≈ 158.8, about 159 times. Melting 1 g at 0 °C takes about 159 times the energy of warming that gram of ice by 1 °C, using the two 0 °C figures from the same table.

For an invented 100 g, warming by 1 °C at that figure would take 210 J; melting at 0 °C still takes 33,340 J. The mass is invented. The two table figures are Harvey’s. Do not extend 2.10 over a large temperature range without noting that the table’s cp itself changes with temperature (for example, the table lists 2.02 at −10 °C, which is not used as today’s working figure). Do not invent a liquid-water cp in order to compare ice with liquid water.

Numerical figures are from A. H. Harvey, “Properties of Ice and Supercooled Water,” a NIST update to a CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics table, at standard atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water (PDF: https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=926353). ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g. The specific heat of ice Ih at 0 °C is cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Last week’s NIST/Harvey densities at 0 °C (ice Ih 0.9167 g/cm³; liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³) remain available as contrasts, not as a new first look. A liquid-water official cp is not taken from a table that was not opened as a definite fetched row for this week, and is not invented.

Remember

At 0 °C, Harvey/NIST gives ice Ih cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Melting 1 g at 0 °C takes 333.4 J, about 159 times the 2.10 J needed to warm that gram of ice by 1 °C, using those two 0 °C figures. Liquid-water cp is not invented.

Key terms: specific heat of ice.

Literature · The neutrals: “look, and pass” (Inferno III.22–69)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Inferno III.22–69 in Longfellow; later identifications are not imported. Charon remains reserved.

Learn

Key terms

the neutrals (Inferno III)
Definition
The neutrals, in Longfellow’s Inferno III.22–69, are “the melancholy souls of those / Who lived withouten infamy or praise,” commingled with angels “who have not rebellious been, / Nor faithful were to God, but were for self.” The guide’s instruction is, “Let us not speak of them, but look, and pass.”
Example
They have “no longer any hope of death”; “No fame of them the world permits to be”; “Misericord and Justice both disdain them.” A banner whirls; a long train follows; they are stung by gadflies and hornets.
Not this
Not a first look at the gate of lines 1–21. Not a later historical name for “him / Who made through cowardice the great refusal.” This course does not identify that shade. Not Charon, who is reserved.
Connects to
Longfellow, Inferno III.22–69, as in the source note.
the great refusal (as worded)
Definition
In Longfellow III.58–60 the speaker “beheld the shade of him / Who made through cowardice the great refusal.” The set wording goes no farther. This lesson does not add a later historical identification, just as earlier weeks did not identify the Greyhound from a later commentary.
Example
The speaker says he “comprehended, and was certain, / That this the sect was of the caitiff wretches / Hateful to God and to his enemies.” That certainty is the speaker’s, still without a name in the set lines.
Not this
Not a first look at the neutrals as a group, taught in the same lesson from the earlier lines. Not a biography imported from later notes.
Connects to
Longfellow III.58–63, as in the source note.

Dante, Inferno III, translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867, as posted at Wikisource: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_3 (page text from the linked scan pages). Spelling and punctuation are quoted as in that text, including “sombre color,” “city dolent,” and “withouten.” This course does not add later identifications beyond what the set wording itself names. Charon, the river, and the rest of the canto after line 69 remain reserved.

After the entrance of lines 20–21, the air is without a star: “sighs, complaints, and ululations loud,” “Languages diverse, horrible dialects,” “a tumult that goes whirling on / Forever in that air forever black, / Even as the sand doth, when the whirlwind breathes.”

The guide names “the melancholy souls of those / Who lived withouten infamy or praise,” mixed with angels neither rebellious nor faithful, “but were for self.” The heavens expelled them; the abyss does not receive them. “These have no longer any hope of death.” “Let us not speak of them, but look, and pass.”

A banner whirls, “indignant” of pause; so long a train follows “That ever Death so many had undone” seems hard to believe. The speaker “beheld the shade of him / Who made through cowardice the great refusal.” This course does not identify that shade. The miscreants “who never were alive” are naked, stung by gadflies and hornets; blood and tears are gathered by worms at their feet.

Line 70 begins another sight, a river’s bank. That stretch, Charon, and the rest of the canto remain reserved.

Remember

The neutrals lived “withouten infamy or praise.” The guide says, “look, and pass.” The set wording names “him / Who made through cowardice the great refusal,” and this course does not identify that shade. Lines 70 onward, including Charon, are reserved.

Atelier · A written pause on the monochrome modeling

60 min · Method: a short written pause on what the monochrome modeling has earned, without a new campaign and without color

Learn

Key terms

written pause on monochrome modeling
Definition
A written pause on monochrome modeling is a short, honest account of what the grisaille or the named drawing rehearsal now has: families that still read, a little modeling in the shadow and in the light, and at most a careful halftone or edge, with full color still reserved.
Example
The note might say that the light still has breadth, or that one halftone had to be lifted in Tuesday’s check. It should not claim a finish that is not there.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s written pause on the lay-in, and not a first look at modeling in the light. Not a new painting session, and not a color plan treated as if color had begun.
Connects to
Last Friday’s pause asked what the lay-in had earned. Today’s pause asks what the modeling, still monochrome, has earned, and what still waits.

This week added modeling in the light, a check, and one careful halftone or edge, all still monochrome. Today is a written pause, not a new pass and not the start of color.

Look at the study at a distance. Write what it now has, what you had to correct, and what still waits (features as a finish, and full color in Term 3). If you are in a drawing rehearsal, say so honestly, as last week allowed.

Do not start a fresh campaign in order to have more to write. The pause is about the work as it stands.

Remember

Write a short, honest pause on the monochrome modeling: families, light and shadow turns, one careful mark, corrections, and what still waits. Do not begin color, and do not pretend a finish.

Modern language · Spanish: decir and traer, a two-stem contrast

40 min · Method: a contrast of dij- and traj-, with last week’s four stems only as a named list, not as first looks

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • dije — Day 86
  • traje — Day 88

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Both new stems are now taught. Today is a contrast, not a third new verb.

traer (traj-): traje, trajiste, trajo, trajimos, trajisteis, trajeron — brought.

Both they-forms lack an extra i: dijeron, trajeron. Last week’s pude, puse, supe, and quise may be listed as already-taught stems, not as first looks.

Dij- is decir (said / told); traj- is traer (brought). Dijeron and trajeron have no extra i. Last week’s four stems are contrasts only.

Elective · Economics: equal installments on a known total (invented figures)

40 min · Method: an invented repayment split into equal installments, using a simple-interest charge that is already computed. Simple and compound interest are not first looks

Learn

Key terms

equal installment repayment
Definition
An equal installment repayment, in this lesson, takes a known total — a principal plus an already-computed simple-interest charge — and divides it into equal payments. The interest formula itself is not a first look; the new job is the equal split of a labeled invented total.
Example
An invented $500 principal plus an invented $40 simple-interest charge makes a total of $540, repaid in 6 equal monthly installments of $90. All of those figures are invented and labeled invented.
Not this
Not a first look at simple interest, compound interest, or the rule of 72, already taught (compound interest and the rule of 72 on Day 9; simple interest used in later weeks). Not a bank’s official APR, and not a declining-balance method, which is reserved. Not last week’s comparison of simple and compound as a first look.
Connects to
The $40 charge may be checked with the already-taught simple-interest formula on invented numbers, but today’s new work is the equal installment, not a new interest law.

Simple and compound interest are already taught. Last week compared them. Today is a new school job on invented figures: once a total is known, it may be repaid in equal installments.

Invented example, labeled invented: a principal of $500, with a simple-interest charge of $40 already computed, makes a total of $540. That total is repaid in 6 equal monthly installments:

$540 ÷ 6 = $90 each month.

The already-taught simple-interest formula can check the $40 if the invented time and rate are given (for example, 8% of $500 for one year would be $40), but that check is not a first look at the formula. What is new is the equal split.

This lesson does not teach a declining-balance charge, a bank’s official rate, or a compound installment plan. Those remain reserved. All classroom money figures here are invented.

Remember

Take a known invented total — principal plus an already-computed simple-interest charge — and divide it into equal installments. Simple and compound interest are not first looks. No official bank rate is invented. A declining-balance method is reserved.

Key terms: equal installment repayment.

Watch me

Watch the $540 / 6 example, then a check that the $40 could come from already-taught simple interest.

  1. Invented: $500 + $40 = $540, and $540 ÷ 6 = $90 each month.
  2. An invented check: 8% of $500 for one year is 0.08 × 500 = $40, the already-taught simple-interest formula, not a new law.
  3. Six payments of $90 recover the $540 total. They are not six payments of $500, and they are not a compound schedule.
