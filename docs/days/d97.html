Latin · The two times of the ablative absolute, contrasted

40 min · Method: present-participle versus perfect-participle absolutes, already begun yesterday and last week

Learn

Key terms

two times of the ablative absolute
Definition
A present-participle absolute, already taught last week, is going on at the same time as the main verb. A perfect-participle absolute, yesterday’s first look, is already complete when the main verb happens. Both are noun plus participle, both ablative, loosely attached.
Example
Puerīs loquentibus, magister tacet: with the boys speaking, the schoolmaster is silent (same time). Urbe captā, agricolae fugiunt: the capturing is already done.
Not this
Not a claim that every ablative is an absolute. An ordinary prepositional ablative, such as ē lūdō, is not an absolute. Not a first look at urbe captā itself, which was yesterday.
Connects to
Yesterday’s perfect-participle absolute, and last week’s present-participle absolute, including deponent participles.

Two classroom pairs, already met, now set side by side.

Magistrō recitante, puerī silent. The reciting is going on while they are silent.

Hīs dictīs, magister tacet. The saying is already finished when he is silent.

The construction is the same kind of loosely attached phrase. Only the time changes with the participle.

Word How to say it Meaning
magistrō recitante, puerī silent with the schoolmaster reciting, the boys are silent la
hīs dictīs, magister tacet with these things having been said, the schoolmaster is silent la
ē lūdō puerī ambulant the boys walk out of the school la

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

Present-participle absolute: same time. Perfect-participle absolute: already done. A prepositional ablative such as ē lūdō is not an absolute.

Mathematics · Geometry LII: the surface area of a right cone

45 min · Method: the lateral surface of a right cone unrolls to a sector of radius l and arc 2πr, so the lateral area is πrl; the total surface is πrl + πr²

Learn

Key terms

surface area of a right cone
Definition
The lateral surface of a right cone unrolls to a sector of a circle whose radius is the slant height l and whose arc is the base circumference 2πr, already taught. The area of that sector is (1/2) × l × 2πr = πrl. Adding the base disk, the total surface area is πrl + πr². The slant height is l = √(r² + h²), by the Pythagorean theorem, if r and h are given.
Example
A classroom right cone has r = 3 and h = 4, invented lengths. Then l = 5, the lateral area is 15π, and the total surface is 15π + 9π = 24π square units, left in terms of π.
Not this
Not last week’s volume V = (1/3)πr²h, and not yesterday’s pyramid. Not a first look at a sector or at arc length, which were taught on Day 83. Classroom lengths are invented. Exact answers stay in terms of π.
Connects to
Day 83’s sector and arc length, last week’s cone volume, and the Pythagorean theorem.
slant height of a right cone
Definition
The slant height l of a right cone is the distance from the apex to a point on the base circle, along the side. It is not the perpendicular height h. l² = r² + h².
Example
If r = 3 and h = 4, invented lengths, l = 5.
Not this
Not the perpendicular height used in last week’s volume. Not yesterday’s face slant height, which was the altitude of a triangular face on a pyramid.
Connects to
The Pythagorean theorem, already taught, and today’s lateral area πrl.

Yesterday’s pyramid faces were triangles. A cone’s side is curved. If you slit it along a straight line from the apex and unroll it, you get a sector of a circle of radius l, the slant height.

The arc of that sector is the cone’s base circumference, 2πr, already taught. Day 83 also taught that a sector of radius l whose arc is 2πr has area (1/2) × l × 2πr, which is πrl. That is the lateral area. The base is a disk of area πr², already taught. The total surface is πrl + πr².

A classroom cone of radius 3 and height 4, invented lengths, has l = √(3² + 4²) = 5. Lateral area 15π, total surface 24π square units, left in terms of π.

Remember

The lateral area of a right cone is πrl, from a sector of radius l and arc 2πr. The total surface is πrl + πr². l = √(r² + h²). Exact answers stay in terms of π. Classroom lengths are invented.

Key terms: surface area of a right cone, slant height of a right cone.

Science · The latent heat of vaporization of water, from OpenStax

40 min · Method: OpenStax College Physics 2e, Table 14.2 — Lv of water is 2256 kJ/kg at 100.0 °C, and Q = mLv. This is the official boiling figure last week would not invent from Harvey’s 0 °C rows

Learn

Key terms

latent heat of vaporization of water (OpenStax)
Definition
OpenStax, College Physics 2e, Table 14.2, lists the latent heat of vaporization of water as 2256 kJ/kg (540 kcal/kg) at the normal boiling point, 100.0 °C. The energy to vaporize a mass m, without a temperature change during the change of phase, is Q = mLv. In this table’s units, 2256 kJ/kg is 2256 J/g. This is not Harvey/NIST’s 0 °C table, and it is not last week’s difference 2834 − 333.4, which this course refused to treat as an official 100 °C figure.
Example
OpenStax’s own example: it would take 2256 kJ to change 1 kg of liquid water at the normal boiling point into steam. An invented classroom mass of 10 g, or 0.010 kg, would need 22.56 kJ, and is labeled invented.
Not this
Not Harvey’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g, or ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g, already taught from a different table. OpenStax also lists Lf = 334 kJ/kg for water; that is close to Harvey’s 333.4 J/g, but the two figures are from different tables and are not blended. Evaporation below the boiling point, which OpenStax also discusses, is reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s enthalpy of sublimation and the earlier enthalpy of fusion, as contrasts with named sources, and OpenStax 14.3, https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat (CC BY).

Last week used Harvey/NIST for ice at 0 °C: ΔfusH = 333.4 J/g and ΔsublH = 2834 J/g. Their difference was not treated as an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, because that row had not been fetched and was not going to be invented.

Today that official boiling figure is taken from a source that does list it: OpenStax, College Physics 2e, Table 14.2, “Heats of Fusion and Vaporization.” For water, the boiling point is 100.0 °C, and Lv is 2256 kJ/kg, which is 2256 J/g, or 540 kcal/kg in the same table. The energy for the change of phase is Q = mLv. OpenStax says it would take 2256 kJ to change 1 kg of liquid water at the normal boiling point into steam.

An invented 10 g of water, already at 100 °C, would need 0.010 × 2256 = 22.56 kJ to vaporize. The mass is invented; the 2256 kJ/kg is OpenStax’s table value.

OpenStax’s Lf for water is 334 kJ/kg. Harvey’s ΔfusH is 333.4 J/g. They are close, but they come from different tables, and this course does not blend them into a single official number. Last week’s 2834 J/g remains a 0 °C sublimation figure, not a 100 °C boiling figure.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 14.3, “Phase Change and Latent Heat,” Table 14.2, https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat (CC BY). Harvey/NIST remains the source for the 0 °C ice figures already taught: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water

OpenStax Table 14.2: Lv of water is 2256 kJ/kg at 100.0 °C, and Q = mLv. This is not Harvey’s 0 °C table, and not 2834 − 333.4. OpenStax’s Lf 334 kJ/kg is not blended with Harvey’s 333.4 J/g. Invented masses are labeled invented.

Literature · The first circle: sighs, and “without hope we live on in desire” (Inferno IV.1–42)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Inferno IV.1–42 in Longfellow. Franklin’s hints on 25–42 began yesterday in writing; this is the literature lesson

Learn

Key terms

the first circle (Inferno IV)
Definition
In Longfellow’s Inferno IV.1–42, a heavy thunder wakes the speaker on the verge of “the abysmal valley dolorous.” The guide, “pallid utterly,” leads him into “the foremost circle that surrounds the abyss.” There are “lamentations none, but only sighs,” from “sorrow without torment,” among crowds “of infants and of women and of men.” They sinned not; baptism, “the portal of the Faith thou holdest,” was wanting, or, if they were before Christianity, “in the right manner they adored not God.” The guide says, “And among such as these am I myself.” Their punishment is “only so far,” “That without hope we live on in desire.”
Example
The descent begins, “Let us descend now into the blind world,” and “I will be first, and thou shalt second be.” The speaker had taken the guide’s pallor for terror; the guide says it is pity: “The anguish of the people / Who are below here in my face depicts / That pity which for terror thou hast taken.”
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s close of Inferno III, except as the waking that begins this canto. Not Thursday’s naming of Limbo, or of the Mighty One, or of the poets, which remain reserved until then. A later commentary label is not to be imported into these lines as if it were in them.

“without hope we live on in desire”
Definition
In Longfellow IV.40–42, the guide says that for these defects, “and not for other guilt, / Lost are we, and are only so far punished, / That without hope we live on in desire.” That is the set wording of the punishment in this stretch.
Example
The defects just named are the want of baptism, or, if they lived before Christianity, that they adored not God in the right manner. The guide includes himself.
Not this
Not a later theological label imported as if it were Longfellow’s wording here. Not Thursday’s “Limbo,” which is named at line 45, after this stretch.
Connects to
Today’s first circle, and the writing reconstruction tomorrow.

The third canto ended in a fall, “as a man whom sleep hath seized.” A heavy thunder now breaks “the deep lethargy” within the speaker’s head, and he finds himself “upon the verge” of “the abysmal valley dolorous, / That gathers thunder of infinite ululations.” It is “obscure, profound,” and “nebulous,” so that he discerns nothing in its depths.

The guide, “pallid utterly,” says, “Let us descend now into the blind world,” and “I will be first, and thou shalt second be.” The speaker takes the pallor for fear; the guide says it is pity for those below, and “the long way impels us.”

They enter “the foremost circle that surrounds the abyss.” There, so far as the speaker can hear, are “lamentations none, but only sighs, / That tremulous made the everlasting air,” arising “from sorrow without torment,” among crowds “of infants and of women and of men.”

The guide explains, before they go farther, that these “sinned not”; merit without baptism is not enough, “baptism / Which is the portal of the Faith thou holdest”; and if they were before Christianity, “in the right manner they adored not God.” “And among such as these am I myself.” For those defects, “and not for other guilt,” “Lost are we, and are only so far punished, / That without hope we live on in desire.”

Keep Longfellow’s typography. Do not import Thursday’s names, or a later commentary label, into these lines.

Atelier · The mouth as a slot in the form, still monochrome

40 min · Method: the mouth is a slot in the already-blocked form, not a drawn line. Still monochrome

Learn

Key terms

the mouth as a slot in the form
Definition
On the monochrome head, the mouth is a slot, or a soft change, in the already-blocked form of the lower face, not a dark line drawn on top of a flat shape. Its corners and its mid-line are placed from the already-taught features, then the value is judged in the families: the upper lip often turns under, a little, into shadow; the lower lip may catch light on its upper plane. Nothing is outlined as a wire, and a smile is not invented if it is not seen.
Example
A careful pass places the opening, then checks the corners against the already-placed nose and chin, already taught in earlier feature work, and against yesterday’s underplane of the jaw.
Not this
Not a first look at placing the features, which was taught earlier in the term. Not yesterday’s underplane, except as a neighbor already judged. Full color remains reserved.
Connects to
Yesterday’s underplane of the jaw, the already-placed features, and last week’s family check.

The mouth is easy to draw as a line. That is the near-miss. On the monochrome head it is a slot in the form already blocked in, a change of plane and of value, not a wire.

Place the opening from the features already taught. Then judge the values in the families. The upper lip often turns under, a little, into the shadow family. The lower lip may have a narrow light plane on its upper surface, still inside the light family or on its edge, if the light is from above. The corners are not dots. They are turns of form, checked against the nose and against yesterday’s underplane of the jaw.

Elective · Music: inversion of a short invented figure

40 min · Method: a first look at inversion, as a short invented figure turned upside down. Last week’s sequence is a contrast, not a first look. Not a copyrighted score

Learn

Key terms

inversion of a short figure
Definition
An inversion, in this lesson’s school sense, takes a short figure and turns its intervals upside down: a step up becomes a step down of the same size, a skip down becomes a skip up, and so on, starting from a chosen note. It is not last week’s sequence, which repeats the same figure at a different pitch, still right-side up. Classroom figures are invented and are not copied from a copyrighted score.
Example
An invented classroom figure goes up a step, then up a skip. Its inversion, from the same starting note or from a named new one, goes down a step, then down a skip. The rhythm may stay the same so the inversion can be heard as the same shape turned over.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s sequence, or at the already-taught period and cadences. Not a tonal or real inversion of a copyrighted theme, which this course does not copy. Not a first look at invertible counterpoint, which is reserved.
Connects to
