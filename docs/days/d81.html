Latin · Capitulum XV: the present in the first and second persons

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV, Magister et discipulī, begins. Original sentences only; the book’s story is read in the book, not copied here.

Learn

Key terms

present first person
Definition
The first-person present endings are -ō (I) in the singular and -mus (we) in the plural. The verb’s ending is enough to name the person; a pronoun is not required.
Example
Recitō. — I recite. Recitāmus. — We recite.
Not this
Not a first look at the third-person endings -t and -nt, which this course has used from the start. Not a past tense.
Connects to
Until now most of this course’s Latin sentences have been in the third person. Capitulum XV closes the present table.
present second person
Definition
The second-person present endings are -s (you, singular) and -tis (you, plural). Again the ending names the person.
Example
Recitās. — You (singular) recite. Recitātis. — You (plural) recite.
Not this
Not a first look at tū or at mē and tē, which were already taught. The new work is the verb ending itself.
Connects to
The four conjugations keep these same person endings; the stem vowel may change (recitā-s, ridē-s, dīci-s, audī-s).

Capitulum XV is called Magister et discipulī, the schoolmaster and the pupils. The book’s story stays in the book. Here the new grammar is taught in original sentences.

Until this chapter, almost all of the verbs in this course have been in the third person: recitat, recitant. The present table now closes. The person endings, for all four conjugations, are:

-ō I · -s you (singular) · -t he, she, it · -mus we · -tis you (plural) · -nt they.

Word How to say it Meaning
recitō reh-KIT-oh I recite
recitās reh-KIT-ahss you (singular) recite
recitāmus reh-kit-AH-mooss we recite
recitātis reh-kit-AH-tiss you (plural) recite

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

A pronoun is not required. Recitāmus already means “we recite.” Ego or tū is written only for contrast: Ego recitō, tū tacēs. — I recite, you are silent.

Third-conjugation second person singular has a short i: dīcis, you say. Fourth conjugation keeps a long ī: audīs, you hear. Those stem vowels are not a first look at the conjugations themselves.

Remember

The present first-person endings are -ō and -mus; the second-person endings are -s and -tis. The ending names the person. A pronoun is added only for contrast, not because the verb is incomplete.

Key terms: present first person, present second person.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXVIII: the circumference of a circle

45 min · Method: the ratio of circumference to diameter is the constant π, already used in physics, now taught as a circle measure

Learn

Key terms

circumference of a circle
Definition
The circumference of a circle is the length of the circle itself, the distance once around. For a circle of diameter d, or radius r, that length is C = πd = 2πr, where π is the same constant already used in this course.
Origin
From Latin circumferentia, from circum, around, + ferō, to carry (Wiktionary).
Example
A circle of radius 5 has circumference C = 2π × 5 = 10π. Left in terms of π, that is exact in this school formula.
Not this
Not the area inside the circle, which is reserved for tomorrow. Not a chord, and not an arc taken as if it were the whole circumference.
Connects to
The word circumference has already appeared as the rim of a circle in earlier angle lessons. Today the length formula is the first look.
π, as C divided by d
Definition
π is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter: C / d = π, the same for every circle. In this course, leave exact answers in terms of π unless a problem asks for a labeled school approximation such as 3.14.
Example
If C = 10π and d = 10, then C / d = π, as the formula requires.
Not this
Not a claim that π is exactly 22/7 or exactly 3.14. Those are approximations (22/7 is a famous upper bound from Archimedes). Not a first look at π as a symbol in the already-taught centripetal formula.
Connects to
Physics has already used π in v = 2πr / T and in the pendulum formula. Today π is taught as this geometric ratio.

The circumference is the length once around a circle. For every circle, that length divided by the diameter is the same constant, written π:

C = πd = 2πr.

π is not a new symbol in this course. It already appeared in physics (for example in v = 2πr / T). What is new is the geometric job: π is C / d, and it is the same for every circle.

Leave exact answers in terms of π. If a problem asks for a decimal, you may use the school approximation π ≈ 3.14, and you must label it as an approximation. π is not exactly 3.14, and it is not exactly 22/7.

A classroom circle of radius 5 has C = 2π × 5 = 10π. That is exact in this formula. A decimal estimate would be about 31.4, using π ≈ 3.14, and is not a tighter official measurement.

Remember

C = πd = 2πr. π is the ratio C / d, the same for every circle. Leave exact answers in terms of π unless a labeled approximation is asked for.

Key terms: circumference of a circle, π, as C divided by d.

Watch me

  2. C = 2πr = 2π × 5 = 10π, or C = πd = 10π. The two versions agree.
  3. If a decimal is wanted, 10 × 3.14 = 31.4, using the school approximation π ≈ 3.14, labeled as an approximation.

Physics · Hooke’s law, and the force constant

40 min · Method: F = −kx, from OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.1, with a classroom spring and one sourced example

Learn

Key terms

Hooke’s law
Definition
Hooke’s law says that, for a system that obeys it, the restoring force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and is opposite in direction: F = −kx. The minus sign marks that opposition.
Example
If a classroom spring is stretched 0.05 m to the right, the restoring force is to the left, with size k times 0.05.
Not this
Not every restoring force. Hooke’s law is the special case of a direct proportion. A bent ruler may follow it only for a small displacement, as OpenStax notes in a related picture. Not a first look at the word oscillation, already taught last week.
Connects to
OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.1, “Hooke’s Law: Stress and Strain Revisited” (CC BY), writes F = −kx, with F the restoring force, x the displacement from equilibrium, and k a constant.
force constant
Definition
The force constant k (also called the spring constant) measures how stiff the system is. A larger k means a larger restoring force for the same displacement. Its units are newtons per meter (N/m). From F = −kx, the size of k is |F| / |x|.
Example
If a classroom spring is stretched 0.05 m by a 10 N force (invented figures), k = 10 / 0.05 = 200 N/m.
Not this
Not a force, and not a period. k is a property of the spring or system, not of a particular stretch, as long as Hooke’s law still holds.
Connects to
OpenStax 16.1: the larger the force constant, the stiffer the system; the slope of a graph of |F| against |x| is k.

Last week’s oscillation can be produced by a mass on a spring, as well as by a pendulum. Today is the force that pulls a spring back.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.1 (CC BY), states Hooke’s law for the simplest oscillations:

F = −kx.

F is the restoring force, x is the displacement from equilibrium, and k is the force constant, in N/m. The minus sign means the force is opposite to the displacement: stretch to the right, and the spring pulls left.

When Hooke’s law holds, a graph of the size of F against the size of x is a straight line, and the slope is k. That is OpenStax’s description, drawn here as a classroom sketch.

OpenStax’s sourced example (Example 16.1) finds k for a car’s suspension: an 80.0 kg person settles the car 1.20 cm. The weight is 80.0 × 9.80 = 784 N, and k = 784 / 0.0120 = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m. Those figures are OpenStax’s, not invented. Other classroom stretches in this lesson are invented and labeled invented.

Remember

F = −kx. The force is opposite to the displacement. k = |F| / |x|, in N/m, and is larger for a stiffer spring. This is OpenStax 16.1, not a first look at oscillation itself.

Key terms: Hooke’s law, force constant.

History · The west entrances, the four piers, and the four arches (Dewing I.1.36–41)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.36–41; no invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

four piers (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.37–38, four man-made eminences (lophoi), “which they call piers (pessoi),” stand in the center of the church, two on the north and two on the south, “opposite and equal to each other, each pair having between them just four columns.” They are “composed of huge stones joined together,” and “one might suppose that they were sheer mountain-peaks.”
Example
The four piers are the supports from which, in I.1.39, four arches spring. This lesson does not invent a height or a plan measurement for them.
Not this
Not a first look at the east half-cylinder or the seeming-float of the crescent, already read in I.1.31–35. Not a claim that Dewing’s “just four columns” has been surveyed in modern units.
Connects to
Dewing I.1.37–38, LacusCurtius posting of the 1940 Loeb, as in the source note.

Dewing, Loeb Classical Library, 1940, as posted at LacusCurtius: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html The 1940 copyright was not renewed and expired in 1968; the translation is in the public domain in the United States. British spellings in Dewing are kept inside quotations. No official measurement of the church is invented.

Last week ended at I.1.35, with the columns “as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance.” Today the description moves to the opposite side and then into the center.

I.1.36: “And on the side opposite the east is reared a wall containing the entrances (eisodoi), and on either side of this there stand in a semicircle (hêmikyklon) not only the columns themselves but also the structure above them, all this being very similar to the columns and structure I have just described.”

I.1.37–38 then name the four piers in the center, two north and two south, of huge fitted stones, like “sheer mountain-peaks.” From these, I.1.39, “spring four arches (apsides) which rise over the four sides of a square, and their ends come together in pairs and are made fast to each other on top of these piers.”

I.1.40–41: two of those arches, on the east and the west, “rise over empty air”; the other two have structure and “rather small columns” under them. Upon the crowns of the arches rests “a circular structure (kykloterês oikodomia), cylindrical (strongylon) in shape; it is through this that the light of day always first smiles.”

Read only this stretch. The four triangles and the great dome of I.1.43–46 remain for Wednesday. No height, breadth, or official modern plan is invented.

Atelier · Grisaille: a thin lay-in of the families

60 min · Method: a first monochrome lay-in of the light and shadow families on the head, or a named drawing rehearsal if paint is not ready

Learn

Key terms

grisaille lay-in
Definition
A grisaille lay-in is a thin first pass in monochrome that states the large light family and the large shadow family on the head, without finishing edges or features. The terminator is kept as a turning, not as an outline drawn around the form.
Example
Thin paint, or a thin monochrome wash, is enough to place the families in the same single light used for last week’s value map. If paint is not ready, the same pass may be made in graphite or charcoal and named as a grisaille rehearsal in drawing.
Not this
Not a first look at grisaille, at the light and shadow families, at the terminator, or at the value map, all taught last week. Not a finished head, and not a first look at full color, which remains reserved for Term 3.
Connects to
Last week’s first look at grisaille named the method. Today is the first working lay-in, still monochrome, still in one light.

Last week you named grisaille and made a value map of the head in one light. Today you begin a lay-in: a thin monochrome pass that states the two families, not a finished painting.

Set the head, or the cast, in the same single light. Mix one hue, or a gray, thin enough that you can still see the drawing underneath. Paint, or wash, the shadow family as one large shape, and the light family as one large shape. Stop before you model every halftone, and stop before you pick out eyelashes or a bright highlight.

The terminator, checked last week, is still a turning of the form, not a dark line drawn around the head. If a line starts to fence the families off, soften it back into a turn.

If paint is not in the room, make the same lay-in in graphite or charcoal, still in large families, and name it honestly as a rehearsal of a grisaille lay-in, not as a finished monochrome painting.

Remember

A thin monochrome lay-in states the two families on the head, in one light. The terminator stays a turning. This is not a finish, and not full color. A drawing rehearsal is enough if paint is not ready, and must be named as a rehearsal.

Key terms: grisaille lay-in.

Key terms

pude
Definition

Elective · Logic: Camestres, a second-figure AEE syllogism

40 min · Method: Camestres as second-figure AEE, after last week’s Cesare, with the middle term as predicate of both premises

Learn

Key terms

Camestres
Definition
Camestres is a second-figure syllogism of the form AEE: a universal affirmative premise, a universal negative premise, and a universal negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: All P are M; No S are M; therefore No S are P.
Example
All birds have feathers. No cats have feathers. Therefore no cats are birds. The middle term, “have feathers,” is the predicate of both premises.
Not this
Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2), taught last week. Cesare begins with a universal negative about P; Camestres begins with a universal affirmative about P. Not a first-figure mood, and not Celarent.
Connects to
Last week: Cesare, EAE, second figure. Today: Camestres, AEE, still second figure. The vowels in the name mark the three propositions: A, E, E.

Last week’s Cesare was second-figure EAE: No P are M; All S are M; therefore No S are P. Today is the next second-figure mood, Camestres, whose vowels are A, E, E:

All P are M.

No S are M.

Therefore No S are P.

The middle term M is still the predicate of both premises, which is what makes the figure the second figure, already taught. What is new is the order of A and E in the premises.

A clean classroom example: All birds have feathers. No cats have feathers. Therefore no cats are birds.

A near-miss is to write Cesare by habit (No P are M first) and still call it Camestres. If the first premise is a universal negative about P, you have Cesare, not Camestres.

Another near-miss is to swap S and P in the conclusion. The conclusion of Camestres is No S are P, a universal negative about the minor term.

Remember

Camestres is AEE in the second figure: All P are M; No S are M; therefore No S are P. Cesare, already taught, is EAE in the same figure. The middle term is the predicate of both premises.

Key terms: Camestres.

Watch me

Watch the birds-and-cats example labeled as Camestres, then compared with Cesare.

  1. All birds (P) have feathers (M). That is A: All P are M.
  2. No cats (S) have feathers (M). That is E: No S are M.
  3. Therefore no cats are birds: No S are P. If the first premise had been “No birds are cats’ kind of thing” in E form about P, the mood would have been Cesare instead.

Together

Write a Camestres syllogism of your own, then say how it would have to change to become Cesare.

Hint 1: The vowels

First premise A (all P are M), second E (no S are M), conclusion E (no S are P).

Hint 2: The figure

M must be the predicate both times. If M is the subject of a premise, you have left the second figure.
