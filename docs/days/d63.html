Latin · Capitulum XI, part 3: the accusative and infinitive

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. XI · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

accusative and infinitive (AcI)
Definition
A construction after verbs such as vidēre, dīcere, and putāre: an accusative names the person or thing who does the action of an infinitive. English often turns it with “that”: Puella avem volāre videt, “The girl sees that the bird is flying,” or “The girl sees the bird fly.”
Example
Puella avem volāre videt. Magister puellam cantāre dīcit. Putō puerum dormīre.
Not this
Not posse + infinitive (already taught), where possum / potest itself is the finite verb and there is no second person in the accusative. Not two finite verbs in a row (*Puella videt avem volat).
Connects to
Capitulum X already taught the present infinitive (vidēre, cantāre, esse) and posse + infinitive. Today the infinitive is used with an accusative subject after seeing, saying, or thinking.
Word How to say it Meaning
volāre wo-LAH-reh to fly
cantāre kan-TAH-reh to sing
dormīre dor-MEE-reh to sleep
dīcere DEE-ke-reh to say
putāre pu-TAH-reh to think

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

You already have the present infinitive, and you can use it after possum / potest (“is able to see”). Today the infinitive does a different job.

After verbs of seeing, saying, or thinking — vidēre, dīcere, putāre — Latin can name a second action with an accusative and an infinitive (often abbreviated AcI). The accusative is the subject of that infinitive, not the object of a second finite verb.

Original sentences: Puella avem volāre videt. The girl sees the bird fly (she sees that the bird is flying). Magister puellam cantāre dīcit. The teacher says that the girl is singing. Putō puerum dormīre. I think that the boy is sleeping.

Do not write two finite verbs for this idea (*Puella videt avem volat). Keep the second verb in the infinitive, and keep its subject in the accusative.

Remember

After vidēre, dīcere, or putāre, an accusative plus an infinitive names a second action (AcI). The accusative is the subject of the infinitive. This is not the already-taught posse + infinitive.

Key terms: accusative and infinitive (AcI).

Mathematics · Geometry XXVII: a missing side, from a scale factor

40 min · Method: write the similarity, then a proportion for corresponding sides

Learn

Key terms

scale factor (similarity)
Definition
The constant ratio of corresponding sides of two similar triangles. If △ABC ∼ △DEF and the scale factor from ABC to DEF is k, then each side of DEF is k times the corresponding side of ABC, so a missing side can be found from one known pair.
Example
If 4 corresponds to 10 and 6 corresponds to x, and the triangles are similar, then 4/10 = 6/x, so x = 15. The scale factor from the smaller to the larger is 10/4 = 5/2.
Not this
Not a ratio of sides that do not correspond, and not a claim that any two sides from the two triangles may be placed in a fraction. The vertices in the similarity statement tell you which sides match.
Connects to
Monday’s definition (corresponding sides proportional) is the reason a scale factor works. AA, taught yesterday, is often how we know the triangles are similar before we write the proportion.

Once two triangles are known to be similar, one pair of corresponding sides gives the scale factor, and that same factor finds a missing side.

In the figure, the sides 4 and 10 correspond, and the sides 6 and x correspond. The proportion is 4/10 = 6/x, or 4 : 10 = 6 : x. Cross-multiplying, 4x = 60, so x = 15. The scale factor from the smaller triangle to the larger is 10/4 = 5/2; check: 6 × 5/2 = 15.

Write the similarity first, or at least name the corresponding sides, before you write a fraction. A ratio of sides that do not correspond will give the wrong missing length.

Remember

If two triangles are similar, corresponding sides are proportional. One known pair gives the scale factor, which finds a missing corresponding side. Match the vertices before writing the fraction.

Writing & Rhetoric · A warrant is not a restated claim

40 min · Method: the already-taught warrant, set beside a restated claim

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Toulmin model — Day 61
  • grounds (Toulmin) — Day 61

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

A warrant is the rule that makes the grounds count. A restated claim only says the claim again, sometimes with “therefore” or “so” in front of it. The two are easy to confuse, and they are not the same job.

Take yesterday’s set sentence. Claim: the war tests whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure. Grounds: “we are engaged in a great civil war.”

A warrant: a civil war of this kind tests whether such a nation can endure. That is a general rule you can agree or disagree with, and it joins the grounds to the claim.

A restated claim: “therefore the war is a test of whether the nation can endure.” That sentence does not add a rule. It repeats the claim. If you are asked for a warrant and you write only “therefore” plus the claim, you have not yet given a warrant.

The same distinction holds for an invented case. Claim: the tea is cool. Grounds: no steam rises from the cup. Warrant: a hot cup of tea in this room gives off steam. A restated claim: “so the tea is cool.”

Remember

A warrant is a general rule that joins grounds to a claim. A restated claim says the claim again (often after “therefore” or “so”) and does not do that job. You can test a warrant by asking whether it is a rule someone could dispute.

Watch me

For the claim “the tea is cool,” with grounds “no steam rises from the cup,” write one genuine warrant and one restated claim, and say which is which.

  1. A warrant: a hot cup of tea in this room gives off steam (so the absence of steam supports the claim that the tea is cool).
  2. A restated claim: “so the tea is cool,” or “therefore the tea is cool.”
  3. The first is a rule you could argue with. The second only repeats the claim.

Together

Hint 1: Does it add a rule?

Hint 2: A repair

Check

  1. Claim: the path is icy. Grounds: it shines and is slippery. Which is a warrant: (A) “icy paths shine and are slippery,” or (B) “therefore the path is icy”?
  2. Why can a warrant be disagreed with, while a restated claim usually cannot in the same way?
  3. Using only the set Lincoln sentence, write a warrant that is not a restated claim.
  4. A student writes the whole second sentence again as the warrant. What is missing?

Physics · The lever, as a case of rotational equilibrium

50 min · Method: Στ = 0 about a named fulcrum, with labeled invented numbers

Learn

Key terms

fulcrum
Definition
The support about which a lever turns. In this lesson the fulcrum is the point about which torques are taken. A force through the fulcrum gives no torque about it.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin fulcrum, “bedpost, foot of a couch,” from fulciō, “prop up, support” (English Wiktionary, entry “fulcrum”).
Example
In the invented 3.00 m light rod, the fulcrum is 1.00 m from the left end, so a 20 N force at the left end has a moment arm of 1.00 m about the fulcrum.
Not this
Not the load and not the effort. The fulcrum is the turning point, not a second downward force. Moving the fulcrum changes the two distances, and so changes the forces needed for Στ = 0.
Connects to
Rotational equilibrium (Στ = 0) is still the condition. The lever is the special case in which a rigid bar turns about one named support, the fulcrum.
the lever rule
Definition
For a light lever in rotational equilibrium about its fulcrum, the effort times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum equals the load times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum (opposite senses). It is Στ = 0 written for two downward forces and one support.
Example
A 20 N load, 1.00 m to the left of the fulcrum, is balanced by an effort F, 2.00 m to the right: 20 N × 1.00 m = F × 2.00 m, so F = 10 N. The fulcrum then supplies 30 N upward if the lever is at rest.
Not this
Not a new unit, and not an excuse to ignore ΣF = 0 when the lever is at rest. Not a measurement from a named official machine; the numbers in this lesson are invented and labeled as such.
Connects to
Monday’s rotational equilibrium is the general condition. Today it is applied to a bar with a named fulcrum, often called a lever.

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a support called the fulcrum. The condition is the one taught on Monday: Στ = 0 about the fulcrum. If the lever stays at rest, ΣF = 0 as well, and the fulcrum can supply the upward force that balances the downward ones.

The figure is a labeled invented example. A light rod, 3.00 m long, has its fulcrum 1.00 m from the left end. 20 N acts downward at the left end, so its moment arm about the fulcrum is 1.00 m. An unknown downward force F at the right end has a moment arm of 2.00 m. For Στ = 0: 20 N × 1.00 m = F × 2.00 m, so F = 10 N. The downward forces add to 30 N, so the fulcrum supplies 30 N upward if the rod is at rest.

That two-force statement is sometimes called the lever rule: effort times effort arm equals load times load arm, when the two torques are in opposite senses and the bar is light. It is not a new law; it is Στ = 0 in this special case.

Remember

A lever turns about a fulcrum. In rotational equilibrium, load × load arm = effort × effort arm (opposite senses). If the lever is at rest, the fulcrum also supplies the upward force that makes ΣF = 0. Numbers in this lesson are invented classroom figures.

Key terms: fulcrum, the lever rule.

History · 476 CE: a conventional marker, and the East continues

40 min · Method: a conventional later date, clearly labeled, without an invented extract

Learn

Key terms

476 as a conventional year
Definition
The year later historians often use as a marker for the end of the western imperial line in Italy, when Romulus Augustulus was deposed by Odoacer. It is a conventional marker, not a day on which “the empire vanished,” and the eastern empire, with its capital at Constantinople, continued.
Example
A careful sentence: later writers often treat 476 CE, the deposition of Romulus Augustulus by Odoacer, as a conventional end of the western imperial line in Italy; the eastern empire continued.
Not this
Not a primary-source extract this course has set, and not a claim that 410 and 476 are the same event. Not a death toll, and not a statement that Roman life, law, or the city of Rome itself disappeared that year.
Connects to
Monday’s 410 sack is an earlier event, sourced from Augustine. 476 is taught here as a conventional later marker, with the East still in being.

410 was a sack of the city of Rome, sourced from Augustine. A later date, 476 CE, is often used by later historians as a conventional marker for the end of the western imperial line in Italy: Romulus Augustulus was deposed by Odoacer. This course has not set a primary-source extract for that deposition, so it is taught as a conventional marker, not as a quotation, and not as a camera in the palace.

Two cautions follow from that honesty. First, 476 is not another name for 410, and it is not a day on which “the empire vanished.” The eastern empire, ruled from Constantinople, continued. Second, a conventional year is a later way of organizing events; it is not a reason to invent a death toll or a scene that has not been sourced.

A sentence worth keeping: later writers often treat 476 CE as a conventional end of the western imperial line in Italy; the East continued. Late antiquity, named on Monday, is the longer stretch in which both 410 and 476 sit.

Remember

476 CE is a conventional later marker for the deposition of Romulus Augustulus by Odoacer and the end of the western imperial line in Italy. The eastern empire continued. 476 is not 410, not a sourced quotation in this course, and not a day on which the empire vanished.

Art history · The Arch of Constantine, from Platner

30 min · Method: a named Platner entry, with a short set extract and mixed-period sculpture kept distinct

Learn

Key terms

Arch of Constantine
Definition
A three-bay triumphal arch in Rome, which Platner records as erected by the senate in honour of Constantine to commemorate his victory over Maxentius in 312 CE, with completion dated to 315–316 CE by the decennalia inscriptions of the side arches. Platner notes that it is not mentioned by any of the literary sources he uses, and that its sculptures belong to several different periods.
Example
Platner places the arch at the beginning of the road through the valley between the Palatine and the Caelian, from the Colosseum toward the south-east end of the Circus Maximus, and records its measurements and the mixed dates of its sculpture.
Not this
Not the already-taught Arch of Titus, and not a claim that every sculpture on the arch was carved for Constantine in 315–316. Platner is explicit that some sculpture was taken from earlier monuments, and that some work is Constantinian.
Connects to
Term 1 used Platner for Flavian buildings, including the Arch of Titus. Today the same dictionary is used for a later monument, in late antiquity, with the same rule: later fabrics, not photographs, and annotators’ debates are not treated as Platner’s own sentences.

The method is the one already used with Platner: a named entry, a short extract, and a careful distinction between what the entry states and what it does not. Today’s monument is later than the Flavian buildings of Term 1. It sits in the same city, in late antiquity.

Read what is there. The senate erected the arch in honour of Constantine, to commemorate his victory over Maxentius in 312 CE, as the attic inscription (CIL VI.1139) records. Completion is dated to 315–316 CE by the decennalia inscriptions of the side arches. Platner adds that no literary source he uses mentions the arch, and that the sculptures belong to several different periods.

Those periods, as the entry then sets them out, include work associated with Trajan’s Forum (conflicts with Dacians, and statues of Dacians), round medallions often referred to Hadrian, rectangular reliefs from a monument of Marcus Aurelius, and, belonging to the Constantinian period, a low frieze of episodes in Constantine’s own career, with other Constantinian work. This course follows Platner’s grouping; it does not pretend to have settled every scholarly argument the entry records, and it does not treat a later photograph as a source.

Remember

Platner records the Arch of Constantine as erected by the senate to commemorate the victory over Maxentius in 312 CE, completed in 315–316 CE, unmentioned in the literary sources he uses, and decorated with sculpture of several periods — some earlier, some Constantinian. This is not the Arch of Titus. Later fabrics, not photographs, remain the method.

Together

What does Platner say about literary sources, and about the dates of the sculpture? Why do those two remarks matter?

Hint 1: The two remarks

He says the arch is not mentioned by any of the literary sources he uses, and that the sculptures belong to several different periods.

Hint 2: Why they matter

The date and purpose come from the inscriptions, not from a surviving literary description in his list. The sculpture cannot all be read as if it were carved for Constantine in 315–316.
