Latin · Capitulum X, part 4: nouns and infinitives together

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. X · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

nouns with an infinitive
Definition
A mix, not a new ending: today’s sentences combine a third-declension noun (homō, leō, pēs, or last week’s rēx, vōx, canis) with posse + an active or passive infinitive.
Example
Homō leōnem vidēre potest. Rēx vocārī potest. Pēs canis vidērī potest.
Not this
Not a new case, and not a finite passive. Keep the accusative for the object of an active infinitive (leōnem vidēre), and do not put that object on a passive infinitive as if it were still the object of ‘to see.’
Connects to
If the infinitive is vidēre (to see), the thing seen is accusative. If it is vidērī (to be seen), there is no object of that kind; the subject is the one who can be seen.

Put the week together, still in original sentences.

  • Nouns: homō, leō, pēs (and rēx, vōx, canis from last week).
  • possum / potes / potest + infinitive, active or passive.

A useful test: if you can still ask “what does he see?” the infinitive is active and the answer is accusative. If the meaning is “he can be seen,” do not add an accusative object of vidērī.

Original sentences: Homō canem vidēre potest. Leō in campō vidērī potest. Rēx hominem vocāre potest. Potes pedem canis vidēre.

Remember

Combine a third-declension noun with posse + infinitive. Active infinitive may take an accusative object; a passive infinitive does not take that object.

Key terms: nouns with an infinitive.

Mathematics · Algebra XII: the inverse of a linear function

60 min · Method: undo the operations in reverse order, then check by composition; a first look at the graph as a reflection in y = x

Learn

Key terms

inverse of a linear function
Definition
The function that undoes a given linear function. If f(x) = mx + b with m ≠ 0, the inverse is f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m. The notation f⁻¹ is not a power; it names the inverse function. The word inverse here is from Latin inversus, from invertere, “to turn.”
Origin
From Latin inversus, from invertere, “to turn.” In this lesson the word names a function that undoes another, not the inverse of a conditional.
Example
If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f⁻¹(x) = (x − 1)/2. Check: f(3) = 7, and f⁻¹(7) = 3. Also f(f⁻¹(x)) = x and f⁻¹(f(x)) = x.
Not this
Not the logic inverse of a conditional (Day 21: the inverse of “If P, then Q” is “If not P, then not Q”). And not 1/f(x), the reciprocal. And not an inverse for a quadratic in this lesson (a quadratic is not one-to-one on the whole line).
Connects to
A linear function with m ≠ 0 has exactly one output for each input, and each output comes from exactly one input, so it can be undone. If m = 0, the rule is constant and cannot be inverted as a function of x.

Undo, in reverse order. If f(x) = 2x + 1, the rule is: multiply by 2, then add 1. To undo: subtract 1, then divide by 2. So f⁻¹(x) = (x − 1)/2.

In general, if f(x) = mx + b and m ≠ 0,

f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m.

The check. The two functions undo each other: f(f⁻¹(x)) = x and f⁻¹(f(x)) = x. Always run this check.

A first look at the graph. The point (0, 1) on y = 2x + 1 is reflected to (1, 0) on the inverse, across the dashed line y = x. That reflection is a picture of undoing: input and output change places. Today we use the picture as a check, not as a full theory of inverse graphs.

Two near-misses. f⁻¹ is not a power (not 1/f). And this “inverse” is not the logic inverse of a conditional, already taught under the same English word.

Remember

If f(x) = mx + b and m ≠ 0, then f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m. Check by composition. The graph is a first look at reflection in y = x. This is not the logic inverse, and not 1/f(x).

Key terms: inverse of a linear function.

Watch me

  1. f⁻¹(x) = (x − 1)/2.
  2. f⁻¹(7) = 3.
  3. f(f⁻¹(x)) = 2·((x − 1)/2) + 1 = (x − 1) + 1 = x.

Science · Two eyes, and a first look at judging distance

50 min · Method: two slightly different views, named honestly, without invented measurements of the space between the eyes

Learn

Key terms

two views (binocular, first look)
Definition
The two eyes, set a little apart, receive slightly different views of a near scene. That difference is a first help in judging distance. This lesson does not invent a measured distance between the eyes, or a calculated angle.
Example
A near pencil, seen first with one eye and then with the other, appears to jump against a far wall. The jump is a classroom sign that the two views are not the same.
Not this
Not a claim that a person with one eye cannot judge distance at all (other cues remain), and not a full theory of three-dimensional vision. Today is a first look at one cue.
Connects to
Last week’s blind spot is still there in each eye. Two eyes also help us not to notice a single blind spot in ordinary looking; that is a related fact, not a new measurement.

Two views. Your eyes are a little apart, so each has a slightly different picture of a near object. That difference is one cue the brain can use in judging distance. We will not invent a number for the space between the eyes, or a measured angle.

A simple check. Hold a finger in front of a far wall. Close one eye, then the other. The finger appears to shift. That shift is the two views, made visible. It is a qualitative check, not a measurement.

A limit. People can also judge distance with other cues (size, overlap, motion). This lesson does not say that two eyes are the only way, and it does not invent a percentage of “how much” of distance-judging they supply.

The blind spot of each eye (Day 34) is also easier to ignore when both eyes are open. That is named, not measured, today.

Remember

Two eyes give slightly different views of a near scene, a first cue for judging distance. No invented measurements. Two eyes are not the only cue, and they also make a single blind spot easier to ignore.

Key terms: two views (binocular, first look).

Literature · Antigone: the close of the play, taught carefully

50 min · Method: the end is now taught, without a gore-first summary; a short attributed public-domain close

Learn

Key terms

the close of Antigone
Definition
By the end of the play, the edict’s cost has come home: Antigone is dead; Haemon and Eurydice (Creon’s wife) are dead; Creon remains, seeing too late the wreck of his house. This lesson names that close without a catalogue of wounds. The point is the cost of the conflict, not a list of horrors.
Example
A careful sentence: “The play ends with Creon alive, and with the deaths of Antigone, Haemon, and Eurydice already done; the last words turn toward wisdom learned too late.”
Not this
Not a Roman history of 68 CE, and not a license to treat the ending as a joke or as a tally. If you quote, keep the extract short and attributed.
Connects to
Tuesday reserved this close. Today it may be used in writing, carefully. A plot dump is still not a précis.

The end, now taught. Antigone dies. Haemon, her betrothed and Creon’s son, dies. Eurydice, Creon’s wife, dies. Creon is left alive, seeing the cost of his command too late. Name those facts; do not linger on the manner of each death as if the lesson were a list of wounds.

A short attributed close, again from Jebb (1917), the chorus at the end of the play:

“Wisdom is the supreme part of happiness; and reverence towards the gods must be inviolate. Great words of prideful men are ever punished with great blows, and, in old age, teach the chastened to be wise.”

(Sophocles, Antigone, trans. R. C. Jebb, 1917, close of the play.)

Use this as a short extract. It is a comment on pride, wisdom, and learning too late — not a substitute for stating what has happened, and not a moral sticker to end every paragraph automatically.

Writing may now refer to this close, in a careful sentence, with attribution if you quote. A plot dump is still not a précis.

Remember

The play ends with the deaths of Antigone, Haemon, and Eurydice, and with Creon alive, seeing the cost too late. A short Jebb (1917) choral close may be used, attributed. Name the cost; do not make a list of wounds.

Atelier · A few accents, and a finishing pass

50 min · Method: a small number of accents, only where the plate has them, at the end of a last value pass

Learn

Key terms

accents (finishing pass)
Definition
A few of the darkest darks, or a small, careful light, placed late and only where the plate or plaster actually has them, after the value families already sit.
Example
One darker dark in a nostril or in a far hair mass, if the plate has it, will do more than a dozen invented darks. A small catch-light, if seen, is an accent, not a new lighting scheme.
Not this
Not a scatter of sharp marks all over the head, and not a start from eyelashes or single hairs. Accents are few, late, and copied from the plate.
Connects to
Yesterday’s lost edges should survive. An accent that outlines a whole ear usually undoes a lost edge. The plate wins.

Finish the sitting with one more quiet value pass, then add only a few accents — the marks the plate actually has. Stop sooner than you want to. A study can be finished for this week without being covered in sharp ticks.

If you are unsure whether a mark is on the plate, leave it out.

Remember

A last value pass, then a few accents only where the plate has them. Lost edges should survive. Stop sooner than you want to.

Modern language · Spanish: object pronouns with two verbs

40 min · Method: two correct places for a pronoun already taught, with ir a, tener que, and acabar de; English remains the language of instruction

Learn

Key terms

pronouns with two verbs
Definition
When a conjugated verb is followed by an infinitive (ir a, tener que, acabar de, and similar), a direct- or indirect-object pronoun may attach to the infinitive or stand before the conjugated verb. Both are correct in this lesson: Lo voy a ver. Voy a verlo.
Example
La voy a pedir. Voy a pedirla. Lo acabo de leer. Acabo de leerlo. Te tengo que llamar. Tengo que llamarte.
Not this
Not a drop of the pronoun, and not a new past tense. Do not write *Voy a lo ver (the pronoun is not placed between a and the infinitive in that way). And the two options are alternatives, not two different meanings today.
Connects to
Direct- and indirect-object pronouns were already taught (Days 14 and 23). Today’s job is their place with a two-verb pattern you already have.
Word How to say it Meaning
Lo voy a ver lo boy ah BEHR I am going to see him/it (pronoun first)
Voy a verlo boy ah BEHR-lo I am going to see him/it (pronoun on the infinitive)
La acabo de leer lah ah-KAH-bo deh leh-EHR I have just read it (fem.)
Acabo de leerla ah-KAH-bo deh leh-EHR-lah I have just read it (fem.)

Two correct places. With ir a / tener que / acabar de + infinitive, the object pronoun may go before the conjugated verb or on the end of the infinitive. Lo voy a ver and Voy a verlo are both right, and they mean the same today.

Do not put the pronoun between a (or de, or que) and the infinitive: not Voy a lo ver. Original sentences: La voy a pedir. Voy a pedirla. Lo acabo de hacer. ¿Lo tienes que leer? Tengo que leerlo.

Remember

With ir a / tener que / acabar de + infinitive, the pronoun may go before the conjugated verb or on the infinitive: Lo voy a ver / Voy a verlo. Not *Voy a lo ver. Same meaning today.

Economics & personal finance · Saving a leftover (an invented example)

40 min · Method: a leftover from a labeled budget, set aside as saving, with a clearly invented interest figure if interest is used

Learn

Key terms

saving a leftover
Definition
In this first look, saving is a leftover from a labeled budget that is not spent in the same period, but is set aside. The figures in this lesson are invented and labeled as invented, not taken from a real household.
Example
In the classroom example, income 800, needs 500, wants 200, leftover 100. If that 100 is saved, the period’s spending is 700, and 100 is set aside. If 40 of the leftover is then spent, the amount saved is 60.
Not this
Not a claim about a real family’s accounts, and not a new interest rate presented as a market fact. Compound interest was already taught (Day 9); if this lesson uses a rate, the rate is invented and labeled.
Connects to
Last week’s budget supplied the leftover. Today the leftover has a job: it can be saved, or it can be spent after all. The arithmetic is ordinary subtraction.

Saving, in this first look, is setting that leftover (or part of it) aside, rather than spending it in the same period. If the whole 100 is saved, spending is 700. If 40 of it is spent after all, saving is 60.

A link to Day 9, clearly labeled. Compound interest was taught with its own examples. If we ask what a saved 100 might become after a year at an invented rate of 5% simple interest for a first check, the added amount is 0.05 × 100 = 5, so 105 at the year’s end in that invented, simple picture. That 5% is invented and labeled, not a current market rate, and not a promise. A compound-interest check, if you use Day 9’s method on the same invented 5%, is a different, still invented, figure; this lesson will accept the simple check unless a problem asks you to compound.

The units remain classroom units, not a real currency claim about a real person.

Remember

Saving, here, is a leftover (or part of it) set aside. The 800 / 500 / 200 / 100 example is still invented. An interest rate used today is invented and labeled, not a market fact.

Watch me

Using the invented leftover of 100, give the saving if none of it is spent, and if 40 is spent after all. Then add a simple-interest check at an invented 5% for one year on the full 100.

  1. If none is spent, saving = 100, and spending = 700.
  2. If 40 is spent, saving = 60.
  3. Invented 5% simple interest on 100 for a year: 5 added, 105 at year’s end, rate labeled invented.
