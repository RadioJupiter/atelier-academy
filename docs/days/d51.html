Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: et, sed, and an accusative object

40 min · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences, Capitula I–X only. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

et and sed
Definition
Two linking words already used in Capitula I–X: et joins, sed contrasts. They appear today in short original sentences, not in a new chapter.
Example
Puella rosam habet, sed puer rosam nōn habet.
Not this
This is not the start of Capitulum XI, and not a claim that et and sed are the only links in Latin.
Connects to
Weeks 9–10 already used short original I–X sentences. Today the object is in the accusative (rosam).
the accusative as object
Definition
In these I–X sentences, the thing had or seen takes the accusative (rosam, not rosa) when it is the object of the verb.
Example
Puella rosam habet — the girl has a rose. rosam is the object.
Not this
Not every noun after a verb is accusative, and this is not a new case beyond I–X.
Connects to
Place phrases with in, ad, and ē were reviewed in week 10; today’s job is the object.
Word How to say it Meaning
et et and
sed sed but
puella rosam habet PWEH-lah ROH-sahm HAH-bet The girl has a rose
Puer rosam nōn habet POO-ehr ROH-sahm nohn HAH-bet The boy does not have a rose

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook and not a new chapter: Puella rosam habet. Puer rosam nōn habet. Puella rosam habet, sed puer rosam nōn habet. Puer et puella rosam habent.

et joins. sed contrasts. rosam is accusative: it is the rose that is had, not the subject. habent agrees with a plural subject (puer et puella). Italian-voice Hear is an approximation, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

et joins; sed contrasts. An object such as rosam is accusative in these I–X sentences. No Capitulum XI. Instruction in English. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Mathematics · Algebra XVII: a quadratic inequality, from the graph

40 min · Method: the already-taught parabola, then the number line; factor when the roots are clear

Learn

Key terms

quadratic inequality
Definition
An inequality whose sides, once arranged, give a quadratic on one side, such as (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0. We read it from the already-taught graph: the parabola is below the x-axis, or above it, in intervals marked by the roots.
Origin
From Middle English inequalite, from Old French inequalité, from Medieval Latin inaequālitās, from Latin inaequālis, “unequal,” from in- “not” + aequālis “equal” (English Wiktionary, entry “inequality”). The word quadratic was already taught; today’s name is the two together.
Example
For y = (x − 2)(x − 6), which opens upward, y < 0 between the roots: 2 < x < 6.
Not this
Not a linear inequality, and not a request to invent a new kind of number. The discriminant still only tells how many roots; it does not, by itself, write the inequality’s interval.
Connects to
Algebra III taught inequalities; quadratic graphs and roots were taught in weeks 7–8; the discriminant is already known. Today those tools are used together.

A quadratic inequality asks where a quadratic is positive, negative, or zero. We already graph y = ax² + bx + c and we already find roots. Today we read the inequality from that graph.

The method, when the quadratic factors (or the roots are already known).

  1. Write one side as zero: for example (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0, which is y < 0 for y = (x − 2)(x − 6).
  2. The roots split the number line. Here the roots are 2 and 6.
  3. Look at the already-taught shape. If a > 0, the parabola opens upward: it is below the x-axis between the roots, and above the x-axis outside them. If a < 0, those regions swap.
  4. A strict inequality (< or >) does not include the roots (there y = 0). A ≤ or ≥ does include them.

A worked shape. y = (x − 2)(x − 6) = x² − 8x + 12 opens upward. y < 0 between the roots: 2 < x < 6. y > 0 to the left of 2 and to the right of 6: x < 2 or x > 6. The pictured shading is the < 0 region.

What we will not do today. We will not invent a root we have not found, and we will not solve a quadratic inequality by “moving pieces” as if it were linear (for example, we will not jump from (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0 to “x − 2 < 0 and x − 6 < 0” as a complete method: both factors negative is only one of the cases, and it is not the region between the roots).

Remember

Read a quadratic inequality from the graph. Roots mark the splits. For a parabola that opens upward, y < 0 between the roots; y > 0 outside them. Strict inequalities do not include the roots. Do not treat it as a linear “move the terms” problem.

Key terms: quadratic inequality.

  3. y > 0 outside: x < 2 or x > 6. At x = 2 and x = 6, y = 0, so those points are not in a strict inequality.

Writing & Rhetoric · Because, but, so: a later letter, in translation

40 min · Method: The Writing Revolution because / but / so, used on a sourced caution about Pliny’s letter

Learn

Key terms

because / but / so, on a later letter
Definition
A because / but / so sentence that keeps Pliny the Younger’s letter to Tacitus in its job: it is a later request from a historian, in a sourced translation, not a measured log of 79 CE and not a photograph.
Example
The letter can describe a cloud because Pliny says his mother pointed it out, but it was written at Tacitus’s request after the event, so I will not treat Firth’s wording as a stopwatch reading.
Not this
Not a claim that the letter is useless, and do not invent a death toll or a town the set extract does not name.
Connects to
Week 10 used because / but / so on a Josephus caution. Today the caution is about a later letter, in translation.

History will give the letter more fully. Today’s job is one honest sentence-type.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified) renders a letter from Pliny the Younger to Tacitus. Pliny is fulfilling Tacitus’s request for an account of his uncle. The uncle was at Misenum, in command of the fleet. Firth writes: “On the 24th of August, about the seventh hour, my mother drew his attention to the fact that a cloud of unusual size and shape had made its appearance.”

Because / but / so. The because names what the letter can honestly do. The but names a limit (it is a later letter, in translation; the year 79 CE is the conventional event-year, not a year we will pretend the set sentence states). The so states the habit.

A model: Firth’s Pliny can describe a cloud at about the seventh hour because the letter says the mother pointed it out, but the letter is an answer to Tacitus written after the event, and we are reading it in a 1900 translation, so I will not treat the wording as a measured instrument log, and I will not invent a town or a death toll the set extract does not name.

Remember

Because names what the letter can do. But names a limit (later letter; translation; conventional year). So states the habit. Do not invent names or numbers the set extract does not give.

Physics · g on Mars, from the same formula and sourced figures

40 min · Method: g = G M / R², already taught; NASA Planetary Fact Sheet figures; significant figures respected

Learn

Key terms

g on Mars, from the same formula
Definition
The already-taught formula g = G M / R², applied to NASA fact-sheet figures for Mars, giving about 3.7 m/s², which matches the fact sheet’s own listed gravity 3.7 m/s². G still has three significant figures (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹).
Example
With M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 3,396 km (half of the fact-sheet diameter 6,792 km), g comes out about 3.7 m/s².
Not this
Not a new formula, not an invented orbital speed, and not a claim of more significant figures than G and M support.
Connects to
Week 10 computed g on the Earth (about 9.79 m/s², working value 9.8) and on the Moon (about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth). Today is the same method on Mars.

The formula is the one already taught: g = G M / R², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² (three significant figures).

Mars, from the NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (metric). Mass 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg. Diameter 6,792 km, so a radius 3,396 km = 3.396 × 10⁶ m. (The sheet’s own listed gravity is 3.7 m/s²; we will compute, then sit that listed value beside the computation, not average them into a homemade official number.)

The computation.
M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg = 6.42 × 10²³ kg.
G M = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.42 × 10²³ = 4.28 × 10¹³ (three significant figures).
R² = (3.396 × 10⁶)² = 1.153 × 10¹³.
g = 4.28 × 10¹³ / 1.153 × 10¹³ ≈ 3.71 m/s², which we report as about 3.7 m/s², the same two-figure value the fact sheet lists. G and M do not support a tighter official figure here.

Beside Earth and the Moon. Earth’s working value in this course is 9.8 m/s². 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, which is about 19/50, a little less than 2/5 (that comparison was already used, honestly, in this course’s sources). The Moon’s g was about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth of 9.8. We will not invent an orbital speed for Mars.

Remember

g = G M / R², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ (3 s.f.). Mars: M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 3,396 km, g ≈ 3.7 m/s², matching the fact sheet’s listed 3.7. About 0.38 of Earth’s 9.8 (about 19/50), a little less than 2/5. No invented orbital speed.

Key terms: g on Mars, from the same formula.

Atelier · Two forms under one lamp: a sphere and a cube

40 min · Method: one lamp, two already-taught forms; still not a head

Learn

Key terms

two forms, under one lamp
Definition
A sphere and a cube, already studied separately, now set together under one lamp, so the light plane, the dark plane, and a short cast shadow can be compared on both. Still not a head.
Example
The lamp stays in one place. The sphere’s light turns gradually; the cube shows planes. Both throw a short shadow on the same ground.
Not this
Not a portrait, not a second lamp invented to “fix” a plane, and not a claim that two forms make a head.
Connects to
Week 10 drew a cube under one lamp, and looked back at the sphere. Today they sit together, still not a head.

Last Friday’s plan was to look back at the sphere and to keep the cube, still not a head. Today the two forms are set together, under one lamp.

What to look for. The lamp has a direction (upper left in the diagram). The sphere still turns from light to dark through half-tones. The cube still shows planes: a light plane, a side plane, a top or receding plane. Each form has a cast shadow on the same ground. The shadows should agree with one lamp: they lean the same way.

Still not a head. Two geometric forms, however carefully grouped, are not a portrait and not a Bargue head. That is a later job. Today’s job is the agreement of light on two forms.

One lamp, a sphere and a cube together. The sphere turns; the cube shows planes. Cast shadows agree with one lamp. Still not a head.

Modern language · Spanish: ya and todavía (present)

40 min · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Key terms

ya and todavía
Definition
Two present-tense time words: ya (already) and todavía (still, or “not yet” when used with no). Instruction stays in English. They do not, by themselves, create a past-tense lesson.
Example
Ya está aquí. Todavía no está aquí.
Not this
Not a past tense, and not the start of the preterite. todavía no is “not yet,” not a new verb tense.
Connects to
aquí, ahí, and allí were taught in week 9. Today ya and todavía sit with those already-taught place words if needed.
Word How to say it Meaning
ya yah already
todavía toh-THAH-bee-ah still / yet (in a not-yet or still-true sense, taught today with still / not yet)

Logic · Disjunctive syllogism, and an inclusive “or”

30 min · Method: a valid “or” form, beside last week’s conditional forms; inclusive “or” as the default unless a problem says exactly one

Learn

Key terms

disjunctive syllogism
Definition
A valid form: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. From an “or” and the denial of one side, you may infer the other side. In this course’s default “or,” which is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one, you may not, from P, infer not-Q.
Example
The figure is a square or a circle (in a labeled invented pair of options). It is not a square. Therefore it is a circle.
Not this
Do not infer not-Q from P, unless the problem has said the “or” is exclusive (exactly one). Inclusive “or” allows both. Not modus ponens or modus tollens, which use a conditional, already taught last week.
Connects to
Last week taught two valid conditional forms, modus ponens and modus tollens. Today is a valid form that uses “or,” not a conditional. Necessary and sufficient conditions were already taught on Day 16; they are not reteached as new.
inclusive or, in a first look
Definition
An “or” that is true when at least one side is true, including the case where both are true. This is the default in this course unless a problem says exactly one (an exclusive “or”).
Example
“The figure has four right angles or it has four equal sides” can be true of a square, which has both. From “it has four right angles” you may not infer that it lacks four equal sides.
Not this
Not an exclusive “or” (exactly one), and not a claim that English everyday “or” is always exclusive. The school default here is inclusive unless a problem says otherwise.
Connects to
Disjunctive syllogism uses this default: not-P gives Q, but P does not, by itself, give not-Q.

Last week taught two valid forms that use a conditional: modus ponens and modus tollens. Today is a different valid form, which uses or. Necessary and sufficient conditions were already taught on Day 16; they are not a first look today.

Disjunctive syllogism. P or Q. Not P. Therefore Q. (Or: P or Q; not Q; therefore P.) From the “or” and the denial of one side, you may take the other side.

The default “or.” In this course, “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one. Inclusive “or” is true when at least one side is true, including both. So from P you may not infer not-Q. That second move would need an exclusive “or,” and only when the problem has said so.

A worked invented pair. A labeled invented box holds a square or a circle (or, under the default, possibly a description that could allow both, but here the two shapes are alternatives in the invented pair). The figure is not a square. Therefore it is a circle. That is disjunctive syllogism.

A near-miss. The figure is a square. Therefore it is not a circle — only if the problem has said exactly one. Under the inclusive default, that second sentence is not given.

We will not begin a full catalogue of exclusive-or symbols today, and we will not treat everyday English “or” as always exclusive.

Watch me

A labeled invented claim: “The tool is a pencil or a brush.” It is not a pencil. What follows, and why?

  1. The tool is a brush. That is disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q.
  2. The invented “or” is enough, with the denial of one side, to give the other side.
  3. We do not need an exclusive reading for this direction.
