Latin · Purpose and result, a short mix

40 min · Method: keep purpose (ut, nē) distinct from result (ut, ut nōn); sequor and ēgredior; original sentences; no new construction

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a result clause with ut, a first look — Day 166
  • a negative result with ut nōn, a first look — Day 167
  • a result clause with ēgredior, a first look — Day 168

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting mixes the already-taught purpose clauses and result clauses. Keep the jobs distinct. A purpose clause marks an intended purpose: ut for a positive purpose, nē for a negative purpose. A result clause marks what happens as a result, often with tam, ita, or sic: ut for a positive result, ut nōn for a negative result. The verb is the present subjunctive, not the future. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg.

Word How to say it Meaning
ut eum sequātur oot EH-oom seh-KWAH-toor in order that she follow him, or so that she follows him, according to the job
nē eum sequātur nay EH-oom seh-KWAH-toor in order that she not follow him
ut eum nōn sequātur oot EH-oom nohn seh-KWAH-toor so that she does not follow him

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

Purpose: ut or nē. Result: ut or ut nōn, often with tam, ita, or sic. The verb is the present subjunctive, not the future.

Watch me

Label each sentence as purpose or result, and translate it.

  1. Puella venit ut eum sequātur: purpose, she comes in order that she follow him.
  2. Puella tam celeriter venit ut eum sequātur: result, she comes so quickly that she follows him.
  3. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur: negative result, not a nē-clause.

Together

Give one original purpose sentence and one original result sentence, each with sequātur, and say how you can tell them apart.

Hint 1: The signal

A result sentence often has tam, ita, or sic. A purpose sentence need not.

Hint 2: The negative

If the clause is negative, purpose uses nē and result uses ut nōn.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Translate and label: Puella manet nē eum sequātur. Puer tam celeriter surgit ut ēgrediātur.
  2. Why is ut nōn the right negative in Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur?
  3. Why is sequētur still a miss in both a purpose clause and a result clause?

Exercises

  1. Write one original positive purpose, one original negative purpose, one original positive result, and one original negative result. Translate all four.
  2. Change one result sentence so that it uses ēgrediātur, and translate it.

Mathematics · Continuity, a tighter mix

40 min · Method: a tighter mix of already-taught continuity; invented functions; no new rule; a formal epsilon argument reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • continuous at a point, a first look — Day 166
  • a function not continuous at a point where it is not defined — Day 167

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting tightens Monday’s through Wednesday’s work. No new rule. A function is continuous at a when it is defined at a and the limit equals the value. A function that is not defined at a is not continuous at a, even if the limit exists. The algebra of limits, when the values exist, matches continuity of a sum, a product, and a permitted quotient. The functions are invented. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. A derivative remains reserved.

Remember

Continuous at a means defined at a, with the limit equal to the value. A hole is not a point of continuity. The functions are invented.

Watch me

Watch one short mixed set.

  1. Invented f(x) = 2x is continuous at 3, because f(3) = 6 = the limit.
  2. Invented (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not continuous at 2, because it is not defined there, even though the limit is 4.
  3. Invented x + 2 is continuous at 2, because the value and the limit are both 4.

Together

On invented h(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1), is h continuous at 1? At 2?

Hint 1: At 1

h is not defined at 1, so it is not continuous at 1, even though the limit is 2.

Hint 2: At 2

h(2) = (4 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 3, and the function is x + 1 for x ≠ 1, so the limit at 2 is 3. So h is continuous at 2.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key. The functions are invented.

  1. State the two conditions for continuity at a point, and give one invented function that meets them at 3 and one that fails them at 2.
  2. Why is a limit of 4 not enough, by itself, to make (x² − 4)/(x − 2) continuous at 2?
  3. On invented r(x) = x + 1, is r continuous at 0? Give r(0) and the limit.

Exercises

  1. On invented f(x) = 2x and g(x) = x + 1, is f + g continuous at 3? Give the value and the limit.
  2. On invented k(x) = (x² − 16)/(x − 4), at which of 4 and 5 is k continuous, and why?
