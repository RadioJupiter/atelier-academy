Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: adjective agreement

40 min · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English. A review, not a new chapter.

Learn

Key terms

adjective agreement
Definition
An adjective takes the gender, number, and case of the noun it describes. The endings may look like the noun’s, but the job is agreement, not a new family of nouns.
Example
Hortus magnus est: hortus is masculine nominative singular, so magnus matches it. Puella magna est uses magna with puella.
Not this
Not a new chapter, and not Capitulum XI. Agreement is a rule you already use; today names it and practices it with I–X words only.
Connects to
First- and second-family nouns from Day 41, and original classroom sentences. Instruction stays in English.

Mathematics · Algebra XV: iterating a function

60 min · Method: composition already taught, now used as f(f(x)); invented rules stay labeled.

Learn

Key terms

iteration of a function
Definition
Applying the same function more than once: f(f(x)) is f applied to the result of f(x). It is composition of a function with itself, not multiplication of f by itself.
Origin
From Latin iterātiō, from iterō, “to do something for a second time, repeat,” from iterum, “again” (English Wiktionary, entries “iteration” and “iterate”).
Example
If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(f(x)) = 2(2x + 1) + 1 = 4x + 3. So f(f(3)) = 15.
Not this
Not f times f, and not the inverse f⁻¹ unless the lesson asks for an inverse. f(f(x)) is also written (f ∘ f)(x).
Connects to
Day 41’s composition: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Today the two functions are the same function.

Last week, composition meant (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), with g applied first. Today the two functions may be the same function: iteration is applying f more than once.

f(f(x)) means: first find f(x), then put that result into f again. The same idea is (f ∘ f)(x). It is not f(x) · f(x), and it is not the inverse unless a problem asks for the inverse.

A worked function. Let f(x) = 2x + 1, an invented classroom rule, labeled as invented.

  • f(3) = 2 · 3 + 1 = 7.
  • f(f(3)) = f(7) = 2 · 7 + 1 = 15.
  • As a formula: f(f(x)) = 2(2x + 1) + 1 = 4x + 3, and 4 · 3 + 3 = 15, the same value.

You already know how to compose a function with its inverse: (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x. That is a different job from iterating f.

Remember

f(f(x)) means f applied to f(x), also written (f ∘ f)(x). It is not multiplication, and it is not the inverse. If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(f(x)) = 4x + 3.

Key terms: iteration of a function.

Writing & Rhetoric · Because, but, so: a source caution in one sentence

40 min · Method: because / but / so, used only on today's attributed source caution.

Learn

Key terms

because / but / so, on a source caution
Definition
A because / but / so sentence that uses only a source caution already taught today, and that does not invent a verdict the source does not support.
Example
Josephus writes that the house was burnt without Caesar's approbation, but he later says Vespasian gave him an apartment, citizenship, and a pension, so a reader should treat that picture of Titus as a claim by a later client, not as a camera.
Not this
Not a three-word verdict, and not a new fact taken from later books of the War. The sentence uses only what today's history lesson attributes.
Connects to
The because / but / so pattern already taught, now used on today's Josephus caution.

Today's history lesson gives a source caution: Josephus narrates the destruction of the house in the second year of Vespasian (70 CE), and he writes that it was burnt "without Caesar's approbation." In the Life, he also writes that, later in Rome, Vespasian gave him an apartment, the privilege of a Roman citizen, and an annual pension. Writing's job is not to retry the event. It is to put that caution into one because / but / so sentence, using only what was attributed.

A model shape. You may vary the clauses, but you may not add a later book, a modern verdict, or a number the lesson did not give.

Physics · g from G M / R², with sourced Earth figures

50 min · Method: F = G M m / R² and F = m g, then a sourced computation. No invented orbital speed.

Learn

Key terms

g from G M / R²
Definition
Near a sphere, the gravitational field g is G M / R², because F = G M m / R² and F = m g, so the m cancels. g is not the same symbol as G.
Example
Using this course's G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², and the NASA Earth Fact Sheet mass 5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg with equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, the computation gives about 9.79 m/s², limited by the three significant figures in G.
Not this
Not a new measurement of G, and not a claim that 9.8 N/kg is exact at every place on Earth. The fact sheet itself lists more than one surface figure.
Connects to
Day 36's F = G M m / r², and the already-used working value g = 9.8 N/kg near the Earth.

Newton's law, already taught, says the force between two masses is F = G M m / r². Near the Earth, we have also written the weight as F = m g, with this course's working value g = 9.8 N/kg (or 9.8 m/s²). If both describe the same force on a mass m at the surface, then

g = G M / R².

G is still the constant 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², a standard value, not a classroom measurement. M and R must come from a named source.

Sourced figures. NASA's Earth Fact Sheet lists mass 5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg and equatorial radius 6,378.137 km. The same sheet also lists a polar radius 6,356.752 km and a volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km, so Earth is not a perfect sphere. Today's computation uses the equatorial radius, treated as a sphere of that radius, and labels that choice.

The arithmetic, with G kept at three significant figures:

  • R = 6,378.137 km = 6.378137 × 10⁶ m.
  • G M = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(5.9722 × 10²⁴) = 3.9834574 × 10¹⁴.
  • R² = 4.0680638 × 10¹³.
  • g = 3.9834574 × 10¹⁴ / 4.0680638 × 10¹³ ≈ 9.79 m/s².

The extra digits in M and R cannot make the result more precise than G's three significant figures, so we write about 9.79 m/s², not a longer string of digits.

The fact sheet's own surface figures, which we do not pretend to have measured: surface gravity (mean) 9.820 m/s²; surface acceleration (equator) 9.780 m/s². This course's working value 9.8 N/kg is a rounded near-Earth figure, not "the" exact number at every place. The difference between the sheet's two listed figures is noted, not explained by an invented classroom mechanism.

No invented orbital speed.

Remember

g = G M / R². Course G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²; NASA Earth Fact Sheet M and equatorial R give about 9.79 m/s². 9.8 is a rounded working value. Earth is not a perfect sphere.

Key terms: g from G M / R².

  2. Divide both sides by m (m ≠ 0): g = G M / R².

History · 70 CE: Josephus on the house in Jerusalem, and a patronage caution

40 min · Method: a short attributed extract, a conventional event-year, and a caution from the same author's later Life. Not a trial.

Learn

Key terms

70 CE, the destruction of the house in Jerusalem
Definition
The year, in the conventional narrative, of the Roman destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem, which Josephus dates to the second year of Vespasian. This course uses 70 CE for that event-year, and treats Josephus as a narrative source, not as a camera.
Example
Josephus, *War* VI.4 (Whiston), writes of the burning of the “holy house,” and later in the same chapter says that the destruction “happened in the second year of the reign of Vespasian.” Vespasian's reign, already placed after 69 CE in last week's work, makes that second year 70 CE in the conventional count.
Not this
Not a fall day of Rome, and not a claim that we have film of the event. Later chapters of the *War* are not yet this lesson's extract.
Connects to
Last week's 69 CE and the Flavian later name (Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian).
patronage, in a source caution
Definition
Support given by a more powerful person or house, which a later writer may have received. Josephus, in the *Life*, says that in Rome Vespasian gave him an apartment, Roman citizenship, and an annual pension, and that Titus later kept up the same respect. A sparing-story about Titus must be read with that fact in view, without a rushed verdict.
Origin
From Middle English patronage, from Old French patronage; equivalent to patron + -age (English Wiktionary).
Example
Josephus writes that the house was burnt “without Caesar's approbation.” He also writes, later, that he sailed with Titus and was housed and pensioned by Vespasian. Both sentences can be true as sentences Josephus wrote; they do not cancel the need for caution.
Not this
Not a one-word dismissal, and not a one-word exoneration of Titus. The caution is a way of reading, not a trial.
Connects to
Today's extract from *War* VI.4, and the *Life* as Josephus's own later account of how he lived in Rome.

Last week ended 68 CE and taught 69 CE as a civil-war year, not a fall of Rome, with Flavian as a later name for Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian. Today is 70 CE, in the conventional narrative the year of the Roman destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem.

Josephus as a source, not a camera. Flavius Josephus, The War of the Jews Book VI, chapter 4, in William Whiston's translation, public domain, as presented on Wikisource (The War of the Jews/Book VI). In Book VI, chapter 4, after a narrative of gates set on fire and of a later blaze, he writes: “And thus was the holy house burnt down, without Caesar's approbation.” In the same chapter he says that “this its destruction, which happened in the second year of the reign of Vespasian.” Vespasian's reign, already placed after 69, makes that second year 70 CE in the conventional count. This lesson does not convert Josephus's month-and-day remarks into a modern calendar date.

A patronage caution, in Josephus's own later words. Flavius Josephus, The Life of Flavius Josephus, Whiston's translation, public domain, as presented on Wikisource. He writes that he sailed to Rome with Titus, “and when we were come to Rome, I had great care taken of me by Vespasian; for he gave me an apartment in his own house, which he lived in before he came to the empire. He also honored me with the privilege of a Roman citizen, and gave me an annual pension.” He also writes that after Vespasian's death, Titus “kept up the same respect for me which I had from his father.”

So the picture of Titus trying to spare the house is a claim by a writer who, by his own later account, was housed, honored, and pensioned by that same house. We do not have to decide a modern trial. We do have to read the War sentence with the Life sentence in view.

Later chapters of the War, and the Arch of Titus, are reserved for later sittings this week.

Remember

70 CE is the conventional event-year of the destruction Josephus dates to Vespasian's second year. He writes that the house was burnt without Caesar's approbation, and later that Vespasian housed, honored, and pensioned him. Read the first sentence with the second in view. Not a fall of Rome, and not a trial.

Key terms: 70 CE, the destruction of the house in Jerusalem, patronage, in a source caution.

Atelier · A cube under one lamp: three planes

90 min · Method: one lamp, a simple cube, planes of value; not a rushed return to the head.

Learn

Key terms

planes of a cube, under one lamp
Definition
The visible faces of a cube, seen as flat planes of different value under a single lamp: typically a light plane (most turned toward the lamp), a mid plane, and a dark plane. The edges between planes stay edges; they are not rounded like a sphere's turn.
Origin
Plane, in the geometrical sense: from Latin planum, “flat surface,” a noun use of the neuter of planus, “plain”; introduced in the 17th century to distinguish this sense from other senses of plain (English Wiktionary, entry “plane”).
Example
In a one-lamp classroom setup, the top plane may be the lightest, a front plane a midtone, and a side plane darker, if the lamp is high and to one side. A classroom diagram can show that idea; it is not a measured still-life.
Not this
Not last week's sphere, whose turn is curved. A cube's light changes at the edges between planes, not as a smooth wrap.
Connects to
Last Friday's plan named a cube or a cylinder under one lamp as a next simple form, still not a rushed return to the head.

Last week's critique planned a cube under one lamp, still a simple form, still not a rushed new head. Today begins that plan.

A cube shows planes: flat faces. Under one lamp, the visible faces usually fall into a few values: a plane more turned toward the lamp (light), a plane less turned (mid), and a plane turned away (dark). The change happens at the edge between planes. That is the useful contrast with last week's sphere, whose turn was curved.

The diagram is a classroom picture, not a measured still-life. Start by blocking in the three visible planes, and place the lamp so that you can name which plane is light, mid, and dark. Do not round the edges to make the cube "look more like" the sphere.

Modern language · Spanish: saber and conocer (present)

40 min · Method: present tense only; two “know” verbs with different jobs. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

saber and conocer, in the present
Definition
Two present-tense verbs that both translate a kind of “know,” with different jobs. *Saber* is for a fact, or for knowing how (saber + infinitive). *Conocer* is for being familiar with a person or a place. The *yo* forms are *sé* and *conozco*.
Example
Sé la respuesta. Sé nadar. Conozco a María. Conozco el parque. (The personal *a* with a person is already taught.)
Not this
Not two nicknames for the same verb, and not a past tense. *Sé a María* and *conozco la respuesta* are the usual mix-ups this lesson warns against.
Connects to
Present-tense verbs already taught, and the personal *a*. Instruction stays in English.
Word How to say it Meaning
sé SEH I know (a fact, or how to do something)
sabes SAH-bes you know (a fact, or how to)
conozco koh-NOHS-koh I know (a person or a place; I am familiar with)
conoces koh-NOH-ses you know (a person or a place)

Instruction stays in English. Spanish is the subject, not the language of instruction. Today is two present-tense verbs that English often covers with one word, know.

Saber is for a fact, or for knowing how: Sé la respuesta. Sé nadar. (saber + infinitive means “know how to.”)

Conocer is for a person or a place you are familiar with: Conozco a María. Conozco el parque. The personal a is already taught, and it is used with a person.

The yo forms are the ones to watch: sé (with an accent) and conozco. Model sentences: Sé la respuesta. ¿Sabes nadar? Conozco el parque. No conozco a Pedro.

For the guide: no SI units and no year-dates in the Spanish. Hear clips use es-MX.

Remember

Saber: a fact, or how to (sé, sabes). Conocer: a person or a place (conozco, conoces; personal a with a person). Not the same verb, and not a past tense.

Key terms: saber and conocer, in the present.

Logic · Modus ponens and modus tollens

50 min · Method: two valid forms, set beside last week's two invalid forms, with the square-and-rectangle contrast.

Learn

Key terms

modus ponens
Definition
A valid form: If P, then Q; P; therefore Q. From a conditional and its “if” side, you may infer the “then” side. It is not affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P), which was taught as invalid last week.
Origin
From Latin modus ponendo ponens, roughly “mode where affirming affirms” (English Wiktionary).
Example
If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle. This figure is a square. Therefore it is a rectangle.
Not this
Not affirming the consequent, which uses the “then” side as a reason for the “if” side, and is invalid. A non-square rectangle is the already-taught counterexample to that invalid form, not to modus ponens.
Connects to
Last week's affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent, now set beside two valid forms.
modus tollens
Definition
A valid form: If P, then Q; not Q; therefore not P. From a conditional and the denial of its “then” side, you may infer the denial of its “if” side. It is not denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q), which was taught as invalid last week.
Origin
From Latin modus tollendo tollens, “the mode where the denying denies,” from modus (“mode”) and forms of tollere, “to take away” (English Wiktionary).
Example
If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle. This figure is not a rectangle. Therefore it is not a square.
Not this
Not denying the antecedent, which uses “not P” as a reason for “not Q,” and is invalid. A non-square rectangle is a rectangle and not a square, so “not a square” does not give “not a rectangle.”
Connects to
Last week's denying the antecedent, and today's modus ponens.

Last week taught two invalid forms: affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P) and denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q). Today names two valid forms that are easily confused with those two.

Modus ponens. If P, then Q. P. Therefore Q. You affirm the “if” side, and you may take the “then” side.

Modus tollens. If P, then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P. You deny the “then” side, and you may take the denial of the “if” side.

The already-taught square-and-rectangle pair still helps:

  • Valid (ponens): If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is a square. Therefore it is a rectangle.
  • Valid (tollens): If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is not a rectangle. Therefore it is not a square.
  • Invalid (affirming the consequent): It is a rectangle; therefore it is a square. A non-square rectangle blocks that.
  • Invalid (denying the antecedent): It is not a square; therefore it is not a rectangle. The same non-square rectangle blocks that.

Remember

Modus ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q. Modus tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Not affirming the consequent, and not denying the antecedent.

Key terms: modus ponens, modus tollens.

Watch me

Label the form: “If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is not a rectangle. Therefore it is not a square.”

  1. The premises are a conditional and the denial of its “then” side.
  2. The conclusion is the denial of the “if” side.
  3. That is modus tollens, and it is valid.
