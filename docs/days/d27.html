Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 2: the relative quī, quae, quod

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VIII · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

relative pronoun (quī, quae, quod)
Definition
A pronoun that introduces a relative clause and refers back to a noun (its antecedent). The form agrees with the antecedent in gender and number, but takes the case required by its own clause. A first working set: quī (masc. nom.), quae (fem. nom.), quod (neuter nom./acc.), quem, quam.
Example
Puer quī in hortō est Mārcus est. The boy who is in the garden is Marcus. Rosa quam Iūlia habet pulchra est. The rose that Julia has is beautiful.
Not this
Not the interrogative quis? / quid? (‘who?’ / ‘what?’), though the forms overlap in part. A relative clause does not, by itself, ask a question.
Connects to
English often uses who / which / that. Latin shows the job by the ending: quem is a masculine object in its own clause, even if the antecedent was a subject in the main clause.
Word How to say it Meaning
quī kwee who, which (masculine nominative)
quae kweye who, which (feminine nominative)
quod kwod which, that (neuter nominative or accusative)
quem kwem whom, which (masculine accusative)
quam kwahm whom, which (feminine accusative)
quis? kwis who? (interrogative, not the relative)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The agreement rule, in two parts. Gender and number come from the antecedent. Case comes from the job inside the relative clause.

Original sentences: Puer quī in hortō est Mārcus est. Puella quae rosam habet Iūlia est. Liber quem Mārcus habet magnus est. Rosa quam Iūlia Mārcō dat pulchra est.

If the antecedent is puer (subject in the main clause) but the relative is the object of videt, you need quem: Puer quem Iūlia videt Mārcus est. ‘The boy whom Julia sees is Marcus.’

Remember

A relative agrees in gender and number with its antecedent, but takes the case of its own clause. quī / quae / quod; quem / quam. Not the question-word quis?

Key terms: relative pronoun (quī, quae, quod).

Watch me

Read and make sense of: Puer quem Iūlia videt Mārcus est. Why quem, not quī?

  1. The antecedent is puer (masculine singular), so the relative is masculine singular.
  2. Inside the clause, the boy is the object of videt (Julia sees him), so the case is accusative: quem.
  3. quī would be nominative, as if the boy were the subject of the relative clause.

Hint 1: Antecedent

Hint 2: Sentence

Mathematics · Algebra IX: choosing an AP or a GP, and a first applied sum

60 min · Method: test for a constant difference or a constant ratio, then the matching formula; invented applications labeled

Learn

Key terms

mixed progressions
Definition
Problems that ask you to decide whether a sequence is arithmetic, geometric, or neither, and then to use the matching formula for a term or a sum — not to apply one formula to every list of numbers.
Example
3, 6, 12, 24 is a GP (r = 2). 3, 6, 9, 12 is an AP (d = 3). 3, 6, 10, 15 is neither (differences 3, 4, 5; ratios 2, 10/6, 15/10).
Not this
Not ‘if it is increasing, it is a GP,’ and not a claim that every useful sequence is one of the two.
Connects to
Days 20, 25, and 26 already taught the separate tools. Today the skill is choosing the tool, including a first applied question (a growing or steady list of lengths or counts).

A test, before a formula. Subtract consecutive terms: a constant difference means an AP. Divide consecutive terms (when the terms are not zero): a constant ratio means a GP. If neither is constant, neither formula applies.

Applied, still honest. A list of measurements in a problem is a model. If a habitat module’s stored water is described as 80 L, then 40 L, then 20 L, that is a GP with r = 1/2 in the problem, not a measured NASA fact. Label the situation as a course problem when the numbers are invented.

Worked mix:

  • First 8 terms of 5, 9, 13, … : AP, S = 152 (Day 25).
  • First 5 terms of 5, 10, 20, … : GP, S = 155 (Day 26).
  • 7, 7, 7, 7, 7: AP with d = 0 and GP with r = 1; S = 35 either way (n a, or n/2 · (7+7)).

Remember

Test d, then r. Use the AP sum or the GP sum only when the matching constant is there. A sequence can be both (d = 0, r = 1). Invented applications must be labeled.

Key terms: mixed progressions.

Watch me

  1. 3, 6, 12, 24 has ratios 2, 2, 2: a GP, a = 3, r = 2, S = 3(16 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 45. (Or 3+6+12+24=45.)
  2. 3, 6, 9, 12 has differences 3, 3, 3: an AP, a = 3, d = 3, last = 12, S = 4/2 × (3+12) = 30.
  3. The lists start the same and then diverge; the test comes before the formula.

Writing & Rhetoric · Reconstruction from a three-paragraph outline

45 min · Method: Franklin’s reconstruction, now at three-paragraph scale; compare, then repair one miss

Learn

Key terms

reconstruction (three paragraphs)
Definition
A Franklin step in which you write a short three-paragraph piece from a hint-list only, then compare it with your outline or with a model, and note the joints you kept or missed.
Example
From hints — thesis on same-arc inscribed angles; worked 80°/40°; concession about a different arc — a student writes three short paragraphs and then checks the outline, not the other way around.
Not this
Not a first draft written with the outline open the whole time (that is ordinary drafting). Reconstruction is from the hints after they have been set aside for a moment, then a comparison.
Connects to
Days 17 and 24 practiced reconstruction at sentence or paragraph scale. Today it is three short paragraphs, still brief.

The sequence. Finish or tidy a three-line outline (Day 26). Turn the outline into hints of a few words each. Put the outline away. Write three short paragraphs from the hints. Open the outline and compare.

Length. Three short paragraphs, not a packet. Each paragraph does its job and stops.

A comparison list. Did the thesis survive in the opening? Did paragraph two keep one line of support, with a named reason? Did paragraph three grant a real limit and return in new words? Note one miss to repair, rather than recopying the whole for neatness.

Remember

Hints from the outline, outline away, three short paragraphs, then a comparison. Repair the largest miss; do not recopy for neatness only.

Key terms: reconstruction (three paragraphs).

Watch me

From this hint-list only, start an opening paragraph: “Livy late; major narrative; not eyewitness of the kings.”

  1. A model start: Livy is a major narrative source for early Rome, and he is a late one: he wrote centuries after the traditional period of the kings.
  2. The hints name the joints; the sentences supply grammar and a careful wording (“traditional period,” “centuries later”).
  3. A quotation is not required in the opening.

Science · Paper chromatography of leaf pigments

45 min · Method: a carefully described separation; no invented Rf values or promised band distances

Learn

Key terms

paper chromatography
Definition
A separation method in which a solvent moves up a piece of paper by capillary action and carries dissolved substances at different rates, so they form bands. A leaf extract can show more than one pigment, even when the leaf looked simply green.
Origin
From chromato- (‘color’) + -graphy. The technique was named for separating plant pigments into colored bands (Wiktionary).
Example
A green leaf’s extract may show a yellow or yellow-orange band as well as a green one, because carotenoids can travel differently from chlorophyll on the paper.
Not this
Not a proof of every pigment’s chemical formula, and not a measurement this course will invent as an Rf value (the ratio of a band’s travel to the solvent’s travel). Those ratios depend on the paper, the solvent, and the conditions.
Connects to
Tuesday and Thursday of last week taught leftover light and the net photosynthesis equation. Today is a laboratory method, described carefully, not a recipe with invented numbers.
carotenoid (leaf)
Definition
A class of yellow and orange leaf pigments, already present in many green leaves, usually masked by chlorophyll in midsummer views of the leaf. In a chromatogram they may appear as a yellow or orange band.
Example
A yellow band above or apart from a green band, in a leaf chromatogram, is often a carotenoid (or a mixture of them), at the level of this lesson’s reading of the paper.
Not this
Not “the” single yellow chemical, and not an invented percentage of the leaf’s pigment mass. Some leaves also make red or purple anthocyanins, which are a different class and will be named carefully on Day 29.
Connects to
The word is from carotene (Wiktionary notes the carotenoid entry as formed from that name). This course will not press a fuller etymology than that.

The method, in outline (a classroom or home setup, with adult care for the solvent). A strip of paper stands in a little solvent, with a concentrated spot of leaf extract above the liquid line. The solvent climbs. Pigments in the spot dissolve and are carried up, but they cling to the paper and dissolve in the solvent to different degrees, so they separate into bands.

What you might see — described, not promised as a single picture. Green bands (chlorophylls) and yellow or orange bands (carotenoids) are common in green-leaf extracts. The exact order and spacing depend on the solvent and the paper. This course will not invent a diagram of measured distances.

Rf, named and then left unused as a number. Some laboratory courses record Rf = (distance the pigment travels) / (distance the solvent travels). Because that number is condition-dependent, we will not quote a fake “typical Rf of chlorophyll.”

Safety, briefly. Use a school-appropriate solvent as directed in a real lab sheet; do not improvise a household chemical on the basis of this paragraph alone. The lesson teaches the idea of the separation, not a complete lab protocol.

Remember

A solvent carries pigments up paper at different rates, so a green leaf can show more than one band. Carotenoids may appear yellow or orange. No invented Rf numbers. This is a method, not a full safety protocol.

Key terms: paper chromatography, carotenoid (leaf).

Watch me

Why can a leaf that looked simply green show a yellow band as well as a green one on the paper?

  1. Green leaves often already contain yellow or orange carotenoids as well as chlorophyll.
  2. Chlorophyll usually masks those colors in the intact leaf.
  3. Chromatography separates the pigments, so a yellow band can become visible on the paper.

Together

What is an Rf value, and why does this course not give you one to memorize?

Hint 1: The ratio

Distance a pigment travels divided by the distance the solvent travels.

Hint 2: Conditions

The number depends on the paper, the solvent, and the conditions; a memorized “typical” value would be invented here.

Literature · Odyssey, Books 9–12: the wanderings, told in Scheria

45 min · Text: Homer, Odyssey 9–12; Butler (1898) for short public-domain quotation

Learn

Key terms

nostos
Definition
A homecoming or return, especially the return of a Greek hero from Troy. The Odyssey is, among other things, a poem of Odysseus’s nostos, delayed and endangered, and of the home he must still recover when he arrives.
Origin
From Greek νόστος, ‘homecoming, return’ (Wiktionary).
Example
Books 9–12 tell, in Odysseus’s voice, a long delay of that return: the Cyclops, Circe, the visit to the dead, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun.
Not this
Not already an arrival in Ithaca (that is later). A nostos can be narrated before it is completed.
Connects to
The word is from Greek νόστος, ‘homecoming, return’ (Wiktionary). This course uses it as a literary term, not as a claim about a historical voyage.
wanderings (Books 9–12)
Definition
The adventures Odysseus narrates to the Phaeacians in Books 9–12, which belong earlier in the story’s time than Books 5–8. They include, in a usual school list, the Cyclops, Circe, the underworld, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the cattle of the Sun, after which Odysseus reaches Calypso’s island alone.
Example
We hear the Cyclops story after we already know, from Books 1–8, that Odysseus is alive and among the Phaeacians. The listener in the poem is a guest at a feast; the listener outside the poem already holds the Telemachy in mind.
Not this
Not a complete catalogue you must memorize as twelve named episodes in a test of trivia, and not a historical itinerary. Hold the design (a story inside the story) and a few named episodes, carefully.
Connects to
Hospitality and its abuse (the Cyclops as a host who eats his guests; later, the suitors as guests who eat a house) is one thread to notice, without forcing every episode into a single moral.

Books 9–12, as a telling. Odysseus, still in Scheria, names himself and tells his earlier wanderings in order. The poem’s present time is still the Phaeacian visit; the adventures are a narrative inside the narrative.

A short attributed option. If you quote, use a brief public-domain phrase from Butler (1898) with a book number — for example, a clause from the Cyclops episode or from the visit to the dead — not a long paste.

Where the books leave him. By the end of this telling, Odysseus is the sole survivor of his crew and will be back, in the poem’s earlier present, at Calypso’s island, which you have already seen in Book 5. Do not run on into the slaughter in the hall; that is not today’s sitting.

Remember

Books 9–12 are earlier wanderings narrated by Odysseus to the Phaeacians: a story inside the story. nostos is a homecoming, still not completed. Hold a few named episodes and the design; do not treat the list as trivia or as a historical map.

Key terms: nostos, wanderings (Books 9–12).

Watch me

  2. The Cyclops belongs to the earlier wanderings, narrated in Book 9.
  3. The Phaeacians (and we) hear a completed stretch of story that Odysseus, in the poem’s present, has already survived.

Atelier · The nose and mouth, in the context of the head

90 min · Method: mid-face planes on the existing head; measure, then small openings and thicknesses

Learn

Key terms

nose and mouth in context
Definition
The nose and mouth placed as part of the feature triangle on the existing head, with the under-plane of the nose, the center line through the mouth, and their value families, rather than a separately perfected nostril or lip on a blank mid-face.
Example
A nose whose underside is a plane in shadow, and a mouth that sits at the measured distance from the base of the nose, are in context even if the teeth are not drawn.
Not this
Not a smile invented to make a plaster “friendly,” and not a nostril hole punched darker than the head’s large shadow without a look at the light.
Connects to
Yesterday’s eye sitting remains; today you add the mid-face group without restarting the head.

The nose. A wedge or a simple set of planes: front, two sides, and an underside. The underside is often a small under-plane, darker than the lit side, but still judged against the head’s large terminator. Nostrils are openings in that under-plane, not black commas placed first.

The mouth. A center line (which follows the turn), a length compared with the eyes or with the nose’s width (measure), and lips with a little thickness. Teeth, if they appear at all, are a later, quieter plane in a cavity, not a row of white fences.

The group. Recheck the feature triangle after you state nose and mouth. If the mouth has drifted off the center line, the head’s likeness to the plate fails before any lip detail matters.

Remember

Nose as planes (including an underside); mouth on the center line, with thickness; both in the head’s values. Measure the feature triangle again. Do not invent a smile or a black nostril as a first act.

Key terms: nose and mouth in context.

Watch me

  2. Whether the underside of the nose has been stated as a plane first.
  3. A punched black that ignores the head’s value family will sit on the surface like a mark, not like an opening.

Modern language · Spanish: este, ese, and aquel

40 min · Method: a three-way pointing set, with agreement; neuter esto / eso / aquello without a noun

Learn

Key terms

demonstratives (este, ese, aquel)
Definition
Adjectives (and, with small changes, pronouns) that point: este / esta / estos / estas (this, near the speaker), ese / esa / esos / esas (that, near the listener or a mid distance), aquel / aquella / aquellos / aquellas (that, farther away). They agree in gender and number with the noun.
Origin
From Latin dēmōnstrātīvus (Wiktionary).
Example
Este libro (here, by me); ese libro (by you, or just there); aquel libro (over there). Esta casa, esas casas, aquellas frases.
Not this
Not the definite article (el / la), which does not point in this way, and not English “this/that” used without agreement. Neuter pronouns esto, eso, aquello point to a thing or an idea not named as a noun; they are invariable and do not take a noun after them.
Connects to
Latin this fortnight contrasted hic and ille. Spanish here uses a three-way pointing set. Instruction stays in English; Hear is for the Spanish words.

esto ES-toh this (neuter pronoun: this thing / this idea)

Agreement. este libro, esta casa, estos libros, estas casas. The same pattern for ese and aquel (aquel / aquella / aquellos / aquellas).

A first use of esto. Esto es importante. ¿Qué es eso? No noun immediately after esto / eso / aquello.

Original sentences: Este libro es mío; ese libro es tuyo. Aquella casa es grande. Me gusta esta clase. ¿Esto es un lápiz?

Remember

este = this (near me); ese = that (mid / near you); aquel = that (farther). They agree with the noun. esto / eso / aquello are neuter pronouns and do not take a noun after them.

Key terms: demonstratives (este, ese, aquel).

Music theory · I, IV, and V in a major key, and a first authentic cadence

40 min · Method: primary triads from the major scale already taught; V–I as a first cadence; listen first

Learn

Key terms

I, IV, and V (major key)
Definition
The primary triads of a major key: the tonic (I, built on degree 1), the subdominant (IV, on degree 4), and the dominant (V, on degree 5). In C major, I is C–E–G, IV is F–A–C, and V is G–B–D — three major triads.
Example
A short progression I–IV–V–I in C major is C, F, G, C, as complete triads or as a simple accompaniment.
Not this
Not the only chords in a key, and not yet a full set of minor-key primary chords (which would need more than last week’s natural minor in many common-practice uses). Today is a major key only.
Connects to
Week 3 built major triads as stacked thirds. Today those triads are placed on degrees 1, 4, and 5 of a scale you already have.
authentic cadence (first look)
Definition
A close or ending in which V moves to I, so the dominant triad resolves to the tonic. This course uses it as a first, audible cadence, not as a complete catalogue of cadence types (plagal, half, deceptive, and the rest can wait).
Origin
From Italian cadenza, from Latin cadēns, of cadō, ‘I fall’ (Wiktionary).
Example
In C major, a G-major triad (V) moving to a C-major triad (I) is an authentic cadence in this first sense. A more careful later distinction (whether the soprano ends on the tonic, and whether both chords are in root position) is named as existing, not required for today’s ear.
Not this
Not every V–I on a page is the end of a piece, and not a claim that popular or folk music must use this close. It is a common-practice school pattern, taught as such.
Connects to
Listen: V feels unfinished relative to I; I after V feels like a close, in this pattern. The word cadence is connected with a falling or a close (from Italian cadenza, from Latin cadō, ‘I fall,’ Wiktionary), which matches the job more than a literal fall of pitch in every case.

Build the three triads, in C major first. Scale: C D E F G A B C. I: C–E–G. IV: F–A–C. V: G–B–D. All three are major triads in a major key.

Then in G major, as a check: I is G–B–D, IV is C–E–G, V is D–F♯–A (F♯ is the leading tone of G major, already in last week’s relative-minor work as a pitch in G major / E minor).

A first cadence. Play or sing V then I, slowly. That close is today’s authentic cadence in the broad school sense. We will not yet demand a four-part textbook ending.

What we are not doing. No harmonic minor, no inversion drill as the main skill, no long Roman-numeral analysis of a whole piece.

Remember

In a major key, I, IV, and V are the primary triads (all major). In C: C, F, G major. A first authentic cadence is V going to I. Not a full catalogue of cadences, and not a minor-key lesson today.

Key terms: I, IV, and V (major key), authentic cadence (first look).

Watch me
