Learn

How this course works. Ørberg's book teaches Latin through Latin: every word is made clear by a picture, a map, the margin notes or the context. Mathematics · Modeling: from bar model to equation

60 min · Method: Singapore model method (part-whole, comparison and change models), then translation into algebra

Learn

Most errors in applied problems happen before the algebra: the relationship is misread. The Singapore model method draws each quantity as a bar. Three structures cover almost every problem: part-whole (parts make a whole), comparison (one bar is longer than the other by a difference), and change (a bar grows or shrinks). Once the bars are right, the equation is just the same relationship in symbols.

Learn

Why start here. Most errors in applied problems happen before any algebra: the relationship between quantities is misread. The Singapore model method draws each quantity as a bar so that the relationship is visible. Three structures cover almost every problem: part-whole (parts make a whole), comparison (one quantity is a multiple of, or differs from, another) and change (a quantity is increased, decreased or transferred). Draw first; write the equation second; check the answer in the original words third.

Worked example 1 (part-whole with fractions of a remainder). A gallery sold 3/5 of its prints in the morning and 1/4 of the remaining prints in the afternoon. 48 prints were left. How many were there at the start?

all printssold a.m. (3 fifths)2 fifths left
2 fifths leftp.m.48

The remainder is 2/5 of the whole. A quarter of it was sold, so 3/4 of 2/5 = 3/10 of the whole is left. Then 3/10 of the total = 48, so the total is 160. Algebra: (3/10)x = 48, x = 160. Check: 160 → 96 sold, 64 left → 16 sold, 48 left. ✓

Worked example 2 (comparison with one quantity unchanged). Two artists had money in the ratio 5 : 3. After the first spent $84, the ratio was 2 : 3. How much did each have at first?

The second artist's amount does not change, so keep it as 3 units in both ratios. The first artist goes from 5 units to 2 units, so 3 units = $84, and 1 unit = $28. First artist: $140; second: $84. Algebra: (5u − 84)/3u = 2/3 gives 15u − 252 = 6u, so u = 28. The bar model finds the "unchanged quantity" trick that the algebra hides.

Remember

Part-whole: A + B = W. Comparison: larger = smaller + difference. Change: start ± change = end. Draw the bars before you write the equation.

Watch me

Two parts make 84, and one part is 30 more than the other. Draw two bars that together make 84. Cut 30 off the longer bar. The remaining 54 is two equal small parts, so each small part is 27, and the larger is 57. Equation: let the smaller be x. Then x + (x + 30) = 84, so 2x = 54, x = 27.

Together

A length is 50, and it is made of two parts, one twice the other. Draw the bars and write the equation.

Hint 1: Bars

Three equal units make 50: two for the long part, one for the short.

Hint 2: Equation

Let the short part be x. Then x + 2x = 50, so x = 50/3.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. In a part-whole bar, the parts are 12 and 19. What is the whole?
  2. Two numbers differ by 8 and add to 40. What is the smaller?

Exercises

Draw a model for every problem before writing any equation.

  1. A painter used 2/7 of a tube of white on Monday and 1/3 of what remained on Tuesday. 20 mL were left. How much was in the tube?
  2. Sketchbooks and canvases were in the ratio 4 : 7. After 15 more sketchbooks were bought, there were equal numbers of each. How many sketchbooks were there at first?
  3. Two numbers add to 96. The larger is 4 less than three times the smaller. Find both.
  4. One account held three times as much as another. After $40 was added to each, the first held twice as much as the second. Find the original amounts.
  5. A price rose by 20% and then fell by 20%. The final price was $192. What was the original price? Explain with a model why the answer is not $192.
  6. Three boxes hold 125 brushes in all. Box B holds 15 more than box A, and box C holds twice as many as box B. How many are in each box?
  7. (Stretch.) A and B together finish a job in 6 days; B and C in 10 days; A and C in 7.5 days. How long would A alone take?

Writing & Rhetoric · The sentence: kernels and expansion

45 min · Method: Hochman, The Writing Revolution (sentence-level instruction first, practiced on real content)

Learn

A kernel is a bare sentence: subject + verb, and sometimes an object (The painter looks). You expand it by answering who/what, when, where, why, how. Each addition must be a real phrase in the sentence, not a loose note in the margin.

Learn

Principle. Good paragraphs are built from controlled sentences, and controlled sentences are built deliberately. The Writing Revolution begins at the sentence: you take a bare kernel (subject + verb, sometimes an object) and expand it by answering the questions who/what, when, where, why, how. Each addition must be accurate and must earn its place.

Worked example. Kernel: The mural faded. - when: over four centuries - where: on the damp north wall of the refectory - why: because the painter had applied tempera to dry plaster

Expanded: Over four centuries, the mural on the damp north wall of the refectory faded, because the painter had applied tempera to dry plaster rather than pigment to wet plaster. (Notice that the "why" is a causal claim, so it must be true. A real case: Leonardo painted the Last Supper in tempera and oil on a dry wall instead of in true fresco, and the paint began flaking within decades.)

Because / but / so. The same stem finished three ways forces three kinds of thinking: because gives a reason, but gives a contrast, so gives a consequence.

Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, because canvas resisted the city's damp better than wall plaster. Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, but fresco remained common elsewhere in Italy. Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, so large paintings could be rolled and shipped.

Remember

Kernel first. Expand by answering one question at a time. Keep the same verb unless you have a reason to change it.

Watch me

Kernel: The painter looks. When? at dawn. Where? out of the high window. Why? because the light has just reached the street. Expanded: At dawn the painter looks out of the high window, because the light has just reached the street.

Together

Expand the kernel The student draws with a where and a why.

Hint 1: Where

For example: in the studio, from a plaster cast.

Hint 2: Why

For example: to train the eye before painting. Then write one sentence.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What is a kernel, in one sentence?
  2. Name three questions you can use to expand a kernel.

Exercises

  1. Expand each kernel with at least three question words. Label each addition. (a) The bridge collapsed. (b) The painter scraped the canvas. (c) Prices rose.
  2. Complete each stem with because, but and so (nine sentences). Only use facts you are sure of, or check them. (a) Charcoal is used for the first block-in of a drawing … (b) Latin is no longer anyone's first language … (c) A ball thrown straight up slows down as it rises …
  3. Rewrite this sentence so that it is precise: The thing in the picture is kind of really interesting because of how it is.

Physics · Motion: position, displacement, velocity

45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student

Learn

Position is where something is, measured from a chosen origin along a line or axis (it can be positive or negative). Displacement is the change in position: final minus initial. It has a direction. Distance travelled is the length of the path, and is never negative. Velocity is displacement divided by the time taken (average velocity). Speed is distance divided by time, and has no direction.

Learn

How Peer Instruction works here. In Mazur's classes, students read before class, then answer short conceptual questions (ConcepTests) individually, discuss with a neighbour and answer again. Working alone, you will: (1) read the notes; (2) answer each ConcepTest on your own, writing your answer, a confidence rating (1 = guess, 3 = sure) and a one-sentence reason; (3) do the "peer" step: write the strongest argument you can for a different option, or explain your answer aloud to a tutor or family member and let them question it; (4) answer again; (5) only then check the key. Record whether you changed your answer. The point is to expose your reasoning, not to guess.

Pre-reading (10 min). - Position x is where an object is relative to a chosen origin, with a sign for direction. - Displacement Δx = xfinal − xinitial: a vector (size and direction). Distance is the total path length: a scalar, never negative. - Average velocity = Δx / Δt. Average speed = distance / Δt. They differ whenever the path doubles back. - On a position–time graph, the slope is the velocity. A straight line means constant velocity.

ConcepTests

  1. A runner completes one full 400 m lap and stops at the starting line after 80 s. The runner's average velocity is: (A) 5 m/s (B) 0 m/s (C) 2.5 m/s (D) impossible to say without the direction.
  2. A position–time graph is a straight line sloping downward. The object is: (A) moving in the negative direction at constant speed (B) slowing down (C) at rest (D) speeding up in the negative direction.
  3. A car drives 60 km at 30 km/h and then another 60 km at 60 km/h. Its average speed for the whole trip is: (A) 45 km/h (B) 40 km/h (C) 50 km/h (D) 90 km/h.

Problems

  1. A cyclist rides 3.0 km east, then 4.0 km north, in 0.50 h. Find the distance, the size of the displacement and the average speed. What is the size of the average velocity?
  2. Sketch a position–time graph for: walk away from a wall at 1 m/s for 4 s, stand still 2 s, walk back toward the wall at 2 m/s for 2 s. Where do you end up?

Remember

displacement Δx = xfinal − xinitial. average velocity = Δx / Δt. Distance and speed ignore direction; displacement and velocity do not.

Watch me

A walk starts at x = 2 m and ends at x = 8 m in 3 s, going straight there.

  1. Displacement = 8 − 2 = 6 m (in the positive direction).
  2. Distance travelled = 6 m (the same, because the path did not double back).
  3. Average velocity = 6 m / 3 s = 2 m/s. Average speed = 2 m/s as well, in this case.

Together

A walker goes from x = 5 m to x = 1 m in 2 s. What is the displacement, and what is the average velocity?

Hint 1: Displacement

1 − 5 = −4 m (toward the negative side).

Hint 2: Velocity

−4 m / 2 s = −2 m/s.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. A body goes from x = 0 to x = 4 m and back to x = 0. What is the displacement? What is the distance travelled?
  2. If that trip took 8 s, what is the average velocity?

History · How historians work

45 min · Method: chronological study from primary sources

Purpose of the course. This year runs chronologically from the first written records to the modern world. You will read what people at the time actually wrote (primary sources) and judge later accounts (secondary sources) against them.

Primary and secondary. A primary source was produced at the time by someone involved: a law code, a letter, a coin, an inscription, a painting. A secondary source interprets the past afterwards: a textbook, a biography, a documentary. The same document can be either, depending on the question: a 1920s history of Rome is secondary for Rome but primary for how the 1920s saw Rome.

Four questions for every source (sourcing). 1. Who made it, and what position were they in? 2. When and where, and how close to the events? 3. For whom, and why: to inform, persuade, command, flatter, remember? 4. What does it assume that the author did not need to say?

Then corroborate (does another independent source agree?) and contextualise (what else was happening?).

Dating. BCE/CE and BC/AD count the same years; there is no year zero (1 BCE is followed by 1 CE). "c." (circa) marks an approximate date. Dates for the ancient Near East depend on which chronology scholars use, so they can differ by decades between books.

Exercises

  1. Classify each as primary or secondary for the study of ancient Rome, and explain any that could be both: (a) a Roman coin; (b) a 19th-century painting of a Roman triumph; (c) a modern encyclopedia article on Augustus; (d) a letter written by Pliny the Younger; (e) the same 19th-century painting, used to study 19th-century taste.
  2. A king's inscription says he won a great victory. A neighbouring kingdom's chronicle never mentions the battle. List three possible explanations and what evidence would help decide between them.
  3. How many years passed from 15 BCE to 15 CE? Show why.

Atelier · Setting up, sight-size and line control

90 min · Method: academic atelier training (the sequence behind the Bargue–Gérôme Drawing Course)

Learn

Sight-size means the drawing is set so that, from a fixed viewing point, the drawing and the subject appear the same size. You can then compare lengths with a straight arm and a pencil, or with a plumb line. Line control starts with slow, light, straight or slightly curved strokes that you can adjust, not with a heavy outline you cannot erase cleanly.

Learn

The atelier sequence. Classical ateliers train the eye and hand in a fixed order: line and proportion (copying the Bargue plates), then value (light and shadow) on simple forms, then cast drawing from plaster, then painting in limited and full palettes, then the live model. Each stage removes one difficulty at a time. Speed comes later; accuracy comes first.

Materials. Graphite pencils 2H, HB, 2B; a kneaded eraser; smooth heavy drawing paper (Bristol or similar); a sandpaper block or blade for a long, sharp point; a plumb line (thread with a small weight); a thin straight stick or knitting needle for measuring; a hand mirror; masking tape; a board and an easel or a chair-back that holds the board upright.

Sight-size. Set the drawing beside the subject so that, from one fixed viewing spot a few paces back, the drawing and the subject appear the same size. Mark the spot on the floor. Make every judgement from that spot, then walk forward to draw. Because the two are seen side by side at the same size, errors of proportion and angle show up directly. (For copying a printed plate, place the plate beside your paper at the same height and size.)

Holding the pencil. For long lines, hold the pencil overhand, well back from the point, and move from the shoulder and elbow, not the fingers.

Remember

Stand at one marked viewing point. Hold the pencil at arm's length to compare heights and widths. Start light. Check verticals with a plumb line.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Mark a viewing point on the floor, two or three meters from the easel, and always stand there to look.
  2. On scrap paper, draw a light vertical of about 12 cm, using the shoulder more than the fingers.
  3. From the viewing point, compare that vertical to a chosen edge on the subject (or a stick). Adjust the drawn line until the two look the same length.
  4. Add a second line at a measured angle, still light, and only then darken what is accurate.

Together

Why should the first lines be light?

Hint 1: Correction

You will need to move them when a comparison shows an error.

Hint 2: Answer

A light line can be adjusted or erased; a heavy outline fights you.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What is sight-size, in one sentence?
  2. What is a plumb line used to check?

Exercises (timed)

  1. (20 min) Straight lines: fill a page with parallel horizontal, vertical and 45° lines, each about 15 cm. "Ghost" each line (rehearse the movement in the air) twice before drawing.
  2. (20 min) Proportional division by eye: draw ten lines; mark the half on five and the third on five, by eye only. Then measure with a ruler and write the error in millimeters beside each mark.
  3. (20 min) Angle copying: lay a stick at an angle on the table; from your marked spot, copy the angle onto paper using the plumb line as a vertical reference. Do six angles. Check with a protractor against the stick.
  4. (20 min) Ellipses: draw ellipses in sets of five at the same size, through the whole arm, about 8 cm wide.
  5. (10 min) Mirror check: view the morning's lines in the mirror. Reversal reveals tilts the eye has grown used to. Note three errors.

Modern language · Spanish: sounds and greetings

25 min · Method: pronunciation first, then high-frequency phrases used daily; spaced review

Learn

Spanish has five pure vowel sounds. They do not glide the way English vowels often do. Study the sounds and the first greetings, then listen to each word with the Hear-word button.

Word How to say it Meaning
a (casa) ah as in *father*, short
e (mesa) eh as in *they*, short
i (sí) ee as in *machine*
o (todo) oh as in *note*, short
u (luna) oo as in *rude*
hola OH-lah hello
adiós ah-DYOHS goodbye
buenos días BWEH-nohs DEE-ahs good morning
por favor por fah-BOR please
gracias GRAH-syahs thank you

Learn

Vowels. Spanish has five vowel sounds, and each is pure and short: a (casa), e (mesa), i (sí), o (todo), u (luna). They never glide as English vowels do.

Consonants to watch. h is silent (hora); j, and g before e/i, is a breathy sound from the back of the throat (jardín, gente); ñ is the "ny" sound of English canyon (año); ll and y sound like English "y" for most speakers; z, and c before e/i, sound like "s" in Latin America and like "th" in most of Spain; r between vowels is a single tap, while rr and initial r are trilled.

Stress rules. (1) Words ending in a vowel, n or s stress the second-to-last syllable: VEN-ta-na. (2) Other words stress the last syllable: re-LOJ. (3) A written accent overrides both rules: ÁR-bol, can-CIÓN.

Phrases. Hola · Buenos días · Buenas tardes · Buenas noches · ¿Qué tal? · ¿Cómo estás? · Bien, gracias, ¿y tú? · Mucho gusto · Hasta luego · Adiós.

Remember

H is silent (hora). J, and g before e or i, is a breathy back sound (jardín, gente). Ñ is like ny in canyon. Greet with hola or buenos días.

Watch me

The guide says hola and waves, then buenos días in the morning. Gracias answers a kindness; por favor goes with a request. The vowels stay pure: hola is OH-lah, not 'holler'.

Logic · Propositions and truth tables

30 min · Seminar rotation: Logic (Mondays)

A proposition is a statement that is either true or false. "Rome is in Italy" is a proposition; "Shut the door" is not. Propositions combine with connectives:

symbol read true when
¬p not p p is false
p ∧ q p and q both are true
p ∨ q p or q at least one is true (inclusive "or")
p → q if p then q in every case except p true and q false
p ↔ q p if and only if q p and q have the same truth value

The conditional surprises people: "If it rains, the street is wet" is not proven false on a dry, sunny day. It is false only when it rains and the street stays dry.

From p → q come three related statements: converse q → p, inverse ¬p → ¬q, contrapositive ¬q → ¬p. Only the contrapositive always has the same truth value as the original.

Exercises

  1. Write the full truth table for p → q and for ¬p ∨ q. What do you notice?
  2. Statement: "If a shape is a square, then it has four sides." Write its converse, inverse and contrapositive, and mark each true or false.
  3. Let p = "7 is prime" and q = "7 is even". Evaluate: p ∧ q; p ∨ q; p → q; q → p; ¬q → p.
