Seminar · Artistic anatomy: the vertebral column, from Gray 1918

40 min · Method: a first look at Gray’s vertebral column, from Theodora’s public-domain text of Gray 1918. Bartleby no longer serves the page. Official regional lengths remain reserved

Learn

Key terms

vertebral column, from Gray
Definition
Gray 1918, in the public-domain text used here from Theodora, describes the vertebral column as formed of 33 vertebræ: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 4 coccygeal. The vertebræ in the upper three regions remain true, or movable, throughout life. Those of the sacral and coccygeal regions, in the adult, unite to form two bones, the sacrum and the coccyx, and are called false or fixed vertebræ. Gray notes that the number is sometimes increased or diminished, the change being usually in the number of the coccygeal or of the lumbar vertebræ, and that the cervical are rarely more or less than seven. Keep Gray’s spelling vertebræ, and, when the text uses it, flexuous.
Example
Today’s job is this count and the true-or-false division, not a recitation of every process, and not the official regional lengths sometimes given later in Gray. The hyoid, already noted in earlier skull work, is still not one of the 22 skull bones, and is not a vertebra.
Not this
Not a first look at the skull or the mandible, already taught from Gray. Not a requirement to name every process or every curve on this first day. Official lengths such as 71 cm or 61 cm, if they appear in Gray, remain reserved unless a later lesson takes them up with a clear attribution. Bartleby no longer serves this page; the set text is Theodora, https://www.theodora.com/anatomy/the_vertebral_column.html.

Vocabulary review

15–20 min · Spaced review of weeks 1–21 terms · Use your own words; then check the key

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the volume of a right conical frustum, and why is “πR²h − πr²h” a miss when the small cone is similar to the large one?
  2. The volume is the large cone minus the similar small cone, which also equals (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²). A classroom frustum from a large cone of radius 6 and height 8, cut at height 4, has small radius 3 and volume 84π cubic units.
  3. πR²h − πr²h would be the difference of two cylinders, not of two cones, and it would also use the frustum height as if it were each cone’s height.
  4. A near-miss is to drop the 1/3, or to use only the two base areas without the middle term Rr.

Together

In your own words, what is OpenStax Table 14.2’s Lv of water at 37.0 °C, and why is it larger than last week’s figure at 100.0 °C?

Hint 1: The footnote

Lv = 2430 kJ/kg, or 580 kcal/kg, at 37.0 °C, from footnotes 4 and 5 of OpenStax College Physics 2e, Table 14.2. That 37.0 °C is the footnote’s named temperature, not a living person’s measured temperature.

Hint 2: The contrast

At the normal boiling point, 100.0 °C, the same table lists 2256 kJ/kg. More energy is required to evaporate water below the boiling point than at it, in this table. Harvey’s 0 °C ice figures remain a separate table, and 2834 − 333.4 is still not an official boiling or 37.0 °C figure.
