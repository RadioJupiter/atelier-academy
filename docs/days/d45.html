Example
(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)); (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x; x² + y² = r²; substitute a line to count meetings.
Not this
Not a new theorem day, and not a score. It is a mix of what Days 41–44 taught.
Connects to

This Friday mixes the week: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)); order matters; (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x; a circle (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²; and a line substituted into that equation. No new theorem is named today. Invented classroom functions and circles stay labeled.

Remember

Apply g first in f ∘ g. Composition with an inverse gives the identity. x² + y² = r². Substitute a line to count meetings.

Key terms: Friday mix (composition and a circle).

Watch me

With f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x − 3, what is (f ∘ g)(4), and does (0, 2) lie on x² + y² = 4?

  1. (f ∘ g)(x) = 2x − 5, so (f ∘ g)(4) = 3.
  2. 0 + 4 = 4, so (0, 2) is on the circle.
  3. The first is composition; the second is the circle equation. Both were taught this week.

Not this

This Friday mixes the week’s first look at circular motion. The velocity is along the tangent. A centripetal force is toward the center and is needed to keep the path turning. Elective · Artistic anatomy: the hand (carpals, metacarpals, phalanges)

30 min · Method: three named groups after the already-taught forearm, without an invented official bone count

Learn

Key terms

the hand, in three parts
Definition
A first naming of the hand after last week’s arm (humerus, radius, and ulna): the wrist bones (the carpals), the palm bones (the metacarpals), and the finger bones (the phalanges). Today teaches the three groups by place and name, without inventing a bone-by-bone count as a measured classroom fact.
Example
The carpals sit at the wrist, after the radius and the ulna; the metacarpals run through the palm; the phalanges are the bones of the fingers and the thumb.
Not this
Not a demand to memorize a catalog of every named carpal today, and not an invented count treated as if we had counted a skeleton in the room. A later, sourced sitting may add a count or a named list.
Connects to
Day 35’s arm (humerus, radius, ulna), and earlier anatomy of the skeleton, muscles, and the torso. The hand is the next honest part after the forearm.

Friday of week 7 named the humerus, the radius, and the ulna. Today the arm continues into the hand, in three groups, not as a rushed catalog.

Three parts. The carpals are the wrist bones, after the radius and the ulna. The metacarpals are the palm bones, one running toward each finger and the thumb. The phalanges are the finger and thumb bones. You can feel, carefully, that the palm is not “empty,” and that the fingers have joints; this lesson still does not invent a counted official total as if we had taken a skeleton inventory in the room. A later, sourced sitting may give a count.

One honest drawing. A simple outline of the wrist, the palm, and the fingers, with the three group names, is enough. Do not invent a muscle list for the hand today. Do not treat a diagram in another book as today’s source unless it is named and in front of you; this lesson’s source is the already-taught arm, plus these three standard group names, taught as names of places, not as a counted laboratory result.

The sitting is anatomy, not a score, and not a portrait.

Remember

Carpals at the wrist, metacarpals in the palm, phalanges in the fingers and the thumb. No invented official count today. A simple labeled outline is enough.

Vocabulary review

15–20 min · Spaced review of weeks 1–9 terms · Use your own words; then check the key

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is composition of functions different from multiplication of two functions, and why does order matter?
  2. (f ∘ g)(x) is f(g(x)): g is applied first, then f. It is not f times g.
  3. f ∘ g is not generally the same as g ∘ f; with f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x − 3, the two orders gave 2x − 5 and 2x − 2.
  4. A near-miss is to treat the little circle as a decimal point, or as a times sign, and to say the order cannot matter.

Hint 1: The word

Centripetal means center-seeking: toward the center of the circle.

Hint 2: The tangent

The velocity is along the tangent, a different direction. The two are not the same arrow.
