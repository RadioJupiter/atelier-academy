Latin · Capitulum XII: Friday reading (adjectives, a dative possessor, a comparative, and a fourth-declension noun)

40 minutes · Friday mix of this week’s Latin, in original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • third-declension adjective — Day 66
  • dative of possession — Day 67
  • comparative adjective — Day 68
  • fourth declension — Day 69

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting uses only what Capitulum XII has already taught this week: fortis, -e; the dative of possession; fortior, fortius; and exercitus or metus. The sentences are original, not a new chapter.

Mīles fortis est. Mārcō gladius est. Mīles fortior est. Exercitus magnus est.

Read each sentence, name the new job it uses, and keep agreement honest. Comparatives still use -ior / -ius in the nominative singular. A fourth-declension nominative in -us is not automatically a second-declension noun.

Remember

fortis, -e agrees in a third-declension pattern. A dative possessor goes with est and a nominative thing possessed. fortior / fortius is the comparative. exercitus, exercitūs is fourth declension, not gladius, gladiī.

Hint 2: The form

fortius.

  2. Why is *exercitī the wrong genitive for exercitus?

Mathematics · Friday mix: the Pythagorean theorem and its converse

40 minutes · Friday mix of this week’s geometry, not a new theorem

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Pythagorean theorem — Day 66
  • hypotenuse — Day 66
  • altitude to the hypotenuse — Day 67
  • converse of the Pythagorean theorem — Day 69

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting uses the week’s jobs: the Pythagorean theorem, a similar-triangle proof from an altitude to the hypotenuse, a missing side, and the converse. There is no new theorem today.

In a right triangle, a² + b² = c², with c the hypotenuse. If the three sides of a triangle already satisfy that equality, the converse says the triangle is right-angled opposite c. 3-4-5 is one integer triple, not the only right triangle.

Remember

c² = a² + b² in a right triangle. A missing side is found from squares, not by adding the sides. If a² + b² = c², the triangle is right (the converse). 3-4-5 is not the only example.

Watch me

A right triangle has legs 8 and 15. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17².
  2. The hypotenuse is 17.

Together

A triangle has sides 7, 8, and 10. Is it right-angled by the converse?

Hint 1: The squares

7² + 8² = 49 + 64 = 113, and 10² = 100.

Hint 2: The test

113 is not 100, so the converse does not make it right.

Check

These stay inside this week’s geometry.

  1. A right triangle has hypotenuse 26 and one leg 10. Find the other leg.
  2. Why does the similar-triangle proof begin with an altitude to the hypotenuse, not with a median?

Exercises

  1. Name one integer right triangle that is not 3-4-5, and one right triangle whose hypotenuse is not an integer.
  2. This mix does not begin area of a circle, or trigonometry.

Writing & Rhetoric · Friday mix: the précis

40 minutes · Friday mix of this week’s writing, not a new method

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • précis — Day 66

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting uses the précis, and the distinction among quotation, précis, and loose summary, on the same set passage.

In our own age there has been born the Emperor Justinian, who, taking over the State when it was harassed by disorder, has not only made it greater in extent, but also much more illustrious, by expelling from it those barbarians who had from of old pressed hard upon it, as I have made clear in detail in the Books on the Wars.

Procopius, The Buildings, Book I, chapter 1, section 6, translated by H. B. Dewing, Loeb Classical Library, 1940, as posted at LacusCurtius. The 1940 copyright was not renewed and expired in 1968; the translation is in the public domain. https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html Later sections of this chapter remain reserved for history; today’s writing job is the method of a précis, on this one sentence-group only.

No new extract is added. If a Check item asks for a précis, write in your own words. If it asks for a quotation, keep Dewing’s wording and keep it short.

Remember

A précis is a short, faithful restatement in your own words. A quotation keeps the wording. A loose summary can drop the shape of the passage. The set passage remains Buildings I.1.6.

Watch me

In one sentence, say what a précis is, and what it is not.

  1. A précis is a short restatement of a passage in your own words, keeping the author’s points, order, and proportion.
  2. It is not a quotation, not a loose summary that drops the shape, and not a reaction.

Together

Which job is “Justinian was impressive”?

Hint 1: The points

The sentence does not restate the claims of I.1.6.

Hint 2: The job

It is a reaction, or at best a loose remark, not a précis and not a quotation.

Check

These stay with this week’s method.

  1. Write a two-sentence précis of the set passage.
  2. Quote one short phrase from Dewing, in quotation marks, and name the source in a few words.

Exercises

  1. Why would pasting the whole set passage, even with a source, fail the job if the task asked for a précis?
  2. This mix does not open a later section of the chapter.

Physics · Friday mix: center of mass and stability

40 minutes · Friday mix of this week’s physics, not a new law

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • center of mass — Day 66
  • base of support — Day 68
  • stability (against tipping) — Day 68

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting uses the center of mass and the base-of-support test for tipping. Invented masses, distances, and widths stay labeled invented. Last week’s torque and lever are not retaught as a first look, though the mass × distance balance may be used.

A uniform bar balances at its midpoint. Unequal masses balance closer to the larger mass. An object tips when a vertical line from the center of mass falls outside the base of support.

Remember

The center of mass is the balance point. Tipping is a test of a vertical line against the base of support. Invented numbers stay labeled.

Watch me

A labeled invented bar has 6 at 3 units on the left of the balance point, and 2 on the right. Find the right-hand distance, in this model.

  1. 6 × 3 = 18, so 2 × d = 18 and d = 9 units. Invented.

Together

Why does a lower center of mass, with the same base, make an object harder to tip?

Hint 1: The line

A larger tilt is needed to carry the vertical line over the edge of the base.

Hint 2: The test

Until that line leaves the base, the object does not tip.

Check

These stay with this week’s physics.

  1. A uniform rod is 12 units long. Where is its center of mass?
  2. A block is tilted until the vertical line from the center of mass meets the ground at the edge of the base. What is just about to happen?

Exercises

  1. Why will this mix not invent an official center-of-mass figure for a named building?
  2. This mix does not begin oscillations.

Elective · Anatomy: the arm — humerus, radius, and ulna

30 minutes · Method: three long bones of the arm, on a simple unlabeled diagram, after last week’s leg

Learn

Key terms

humerus
Definition
The bone of the upper arm, between the shoulder and the elbow. It is a single bone, not a pair.
Example
On a simple diagram of the arm, the humerus is the long bone above the elbow.
Not this
Not the radius or the ulna (those are the two bones of the forearm), and not a muscle. The name is the bone’s name, not a word for the whole arm.
Connects to
This Friday anatomy continues last week’s leg (femur, tibia, fibula) with the arm’s three long bones. No identifying person is drawn; a unlabeled classroom diagram of the bones is enough.
radius and ulna
Definition
The two long bones of the forearm, between the elbow and the wrist. The radius lies on the thumb side when the palm faces forward; the ulna lies on the little-finger side and forms the point of the elbow with its upper end (the olecranon, named only as the ulna’s elbow end if a finer label is wanted).
Example
In the same palm-forward view, the radius is the lateral bone (thumb side) and the ulna is the medial bone (little-finger side).
Not this
Not a second humerus, and not a claim that the two forearm bones are interchangeable in position. Crossing of the radius over the ulna when the hand is turned is a later, careful look; today’s first look is the palm-forward position.
Connects to
The pair matches last week’s two lower-leg bones (tibia and fibula) in being two bones below a single upper bone, without forcing a false identity of function.

Last week named the three long bones of the leg: femur, tibia, and fibula. Today names the three long bones of the arm: the humerus in the upper arm, and the radius and ulna in the forearm.

The humerus is a single bone, from the shoulder to the elbow. Below the elbow, two bones run to the wrist. In a palm-forward view, the radius is on the thumb side, and the ulna is on the little-finger side. The ulna also makes the point of the elbow at its upper end. This first look uses that palm-forward position; the way the radius crosses the ulna when the hand is turned is a later job, not today’s.

A simple classroom diagram of the bones is enough. No person is identified, and no muscle count or invented measurement is added. The names are the standard names of the bones, not a quotation from a long anatomical treatise.

Remember

The humerus is the single bone of the upper arm. The radius (thumb side, palm forward) and the ulna (little-finger side; elbow point) are the two bones of the forearm. This is a first look, after last week’s femur, tibia, and fibula. No identifying person, and no invented measurements.

Key terms: humerus, radius and ulna.

Vocabulary review

15–20 min · Spaced review of weeks 1–14 terms · Use your own words; then check the key

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the Pythagorean theorem, and why is “every right triangle is a 3-4-5 triangle” a miss?
  2. In a right triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the two legs: a² + b² = c², with c the side opposite the right angle.
  3. 3-4-5 is one integer example (9 + 16 = 25). 5-12-13 is another, and a triangle with legs 1 and 1 has hypotenuse √2, which is not an integer. The theorem is not a claim that every right triangle is 3-4-5.
  4. A near-miss is to say “the sides add, so 3 + 4 = 7 is the hypotenuse,” or to treat 3-5-6 as right without testing the squares.

Together

In your own words, what is a précis, and how is it different from a quotation?

Hint 1: The précis

A short restatement of a passage in your own words, keeping the author’s points, order, and proportion, without a new opinion.

Hint 2: A quotation

A quotation keeps the author’s (or translator’s) wording, marked as such, and names the source.

Check

  1. State the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, and test a triangle with sides 5, 12, and 13.
  2. What is the dative of possession? Give one original Latin sentence that uses it.
  3. What surface tension does IAPWS R1-76(2014), Table 1, give for ordinary water at 20 °C, and with what uncertainty?
  4. Quote Longfellow’s three lines that begin “And lo! almost where the ascent began,” or quote Dewing’s opening of Buildings I.1.6 through “Justinian.” (Either set text is accepted.)
  5. Give the yo preterite of comer and of vivir, and say why they share an ending that hablar does not use.
  6. What is Darii, in mood letters, and how does its conclusion differ from a swapped “all S are P”?
  7. What is simple interest, in the formula used this week, and why is a classroom rate labeled invented?
  8. Name the humerus, the radius, and the ulna, each in one short phrase.
