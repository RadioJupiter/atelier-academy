Latin · Deponent perfect participles in the absolute

40 min · Method: a deponent perfect participle in the absolute remains active in meaning, as deponents do

Learn

Key terms

deponent perfect participle in the absolute
Definition
A deponent verb, already taught in the third person, the infinitive, and the present participle, also has a perfect participle. In the absolute, that participle stays active in meaning, as deponents do: hīs locūtīs, magister tacet means “with these things having been said,” in the sense that someone has spoken, not “having been spoken” as a passive of an ordinary verb would.
Example
Puerō locūtō, magister respondet: with the boy having spoken, the schoolmaster answers. Hostibus secūtīs, agricolae fugiunt: with the enemies having followed, the farmers flee.
Not this
Not a claim that the form looks different from an ordinary perfect participle; the form still looks passive. The translation is active because the verb is deponent. Not a first look at first- or second-person deponents, which remain reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s deponent present participle in the absolute, and yesterday’s two times of the absolute.

Last week, Puerō loquente, magister tacet used a deponent present participle, still active: “with the boy speaking,” not “being spoken.”

The same holds for the perfect: Puerō locūtō, magister respondet. With the boy having spoken, the schoolmaster answers. Locūtō looks passive in form, as deponents do, and is translated as active.

Classroom sentences only. First- and second-person deponent forms remain reserved.

Mathematics · Geometry LIII: the volume of a composite solid

45 min · Method: the volume of a composite solid is the sum (or difference) of already-taught volumes, with a shared face not counted twice

Learn

Key terms

volume of a composite solid
Definition
A composite solid is made by joining or cutting already-taught solids, such as a cube and a pyramid, or a cone and a hemisphere. Its volume is the sum of the pieces, or a difference if a piece is cut out. A face where two pieces join is inside, and is not a reason to invent a new volume formula.
Example
A classroom composite is a cube of edge 6 with a right square pyramid of height 4 on the top face, invented lengths. The cube has volume 216. The pyramid has base 36 and height 4, so volume (1/3) × 36 × 4 = 48. Together, 264 cubic units.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s pyramid or cone volume, or at the cube as a rectangular prism. Not a measured official monument; the lengths are invented. Not today’s later job, surface area, which must not count a hidden face.
Connects to
Last week’s V = (1/3)Bh and V = (1/3)πr²h, and the prism and sphere volumes already taught.

Last week’s solids can be stacked or cut. The volume does not need a new formula: add, or subtract, the volumes you already have.

Cube of edge 6: volume 6 × 6 × 6 = 216. Pyramid on the top, same base 6 by 6, height 4: V = (1/3) × 36 × 4 = 48. Together, 264 cubic units. The square where they join is inside; it does not change the volume.

A second classroom case, still invented: a cone of radius 3 and height 4, volume (1/3)π × 9 × 4 = 12π, with a hemisphere of radius 3 on the base. Last week’s sphere of radius 3 had volume 36π, so a hemisphere is 18π. Together, 30π cubic units, left in terms of π.

Do not invent an official monument’s volume from a photograph.

Remember

A composite’s volume is the sum or difference of already-taught volumes. A joined face is inside and does not change the volume. Classroom lengths are invented. Exact π answers stay in terms of π.

Writing & Rhetoric · A précis of Dewing I.1.64–66

40 min · Method: a précis, already taught, of Dewing, Buildings I.1.64–66. History itself is Monday and Wednesday; today is the précis

Learn

Key terms

précis of Dewing I.1.64–66
Definition
A précis, already taught, is a short restatement in your own words that keeps the author’s points, order, and proportion. Dewing I.1.64–66 says it is impossible to give a precise account of all the treasures Justinian dedicated, but the reader may judge by a single example: the Inner Sanctuary, where only priests may enter, is embellished with forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver. He then closes this stretch by saying, concisely, that the Great Church was constructed in such a manner by Justinian.
Example
A sound précis will still have the impossibility of a full account, the single example, the Inner Sanctuary, and the forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver, and will not invent a modern conversion of that weight, or a count Dewing does not give.
Not this
Not a first look at the précis, which was taught on Day 15 and continued in weeks 14–19. Not a Franklin reconstruction. British spellings in Dewing, if you quote them, stay inside quotations. The figure forty thousand is Dewing’s, and is quoted or clearly attributed, not turned into a different official modern mass.
Connects to
The précis as a method, and Monday’s history reading of the same lines.

Physics · The speed of the projection, from similar triangles

40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.6 — v = vmax √(1 − x²/X²), the same speed already obtained from energy

Learn

Key terms

speed of an SHM projection
Definition
OpenStax 16.6 compares two similar right triangles in the circular picture, one of displacements and one of velocities, and obtains v = vmax √(1 − x²/X²) for the speed of the projection. This is the same speed already obtained from energy in section 16.5. At x = 0, v = vmax; at x = ±X, v = 0.
Example
Invented classroom case: X = 0.10 m, vmax = 2.0 m/s, at x = 0.06 m, v = 2.0 × √(1 − 0.36) = 2.0 × √0.64 = 1.6 m/s.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s energy equation, or at Monday’s T = 2πX / vmax. Not a new official measurement. The cosine-in-radians form remains reserved. Damped motion remains reserved.
Connects to
Monday’s projection picture, last week’s energy in SHM, and OpenStax 16.6.

Monday’s picture is enough, with similar triangles already taught, to find the speed at a position x, without going through the reserved cosine formula.

OpenStax 16.6 notes that the displacement triangle and the velocity triangle are similar right triangles, and obtains

v = vmax √(1 − x²/X²).

This is the same expression already obtained from energy in section 16.5. At the center, x = 0, v = vmax. At either end, x = ±X, v = 0, which matches last week’s “all PE_el at the ends, all KE at x = 0,” if damping is neglected.

Invented classroom case: X = 0.10 m, vmax = 2.0 m/s, x = 0.06 m. Then x²/X² = 0.36, and v = 2.0 × √0.64 = 1.6 m/s.

History · The eastern arch that hung secure (Dewing I.1.67–73)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.67–73. Anthemius and Isidorus are already-taught names

Learn

Key terms

the eastern arch (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.67–73, the eastern arch had been built up from either side but was not wholly completed in the middle. The piers began to crack and seemed on the point of collapsing. Anthemius and Isidorus, having no hope in their technical skill, went to the Emperor, who commanded them to complete the curve: “For when it rests upon itself,” he said, “it will no longer need the props beneath it.” The artisans did so, “and the whole arch then hung secure, sealing by experiment the truth of his idea.”
Example
Dewing tells the story to “shew” that the Emperor built the church “not with money alone,” but also “with labour of the mind and with the other powers of the soul.” He says that if the story were without witness it would have seemed flattery, but there are many witnesses.
Not this
Not a first look at Anthemius, Isidorus, or Justinian, already taught. Not Monday’s treasures and forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver, except as already-read context. Not a modern official measurement of the crack, which Dewing does not give. The south and north arches in I.1.74–75 remain reserved.
Connects to
Monday’s I.1.64–66, and the writing précis of these lines on Thursday.

“But it was not with money alone that the Emperor built it, but also with labour of the mind and with the other powers of the soul, as I shall straightway shew.”

One of the arches, the one toward the east, “had already been built up from either side, but it had not yet been wholly completed in the middle, and was still waiting.” The piers, “unable to carry the mass which bore down upon them, somehow or other suddenly began to crack, and they seemed on the point of collapsing.”

Anthemius and Isidorus, “terrified at what had happened, carried the matter to the Emperor, having come to have no hope in their technical skill.” The Emperor, “impelled by I know not what, but I suppose by God (for he is not himself a master-builder), commanded them to carry the curve of this arch to its final completion.” “For when it rests upon itself,” he said, “it will no longer need the props beneath it.”

If the story were without witness, Dewing says, it would have seemed “a piece of flattery and altogether incredible”; but there are many witnesses. “So the artisans carried out his instructions, and the whole arch then hung secure, sealing by experiment the truth of his idea.”

Keep “labour” and “shew” inside quotations. Invent no width for the crack. The south and north arches, beginning at I.1.74, wait.

Definition

preterite of dormir
Definition
Dormir, already taught in the present with the o → ue stem change, has a preterite in which the stem vowel becomes u only in the third persons: dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron. The other four forms keep o. The pattern is the same shape as yesterday’s pedir, but the vowels are o and u, not e and i.
Example
Anoche dormí bien. El niño durmió. Ellos durmieron temprano.
Not this
Not a first look at the present of dormir (o → ue), already taught. Not a copy of pedir’s e → i vowels onto this verb. Not a first look at the regular -ir preterite endings. Instruction remains in English.
Connects to
Monday’s preterite of pedir, as the same shape with different vowels, and the present o → ue change (Day 12).

Pedir yesterday changed e to i in pidió and pidieron. Dormir, in the preterite, changes o to u in the same two forms.

Dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron. Only durmió and durmieron have u in the stem.

The present, already taught, is a different pattern: duermo, duermes, duerme, dormimos, dormís, duermen, with o → ue except in nosotros and vosotros. Do not mix the present ue with the preterite u, and do not put i from pedir into dormir.

Word How to say it Meaning
dormí I slept es
durmió he or she slept es
durmieron they slept es

Remember

Dormir in the preterite: dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron. Only the third persons change o to u. Do not copy pedir’s i, and do not use the present ue in the preterite.

Elective · Art history: Dewing’s eastern arch, reread

40 min · Method: a rereading of Dewing I.1.67–73, already read in history, now as an art-historical claim about a work “not with money alone”

Learn

Key terms

the work not with money alone (Dewing)
Definition
Dewing I.1.67–73, already read in history this week, is reread here as a claim about how the church was made: “not with money alone,” but also “with labour of the mind and with the other powers of the soul.” The story of the eastern arch — the cracked piers, the Emperor’s command to complete the curve, the arch that “hung secure” — is the example he uses to “shew” that claim. The rereading keeps his wording and invents no modern measurement or later technical name as if it were in the passage.
Example
A sound rereading will still have the unfinished eastern arch, the cracked piers, the builders’ loss of hope in their skill, the command, “when it rests upon itself,” and the arch hanging secure, and will treat those as Dewing’s way of showing a work of mind, not as a modern engineering report.
Not this
Not a first look at these lines, already read in history, or at Anthemius, Isidorus, and Justinian, already taught. British spellings stay inside quotations. I.1.74–75 remains reserved. Monday’s forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver is already-taught context, not today’s first look.
Connects to
Wednesday’s history reading of I.1.67–73, and last week’s rereading of the meadow of stones and the sight new each time.

History has already read I.1.67–73. Today the same stretch is reread as Dewing’s claim that the church was a work of mind, “not with money alone.”

The example is the eastern arch, unfinished in the middle, the piers cracking, the master-builders having “no hope in their technical skill,” and the Emperor, “not himself a master-builder,” commanding them to complete the curve, “For when it rests upon itself, it will no longer need the props beneath it.” The arch then “hung secure, sealing by experiment the truth of his idea.”

Keep “labour” and “shew” inside quotations. Do not invent a modern official span or a later technical name as if Dewing had used it. The rereading is of his claim and his example, not a replacement of them by a modern report. The south and north arches remain reserved.

The set text remains Dewing, Buildings I.1.67–73, LacusCurtius: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html

Remember

Dewing uses the eastern arch to “shew” that the church was built not with money alone, but also with labor of the mind. Keep his wording, including quoted British spellings. Invent no measurement and no later technical name as if it were in the passage. The lines themselves were already read in history.

Key terms: the work not with money alone (Dewing).
