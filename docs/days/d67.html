Latin · Capitulum XII, part 2: the dative of possession

40 minutes · Method: a dative possessor with est. Instruction in English. Original sentences.

Learn

Key terms

dative of possession
Definition
A use of the dative with a form of esse to say that someone has something: the possessor is in the dative, and the thing possessed is in the nominative. Mārcō gladius est means “Marcus has a sword” (a sword is to Marcus).
Origin
From Latin datīvus, “appropriate for giving,” from datus (the past participle of dō, “to give”) + -īvus.
Example
Mārcō gladius est. Iūliae scūtum est.
Not this
Not a nominative possessor with a made-up verb for “has” in this pattern, and not an accusative of the person who has the thing. Mārcus gladium est would not be this construction.
Connects to
The dative here is a job for the possessor. Origin: English dative is from Latin datīvus, “appropriate for giving,” from datus, the past participle of dō, “to give.”
Word How to say it Meaning
Mārcō gladius est MAHR-koh GLAH-dee-us est Marcus has a sword
Iūliae scūtum est YOO-lee-eye SKOO-tum est Julia has a shield
gladius GLAH-dee-us a sword (nominative singular, masculine)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Latin can say that someone has something by putting the possessor in the dative and using a form of esse. The thing possessed stays in the nominative. Mārcō gladius est is “Marcus has a sword”: a sword is to Marcus.

This is the dative of possession. It is a job for the dative you already use for an indirect object, now with est (or sunt). The person who has the thing is not the nominative subject in this pattern.

Original sentences: Mārcō gladius est. Iūliae scūtum est. Mārcō scūtum nōn est. Iūliae gladius nōn est.

Today does not begin comparatives or the fourth declension. Keep yesterday’s fortis, -e in agreement when you describe a soldier or a shield, but the new job is the dative possessor.

Remember

The possessor is in the dative; the thing possessed is in the nominative; a form of esse completes the sentence. Mārcō gladius est is “Marcus has a sword.” This is not Mārcus gladium est.

Mathematics · Geometry XXX: a similar-triangle proof of the Pythagorean theorem

40 minutes · Method: an altitude to the hypotenuse, AA similarity, then a² + b² = c² again

Learn

Key terms

altitude to the hypotenuse
Definition
The perpendicular from the right angle to the hypotenuse in a right triangle. It meets the hypotenuse at a point that splits the original triangle into two smaller right triangles, each similar to the original triangle and to the other.
Example
In the figure, the altitude from C meets AB at D. Then triangle ACD, triangle CBD, and triangle ABC are similar.
Not this
Not a median (which would only bisect the hypotenuse, and need not be perpendicular), and not an altitude to a leg.
Connects to
Because the triangles are similar, the legs of the original triangle are geometric means, and adding the two resulting equations gives a² + b² = c². This is a second proof of the Pythagorean theorem, using AA similarity from week 13.

Yesterday’s theorem can be proved with similar triangles, the work of week 13.

In a right triangle ABC, with the right angle at C, draw the altitude from C to the hypotenuse, meeting AB at D. Each of the two smaller triangles, ACD and CBD, is a right triangle. Each also shares an acute angle with the original triangle ABC. By AA similarity, already taught, ACD is similar to ABC, and CBD is similar to ABC. The two smaller triangles are therefore similar to each other as well.

From ACD ~ ABC, the corresponding sides are proportional: AC / AB = AD / AC, so AC² = AB · AD. From CBD ~ ABC, BC² = AB · BD. Add these two equalities:

AC² + BC² = AB · AD + AB · BD = AB · (AD + BD) = AB · AB = AB².

If AC = b, BC = a, and AB = c, this is a² + b² = c²: the Pythagorean theorem again, now from similarity, not only from a numerical check of 3-4-5.

The altitude must meet the hypotenuse at a right angle. A line that only bisects AB is a median, and that is a different line unless the triangle is isosceles.

Remember

The altitude from the right angle to the hypotenuse makes two smaller right triangles, each similar to the original by AA. Then a² = c · p and b² = c · q, and a² + b² = c². This is a second proof of yesterday’s theorem, not a new theorem.

Key terms: altitude to the hypotenuse.

Science · Water: surface tension

40 minutes · Method: the surface as a stretched skin, then a sourced IAPWS value at 20 °C

Learn

Key terms

surface tension
Definition
A tension in a liquid surface, arising because the surface behaves as if it were a stretched skin: it tends to contract. It is reported as a force per length, or equivalently as an energy per area, in units such as mN/m (which are the same as mJ/m²).
Example
A small steel needle can rest on still water, even though steel is denser than water, because the surface must be deformed to let it through.
Not this
Not the same quantity as last week’s boiling temperature, and not a claim that the surface is a separate solid film. The surface is still water, with a tension.
Connects to
A sourced value is used when a number is required: IAPWS R1-76(2014), Table 1, gives a recommended surface tension of ordinary water at 20 °C of 72.74 mN/m, with an uncertainty of 0.36 mN/m. A classroom dish or needle is invented and labeled, and is not that official figure.

A liquid surface behaves as if it were stretched. It tends to contract. That tension is surface tension. It is not a second solid skin laid on the water; it is a property of the surface of the liquid itself. The usual unit in the table we shall use is millinewtons per meter (mN/m).

A familiar picture is a small needle, carefully laid on still water, that does not at once sink, even though steel is denser than water. The surface has to be deformed for the needle to pass, and the tension can support that small load. The needle and the dish in a classroom demonstration are ordinary teaching objects, not official instruments.

When a number is required, this lesson uses a named official table, not a remembered textbook figure. The International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam, IAPWS R1-76(2014), Revised Release on Surface Tension of Ordinary Water Substance, Table 1, gives recommended values of the surface tension of ordinary water (the liquid in equilibrium with its vapor). At 20 °C, the recommended surface tension is 72.74 mN/m, with an uncertainty of 0.36 mN/m. Temperatures in that table are on ITS-90, the same scale already used last week for the boiling figure.

The table also lists 75.64 mN/m at 0.01 °C (near the triple point already taught) and 71.98 mN/m at 25 °C. Surface tension of water is a little larger at the lower of those temperatures. This sitting does not invent a value at a temperature the table does not list, and it does not treat the uncertainty as zero.

Remember

Surface tension is a tension in a liquid surface, reported for example in mN/m. For ordinary water at 20 °C, IAPWS R1-76(2014), Table 1, gives 72.74 mN/m, with an uncertainty of 0.36 mN/m. A classroom needle is not that official figure. The surface is not a separate solid film.

Key terms

the panther (Longfellow, Inferno I)
Definition
The first of the three beasts in the set lines of Canto I, in Longfellow’s wording: “A panther light and swift exceedingly, / Which with a spotted skin was covered o’er,” that impedes the speaker on the desert slope and does not move from before his face.

Connects to

Upward I looked, and I beheld its shoulders,
Vested already with that planet’s rays
Which leadeth others right by every road.
Then was the fear a little quieted
That in my heart’s lake had endured throughout
The night, which I had passed so piteously.
And even as he, who, with distressful breath,
Forth issued from the sea upon the shore,
Turns to the water perilous and gazes;
So did my soul, that still was fleeing onward,
Turn itself back to re-behold the pass
Which never yet a living person left.
After my weary body I had rested,
The way resumed I on the desert slope,
So that the firm foot ever was the lower.
And lo! almost where the ascent began,

Atelier · The nose, as a wedge, on the blocked-in head

40 minutes · Method: a wedge of three planes, on the mid-line, in the existing light

Learn

Key terms

the nose, as a wedge
Definition
The nose on the blocked-in head, first drawn as a wedge with a front plane and two side planes, under the brow, on the mid-line, not as a pair of nostrils drawn first.
Example
A first pass sets the wedge under the brow, down to a base level with the bottom of the sockets’ nearest corners, then finds the side planes in the same light as the rest of the head.
Not this
Not a line drawing of two nostrils and a tip, floating off the mid-line, and not a new head. The wedge must share last week’s terminator if it crosses that edge of the light.
Connects to
The mouth will be placed tomorrow from this base. Today the job is the wedge, in proportion and in the same light.

The nose is first a wedge: a front plane and two side planes, under the brow, seated on the mid-line of last week’s head. The base of the wedge sits near the level of the lower sockets, not halfway down the head by guess.

Do not begin with two nostrils and a tip. Those are small forms on the wedge, and they wait until the wedge itself is placed and is sharing the same light as the forehead and the cheeks. If the large terminator of last week crosses the nose, the wedge changes value there, just as a cube would.

Check the mid-line: a nose that drifts off it is not yet this head. Check the width of the base against the sockets; a wedge that is wider than the sockets usually needs to be narrowed before any nostril is drawn.

Remember

The nose is a wedge of three planes on the mid-line, under the brow, in the same light as the head. Nostrils wait. The terminator, if it crosses the wedge, changes its value as it would on any form.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -ir verbs, with the same endings as -er

15–20 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · shared -er / -ir preterite endings

Learn

Key terms

the preterite of regular -ir verbs
Definition
The past-tense forms of a regular -ir verb, such as vivir: viví, viviste, vivió, vivimos, vivisteis, vivieron. In the preterite, regular -ir verbs use the same endings as regular -er verbs.
Example
Ayer viví aquí. El lunes pasado vivieron allí.
Not this
Not a new, different ending-set from comí, comiste, comió…, and not the present (vivo, vives, vive). The shared endings are the point of today’s sitting.
Connects to
comer and vivir take the same preterite endings. The -ar set remains the other pattern, already taught. Instruction in English. Irregular pasts are still not begun.
Word How to say it Meaning
viví bee-BEE I lived
viviste bee-BEES-teh you lived (singular, informal)
vivió bee-BYOH he or she lived
vivimos bee-BEE-mohs we lived
vivieron bee-BYEH-rohn they lived

Regular -ir verbs, in the preterite, use the same endings as regular -er verbs. The model is vivir (to live):

viví, viviste, vivió, vivimos, vivisteis, vivieron.

Compare comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron. The endings -í, -iste, -ió, -imos, -isteis, -ieron are shared. You do not learn a third preterite pattern today. The -ar set (hablé, hablaste, habló…) remains the other pattern.

Original sentences: Ayer viví aquí. Anoche vivió allí. El lunes pasado vivimos aquí. Ayer no viví allí.

Instruction stays in English. Irregular pasts (ser, ir, hacer) are not begun.

Remember

Regular -ir preterite uses the same endings as regular -er: viví, viviste, vivió, vivimos, vivisteis, vivieron, like comí, comiste, comió…. The -ar set is still the other pattern. Instruction in English.

Music theory · A short I–IV–V–I progression, with inversion

30 minutes · Method: tonic, subdominant, and dominant triads, already-taught inversions applied

Learn

Key terms

a I–IV–V–I progression
Definition
A short harmonic progression using the tonic, subdominant, and dominant triads of a key, returning to the tonic: I–IV–V–I. Each triad may be in root position or in an inversion already taught.
Example
In C major: C major, F major, G major, C major. The G major triad may be in first inversion (B in the bass) without changing the fact that it is the dominant triad.
Not this
Not a new kind of cadence name for the whole four-chord pattern, and not a claim that every piece of music uses only these three triads. The authentic cadence already taught is V–I, which this progression contains at the end.
Connects to
Last week’s triad inversion (the third or the fifth in the bass) can be applied to I, IV, or V. Inversion does not change the triad’s name; it changes the bass.

The tonic (I), subdominant (IV), and dominant (V) triads of a key can be used in a short progression that returns home: I–IV–V–I. In C major, that is C major, F major, G major, C major.

Last week taught triad inversion: the third or the fifth may be in the bass, and the triad’s name does not change. So the V chord in this progression may be in root position (G in the bass, in C major) or in first inversion (B in the bass). It is still the dominant triad. The last two chords, V–I, are the authentic cadence already taught.

This is a first short progression, not a claim that music uses only three chords, and not a new cadence name for the whole four-chord pattern. Classroom keys in the exercises are ordinary teaching keys (C major, G major), not a sourced official analysis of a named recording.

Remember

I–IV–V–I uses the tonic, subdominant, and dominant triads, and returns to the tonic. A triad may be inverted without changing its name. The last two chords are an authentic cadence (V–I). Inversion was taught last week.

Together

If the G major triad in that progression is in first inversion, what note is in the bass, and is it still V?

Hint 1: The inversion

First inversion puts the third of the triad in the bass.

Hint 2: The name

The third of G major is B. It is still V.
