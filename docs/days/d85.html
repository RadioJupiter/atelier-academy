Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today is a mix of this week’s Latin, not a new topic. Close the cards and write from what you were taught: the present first- and second-person endings; the full present of sum and of possum; nōs, vōs, nōbīs, vōbīs; licet with a dative and an infinitive; and sī, nisi, at, and antequam.

Keep posse and licet apart. Keep at and sed apart. Original sentences only; the book’s story stays in the book.

Remember

Person is in the ending. Posse is pot- plus esse (t becomes s before s) and takes an infinitive. Licet takes a dative and an infinitive, and names permission, not ability. Sī, nisi, at, and antequam hook clauses.

Watch me

Watch one mixed sentence, then name each tool in it.

  1. Antequam magister venit, nōbīs recitāre licet.
  2. Antequam introduces the time clause. Nōbīs is the dative with licet. Recitāre is the infinitive of what is allowed.
  3. The sentence does not use posse: it is about permission, not ability.

Together

Build one mixed sentence from this week’s tools.

Hint 1: Pick the job

Decide first: condition (sī / nisi), time (antequam), permission (licet), or ability (posse).

Hint 2: The ending

If you write recitāmus, you already have ‘we.’ Add nōs only for contrast.

Check

This Check mirrors only this week’s Capitulum XV work.

  1. Give the four first- and second-person present forms of recitō.
  2. Form possumus from sumus, and write a sentence with it.
  3. Write Latin for “You (plural) are not allowed to go out,” using exīre.
  4. Translate: Nisi tacētis, audīre nōn possum.
  5. Write a two-side contrast with at, and a time clause with antequam.

Physics · Friday mix: Hooke’s law and the mass-spring period

40 min · Friday mix of this week’s spring work, contrasted with last week’s pendulum. No new formula.

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Hooke’s law — Day 81
  • force constant — Day 81
  • period of a mass-spring — Day 83
  • simple harmonic motion — Day 83

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today is a mix of this week’s physics. Close the cards. Use F = −kx, k = |F| / |x|, and T = 2π√(m/k), from OpenStax 16.1 and 16.3. Keep last week’s T = 2π√(L/g) for the simple pendulum, small swing, in its own place. Do not mix OpenStax’s car figures with invented classroom springs.

Remember

F = −kx and T = 2π√(m/k). T depends on m and k, not on amplitude. A small-swing pendulum’s period depends on L and g, not on mass.

Watch me

Watch one mixed invented case.

  1. An invented spring stretches 0.05 m under 10 N, so k = 200 N/m.
  2. A mass of 0.50 kg on that spring has T = 2π√(0.50/200) = 2π√(0.0025) = 0.10π s.
  3. Doubling the amplitude would not change T, according to OpenStax 16.3.

Together

An invented spring has k = 50 N/m and a mass of 0.20 kg. Find T in the form 2π times a square root. Then say what T would not be if this were last week’s pendulum.

Hint 1: The spring

T = 2π√(0.20/50) = 2π√(0.004).

Hint 2: The contrast

A pendulum formula would have used L and g, and would not have used this k.

Check

This Check uses only this week’s spring formulas and last week’s contrast.

  1. An invented stretch of 0.08 m produces a restoring force of size 16 N. Find k.
  2. Find T for m = 0.32 kg and k = 50 N/m, as 2π times a square root.
  3. If k is multiplied by 4 and m is unchanged, what happens to T?
  4. State one contrast between T = 2π√(m/k) and T = 2π√(L/g).
  5. What does OpenStax 16.3 say about amplitude and T?

Elective · Anatomy: the seven tarsal bones, from Gray 1918

40 min · Method: the seven individual tarsal names reserved last week, now taught from Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 1918

Learn

Key terms

the seven tarsal bones
Definition
Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 1918, states: “The tarsal bones are seven in number, viz., the calcaneus, talus, cuboid, navicular, and the first, second, and third cuneiforms.” Last week taught the tarsus as a group and reserved these names; today the names are taught from that sentence, not guessed.
Example
The calcaneus is the heel bone, “the largest of the tarsal bones… situated at the lower and back part of the foot.” The talus (also called the astragalus, the ankle bone, in Gray) is “the second largest,” supporting the tibia above and resting on the calcaneus below.
Not this
Not a first look at the tarsus as a group, or at the metatarsals and phalanges, taught last week. Not a first look at the eight carpal names, which remain reserved. Not an official length of a particular foot.
Connects to
Henry Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 20th ed., ed. Warren H. Lewis, 1918, “6d. The Foot. 1. The Tarsus,” as posted at https://www.bartleby.com/lit-hub/anatomy-of-the-human-body/6d-the-foot-1-the-tarsus/ (public domain in the United States).
calcaneus and talus
Definition
The calcaneus (Gray also gives os calcis) is the largest tarsal bone, at the lower and back part of the foot, “serving to transmit the weight of the body to the ground, and forming a strong lever for the muscles of the calf.” The talus (Gray also gives astragalus, ankle bone) is the second largest, “occupying the middle and upper part of the tarsus, supporting the tibia above, resting upon the calcaneus below, articulating on either side with the malleoli, and in front with the navicular.”
Example
In school language: the calcaneus is the heel; the talus is the ankle bone that sits on the heel and carries the leg from above.
Not this
Not a metatarsal, and not a first look at the tibia as a bone of the leg in a later detailed lesson. Gray’s wording is the source for today’s placements.
Connects to
Gray 1918, same section as the seven-name sentence. Later school names (medial, intermediate, and lateral cuneiform) correspond to Gray’s first, second, and third (he also calls the first the internal cuneiform); this lesson uses Gray’s numbering first and may add those later names as a gloss, not as a second official list invented here.

Last week the tarsus was taught as a group of seven, and the individual names were reserved. Today those names are taken from Gray, 1918, not from memory:

“The tarsal bones are seven in number, viz., the calcaneus, talus, cuboid, navicular, and the first, second, and third cuneiforms.”

Word How to say it Meaning
calcaneus cal-KAY-nee-us the heel bone, the largest tarsal, at the back and below
talus TAY-lus the ankle bone, second largest, above the calcaneus
cuboid KYOO-boyd on the lateral side, in front of the calcaneus
navicular nah-VIK-yoo-ler on the medial side, in front of the talus
cuneiforms KYOO-nee-ih-forms the first, second, and third, in Gray’s numbering, in front of the navicular

Gray’s further placements, used here: the cuboid is “on the lateral side of the foot, in front of the calcaneus, and behind the fourth and fifth metatarsal bones.” The navicular is “at the medial side of the tarsus, between the talus behind and the cuneiform bones in front.” The first cuneiform is the largest of the three, “at the medial side of the foot, between the navicular behind and the base of the first metatarsal in front.” Gray also calls it the internal cuneiform. Later school names — medial, intermediate, and lateral — may be used as a gloss on first, second, and third, not as a rival official list invented in this room.

The eight carpal names remain reserved. No official length is invented.
