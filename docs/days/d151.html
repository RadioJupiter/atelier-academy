Latin · The present subjunctive of sequor, a first look

40 min · Method: sequar, sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur, contrasted with the future sequēris and sequētur; original sentences, not Ørberg; purpose clauses reserved

Learn

Key terms

present subjunctive of sequor
Definition
The present subjunctive of sequor is sequar, sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur. The first-person singular sequar is spelled the same as the already-taught future sequar; the other persons are distinct (sequātur, not sequētur). The forms are taught here by contrast with the future, not yet as a purpose clause.
Example
In an original sentence used only to show the form, Puella eum sequātur can be glossed she follows him, in the present subjunctive. The ending is -ātur, not the future -ētur. The sentence is original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg.
Not this
Not the future sequētur, he will follow, or she will follow, which has ē in the ending. Not secūtus sit, a later perfect subjunctive, which remains reserved. Not the present indicative sequitur. A purpose clause with ut remains reserved.
Connects to
The already-taught future sequar, sequēris, sequētur, and the other tenses of sequor. The present subjunctive of ēgredior waits until tomorrow.

Sequor already has a present indicative sequor, a future sequar, sequēris, sequētur, and the perfect tenses already taught. Today the present subjunctive: sequar, sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur. The first-person singular is spelled sequar, the same as the future; the other persons split. The future has ē: sequēris, sequētur, sequēmur, sequēminī, sequentur. The present subjunctive has ā: sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur. Context, and the vowel of the ending, tell them apart. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the English gloss, not with an unquoted English “boy” or “girl.” A purpose clause with ut, and the present subjunctive of ēgredior, wait until they are taught; ēgredior’s forms wait until tomorrow.

Mathematics · Geometry XCI: the area of a sector in radians, a first look

40 min · Method: Singapore, faithful; A = (1/2)r²θ with θ in radians, shown to agree with the Day 83 degree formula; invented figures, labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

area of a sector in radians
Definition
The area of a sector of a circle of radius r, with central angle θ measured in radians, is A = (1/2)r²θ. This is the same area already taught in degrees as A = (θ°/360) × πr². Because a radian measure satisfies θ = s/r, the same area can also be written A = (1/2)r s, where s is the arc length.
Origin
Sector was already taught on Day 83; today’s first look is the same area with θ in radians, A = (1/2)r²θ, which agrees with the degree formula because θ_rad = θ° × π/180.
Example
On an invented figure, r = 6 and θ = π/3. Then A = (1/2) × 36 × (π/3) = 6π, in square units. The same angle is 60°, and the already-taught degree formula gives (60/360) × π × 36 = 6π, the same area. The figure is invented and labeled invented.
Not this
Not (θ°/360) × πr² applied to a θ that is already in radians, which would treat π/3 as if it were 60 and shrink the area. Not the already-taught arc length s = rθ in place of the area. Not the area of the whole disk, πr², unless θ is 2π.
Connects to
The sector of a circle and its degree formula from Day 83, the radian from Day 141, and the arc s = rθ from Day 142. A circular segment waits until Thursday.

A sector of a circle is already known: the region bounded by two radii and the included arc, with area A = (θ°/360) × πr² when the central angle is measured in degrees. A radian is already known, and so is the arc length s = rθ when θ is in radians. Today the same sector area is written with θ in radians. Because θ° / 360 = (θ° × π/180) / 2π = θ_rad / 2π, the degree formula becomes A = (θ_rad / 2π) × πr² = (1/2)r²θ. That is the formula to keep: A = (1/2)r²θ, with θ in radians. Since s = rθ, it is also A = (1/2)r s. On an invented figure, r = 6 and θ = π/3. Then A = (1/2) × 36 × (π/3) = 6π, in square units. The same angle is 60°, and (60/360) × π × 36 = 6π, so the two formulas agree. The figure is invented and labeled invented. A circular segment waits until Thursday.

Remember

The area of a sector with radius r and central angle θ in radians is A = (1/2)r²θ, or A = (1/2)r s. It is the same area as A = (θ°/360) × πr². Do not put a radian measure into the degree formula as if it were a degree measure.

Writing · A commonplace, a first look, after last week’s confirmation

40 min · Method: amplify a general, already-acknowledged fault of a type; invented type, no living person; not a confirmation or a refutation

Learn

Key terms

commonplace (progymnasmata)
Definition
A commonplace, in this course, is an amplification of a general, already-acknowledged fault of a type, not a trial of a living person, and not a new claim that still has to be confirmed or refuted. It takes a fault already shown to fail and enlarges the reasons why it fails.
Origin
The English name commonplace is a calque of Latin locus commūnis, itself a calque of Ancient Greek κοινὸς τόπος (koinòs tópos), “common place,” as given on Wiktionary.
Example
The invented type used this week is the fault of treating a fable as if it were a record of a historical event. That fault has already been taught: a story can be told often, like “The Crow and the Pitcher,” and still not be history. A commonplace amplifies why that treatment fails, without naming a living person.
Not this
Not a confirmation, which shows why a claim holds, and not a refutation, which shows why a claim does not hold, both already taught. Not an attack on a living person. Not a first look at a new untried claim.
Connects to
Last week’s confirmation, and the earlier refutation, of set claims. The type is invented and labeled invented; no living person is named.

A confirmation, already taught, answers a claim and shows why it holds. A refutation shows why a claim does not hold. A commonplace, in this course, comes after those two jobs. It does not try a new claim, and it does not try a living person. It takes a general fault of a type that has already been acknowledged in this course, and it amplifies the reasons why that fault fails. The invented type used this week is the fault of treating a fable as if it were a record of a historical event. That has already been taught: “The Crow and the Pitcher” can be told often, and it is still not a historical record. A commonplace states the type of fault, enlarges the already-taught reasons, and may contrast the fault with a claim that does hold, such as last week’s invented confirmation that if two sides of a triangle are equal, the angles opposite those sides are equal. Learn

Key terms

ray (optics)
Definition
In OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 25.1, light is modeled as traveling in straight lines called rays. The book says: “The word ray comes from mathematics and here means a straight line that originates at some point.” Light may change direction when it encounters an object or passes from one material to another, “but it then continues in a straight line or as a ray.”
Example
OpenStax 25.1 lists three ways light can travel from a source to another location: directly through empty space, such as from the Sun to Earth; through media such as air and glass; and after being reflected, such as by a mirror. In each case the book models the path as a ray.

Definition

Seminar · Logic: independent events on invented figures, a first look

40 min · Method: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) when A and B are independent; invented fair coin and cube, labeled invented; not conditional probability

Learn

Key terms

independent events (invented figures)
Definition
Two events are independent, in this first look, when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events A and B, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). The figures used here are invented and labeled invented. This is not last week’s conditional probability, and it is not an official probability of a real event.
Example
On an invented fair coin and an invented fair cube, used independently, P(heads) = 1/2 and P(a six) = 1/6, so P(heads and a six) = (1/2) × (1/6) = 1/12. The coin and the cube are invented classroom figures, labeled invented.
Not this
Not last week’s conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B), a different job. Not a claim that two events are independent just because they cannot both happen; that would be a confusion with mutually exclusive events, which remain reserved as a first look. Not an official probability of a real event.
Connects to
Last week’s conditional probability on an invented fair cube. Official probabilities of real events remain reserved.
