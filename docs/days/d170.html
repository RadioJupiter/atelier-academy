Latin · Purpose and result, a tighter mix

40 min · Method: a tighter mix of already-taught purpose and result; no new construction

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a result clause with ut, a first look — Day 166
  • a negative result with ut nōn, a first look — Day 167
  • a result clause with ēgredior, a first look — Day 168

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday tightens Monday’s through Thursday’s work. No new construction. Keep purpose (ut, nē) distinct from result (ut, ut nōn). Keep sequātur distinct from sequētur, and ēgrediātur distinct from ēgrediētur. Original classroom sentences only.

Remember

Purpose marks an intended purpose. Result marks what happens as a result, often with tam, ita, or sic. Negative purpose uses nē; negative result uses ut nōn. The verb is the present subjunctive, not the future.

Watch me

Watch one mixed set, already in the shape of a short examination item, and keep the labels straight.

  1. Puella venit ut eum sequātur is purpose.
  2. Puella tam celeriter venit ut eum sequātur is result.
  3. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur is a negative result, not a nē-clause.

Together

Translate and label Puer sedet nē ēgrediātur and Puer tam lentē surgit ut nōn ēgrediātur.

Hint 1: The first

Nē marks a negative purpose: he sits in order that he not go out.

Hint 2: The second

Ut nōn, with tam, marks a negative result: he rises so slowly that he does not go out.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Translate and label: Puella venit ut eum sequātur. Puella tam celeriter venit ut eum sequātur. Puella manet nē eum sequātur. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur.
  2. Why is nē the wrong negative in the fourth sentence?
  3. Give the present subjunctive and the future, third person singular, of sequor and of ēgredior.

Exercises

  1. Write four original sentences, one for each of the four jobs, using sequor or ēgredior. Translate them.
  2. In one sentence, replace the present subjunctive with the future and say why that form is a miss.

Mathematics · Continuity, a short weekly mix

40 min · Method: a short mix of this week’s continuity; invented functions; no new rule; a formal epsilon argument and a derivative reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • continuous at a point, a first look — Day 166
  • a function not continuous at a point where it is not defined — Day 167

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes the week’s already-taught continuity. No new rule. A formal epsilon argument, and a first look at a derivative, remain reserved. The functions are invented classroom examples, not official data.

Remember

f is continuous at a when f(a) is defined and the limit equals f(a). A function not defined at a is not continuous at a, even when the limit exists.

Watch me

Watch one Friday-style mix.

  1. Invented 2x is continuous at 3.
  2. Invented (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not continuous at 2.
  3. Invented x + 2 is continuous at 2, with value and limit both 4.

Together

On invented p(x) = 3x and q(x) = x − 2, is p continuous at 2? Is p / q continuous at 2?

Hint 1: p

p(2) = 6, and lim p = 6, so p is continuous at 2.

Hint 2: p / q

q(2) = 0, so p / q is not defined at 2, and is not continuous at 2.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key. The functions are invented.

  1. On invented f(x) = 2x, show that f is continuous at 0 and at 3.
  2. On invented f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2), why is f not continuous at 2, and is f continuous at 3?
  3. State the two conditions for continuity at a point.

Exercises

  1. On invented g(x) = x + 1, show that g is continuous at 2.
  2. On invented h(x) = (x² − 9)/(x − 3), is h continuous at 3? At 0?

Seminar · Anatomy: the coccyx, Gray 1918

40 min · Method: Gray 1918 on the coccyx; usually four rudimentary “vertebræ”; first largest, last a nodule; articulates with the sacrum; official lengths reserved; not a diagnosis

Learn

Key terms

the coccyx, Gray 1918
Definition
Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 1918, “The Sacral and Coccygeal Vertebræ,” as posted by Theodora. “The coccyx is usually formed of four rudimentary “vertebræ”; the number may however be increased to five or diminished to three.” “In each of the first three segments may be traced a rudimentary body and articular and transverse processes; the last piece (sometimes the third) is a mere nodule of bone.” “All the segments are destitute of pedicles, “laminæ”, and spinous processes.” “The first is the largest; it resembles the lowest sacral vertebra, and often exists as a separate piece; the last three diminish in size from above downward, and are usually fused with one another.” The base “presents an oval surface for articulation with the sacrum.” “Vertebræ” and “laminæ” stay inside quotations. Official lengths remain reserved. The lesson is not a diagnosis, and no body is described from life. Later details of the cornua and of the muscle attachments remain reserved.
