Latin · The ablative absolute with a perfect participle

40 min · Method: a first look at the perfect-participle ablative absolute (urbe captā). Last week’s present-participle absolute is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

ablative absolute with a perfect participle
Definition
An ablative absolute may be built with a noun and a perfect participle, both ablative. The participle is already complete when the main verb happens: urbe captā, agricolae fugiunt, “with the city having been captured, the farmers flee.” The capturing is over; the fleeing follows.
Example
Opere cōnfectō, puerī lūdunt: with the work finished, the boys play. His dictīs, magister tacet: with these things having been said, the schoolmaster is silent.
Not this
Not last week’s present-participle absolute, which is going on at the same time as the main verb (Magistrō recitante, puerī silent). Not a first look at first- or second-person deponents, which remain reserved. Not a copied sentence from Ørberg.
Connects to
Last week’s present-participle absolute, and the perfect participle as a form already met in earlier reading.

Last week’s ablative absolute used a present participle already taught in Capitulum XIV: Magistrō recitante, puerī silent. The reciting and the silence happen together.

A new kind uses a perfect participle. The noun and the participle are still both ablative, and the phrase is still loosely attached. What is new is the time: the participle is already done when the main verb happens.

Urbe captā, agricolae fugiunt. With the city having been captured, the farmers flee. The city is already taken; then the farmers flee.

Classroom sentences only, not copied from Ørberg. A first- or second-person deponent form is still reserved.

Mathematics · Geometry LI: the surface area of a right pyramid

45 min · Method: the surface area of a right square pyramid is the base plus four triangular faces; the slant height is the altitude of a face, and may be found by Pythagoras

Learn

Key terms

surface area of a right pyramid
Definition
The surface area of a right pyramid is the area of the base plus the areas of the triangular faces. For a right square pyramid, there are four congruent triangular faces. If l is the slant height of a face — the altitude of that triangle from the apex to the base edge — the four faces have total area 2bl, and the whole surface is B + 2bl, where b is a base edge and B = b².
Example
A classroom right square pyramid has base 6 by 6 and face slant height 5, invented lengths. Each face has area (1/2) × 6 × 5 = 15, four faces 60, plus the base 36, so the surface area is 96 square units.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s volume V = (1/3)Bh, which used the perpendicular height, not the slant height. Not the surface area of a cone, which is tomorrow. Classroom lengths are invented and labeled invented.
Connects to
The area of a triangle, already taught, and the Pythagorean theorem, already taught, when the slant height is not given.
slant height of a pyramid face
Definition
The slant height l of a face of a right square pyramid is the altitude of that triangular face, from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge. It is not the perpendicular height h from the apex to the base plane. If h and the half-edge of the base are known, l comes from the Pythagorean theorem: l² = h² + (b/2)².
Example
A classroom pyramid has base 6 by 6 and perpendicular height 4, invented lengths. Then b/2 = 3, and l² = 4² + 3² = 25, so l = 5, the same slant height used in the surface-area example.
Not this
Not last week’s perpendicular height h in V = (1/3)Bh. Using h in place of l would understate the faces.
Connects to
The Pythagorean theorem, already taught on Day 66, and today’s surface area.

Last week’s volume used the perpendicular height from the apex down to the base. Today’s job is the outside: the base and the faces.

A right square pyramid has a square base and four triangular faces. Each face is a triangle whose altitude, measured on the face itself, is the slant height l.

If the base is 6 by 6 and l = 5, invented lengths, each face has area (1/2) × 6 × 5 = 15, and four faces make 60. The base is 36. The surface area is 96 square units.

If l is not given, but h = 4 and the base is 6 by 6, then half the base edge is 3, and l² = 4² + 3² = 25, so l = 5, by the Pythagorean theorem already taught. Do not put the perpendicular height in place of l when you find a face.

Remember

Surface area of a right square pyramid is the base plus four triangular faces. The slant height l is the altitude of a face, not the perpendicular height h. If h and b/2 are known, l² = h² + (b/2)². Classroom lengths are invented.

Key terms: surface area of a right pyramid, slant height of a pyramid face.

Physics · A projection of uniform circular motion is SHM

40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.6 — the projection of uniform circular motion onto a fixed axis is simple harmonic motion, and T = 2πX / vmax recovers last week’s period

Learn

Key terms

projection of uniform circular motion
Definition
OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.6, shows that if a point P moves at constant speed around a circle of radius X, its projection on a fixed axis undergoes simple harmonic motion of amplitude X. The time for one revolution is the period of that SHM: T = 2πX / vmax, where vmax is the constant speed around the circle, which is also the maximum speed of the projection. Combined with last week’s vmax = X√(k/m), this recovers the already-taught period T = 2π√(m/k).
Example
Last week’s invented classroom oscillator had X = 0.10 m and vmax = 2.0 m/s. Then T = 2π(0.10) / 2.0 = π/10 s, or about 0.314 s, and this matches 2π√(m/k) for the invented m = 0.50 kg and k = 200 N/m already used.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s vmax = X√(k/m), or at T = 2π√(m/k), already taught on Day 83. Not a first look at radians, or at the formula x = X cos(ωt), which OpenStax also writes; this course has used degrees only in the circle work, so that cosine form is reserved and is not computed this week. Damped motion (OpenStax 16.7) is also reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s energy in SHM and vmax, Day 83’s period, and OpenStax 16.6, https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-6-uniform-circular-motion-and-simple-harmonic-motion (CC BY).

Last week found vmax from energy. OpenStax 16.6 gives another picture of the same motion.

A point P goes around a circle of radius X at constant speed vmax. Its shadow, or its projection, on a fixed axis moves back and forth through a distance X either side of the center. OpenStax says that this projection is simple harmonic motion.

The period is the time P takes to go once around: the circumference 2πX divided by vmax, so T = 2πX / vmax. Last week, if damping is neglected, vmax = X√(k/m). Substituting that in recovers T = 2π√(m/k), already taught on Day 83.

The invented classroom case already used: k = 200 N/m, m = 0.50 kg, X = 0.10 m, so vmax = 2.0 m/s, and T = 2π(0.10)/2.0 = π/10 s ≈ 0.314 s.

OpenStax also writes x = X cos(ωt), with ω = 2π/T in radians. This course has used degrees only, in the circle work of weeks 16–17, and has not yet taken the radian as a first look. That cosine form is therefore reserved, and is not computed this week.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.6, “Uniform Circular Motion and Simple Harmonic Motion,” https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-6-uniform-circular-motion-and-simple-harmonic-motion (CC BY). Last week’s 0.852 m/s remains the sourced vmax from Example 16.6 in section 16.5; today’s 2.0 m/s is the invented classroom case and is labeled invented. Damped motion, section 16.7, is reserved.

Remember

A projection of uniform circular motion of radius X is SHM of amplitude X. T = 2πX / vmax, and with last week’s vmax = X√(k/m) this is the already-taught T = 2π√(m/k). The cosine-in-radians form is reserved. Classroom numbers are invented except where a sourced OpenStax figure is named.

Key terms

Inner Sanctuary (Dewing)
Definition

Definition

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of pedir, e → i in the third person

40 min · Method: a first look at the stem-changing preterite of an -ir verb: pedir has pidió and pidieron, while pedí, pediste, pedimos, and pedisteis keep e. Instruction remains in English

Learn

Key terms

preterite of pedir
Definition
Pedir, already taught in the present with the e → i stem change, has a preterite in which the stem vowel becomes i only in the third persons: pedí, pediste, pidió, pedimos, pedisteis, pidieron. The other four forms keep e. This is not the present-tense pattern, in which the change happens in all forms except nosotros and vosotros.
Example
Ayer pedí un libro. Ella pidió ayuda. Ellos pidieron la cuenta. The endings are the already-taught regular -ir preterite endings; what is new is the third-person stem.

Elective · Logic: the third figure, and Darapti

40 min · Method: a first look at the third figure, and at Darapti (AAI-3). Last week’s Baroco and the already-taught second figure are contrasts, not first looks

Learn

Key terms

third figure
Definition
In a third-figure syllogism, the middle term is the subject of both premises. That is a different placement from the second figure, already taught, in which the middle term is the predicate of both premises, and from the first figure, already taught, in which the middle term is the subject of the major premise and the predicate of the minor.
Example
All M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P, which is today’s Darapti, is third figure because M is the subject of both premises.
Not this
Not a first look at the first or second figure, or at last week’s Baroco (AOO-2) or at Festino, Cesare, or Camestres already taught. Not a claim that every third-figure mood is taught today; only Darapti is the first mood of this figure in this lesson.
Connects to
The already-taught first and second figures, and today’s Darapti.
Darapti
Definition
Darapti is a third-figure syllogism of the form AAI: a universal affirmative major, a universal affirmative minor, and a particular affirmative conclusion. All M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P. Both premises have M as subject. The conclusion is particular, not universal: “some S are P,” not “all S are P.”
Example
Classroom invented terms, not a claim about a living person: All of today’s classroom poems are short; all of today’s classroom poems are recited; therefore some recited things are short. The conclusion is only “some,” because the premises have not said that everything recited is a poem of today’s class.
Not this
Not last week’s Baroco, which is second figure and whose conclusion is a particular negative. Not a first-figure Barbara, already taught, whose conclusion may be universal affirmative. Not a claim that “all S are P” follows from two “all M” premises.
Connects to
Today’s third figure, and the already-taught A, E, I, and O sentences.

Last week’s Baroco was second figure: the middle term was the predicate of both premises. Today the middle term is the subject of both premises. That placement is the third figure.

The first mood taught in this figure is Darapti, AAI-3: All M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P.

The conclusion is particular. The premises say that the whole of M lies in P, and that the whole of M also lies in S, so the two classes P and S overlap at least in M. They do not say that the whole of S lies in P. “All S are P” is the near-miss.

Classroom invented terms only, not a claim about a living person or a named official set.

Remember

Third figure: the middle term is the subject of both premises. Darapti is AAI-3: All M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P, not all S are P. Last week’s Baroco is a second-figure contrast, not a first look. Classroom terms are invented.
