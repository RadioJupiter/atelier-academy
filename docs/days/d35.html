Learn

Key terms

Friday mix: power and torque
Definition
A Friday mix, not a new topic: compute a power in watts, and a torque in newton-meters, keeping the units distinct.
Example
200 J in 5 s is 40 W. A 3 N force with d⊥ = 0.2 m is 0.6 N·m of torque — not 0.6 J.
Not this
Not treating a watt as an energy, and not calling a torque a joule. The two products (work, torque) use distance differently.
Connects to
Monday and Wednesday’s definitions are enough. Today is retrieval and clean units.

This is a mix of the week, not a new chapter. You may use P = W / t and τ = F × d⊥, and you must keep the watt and the newton-meter in their jobs.

Remember

Power is work over time, in watts. Torque, in this first look, is force times perpendicular distance, in N·m, not joules.

Key terms: Friday mix: power and torque.

Watch me

A 100 J job is done in 4 s, and later a 5 N force acts with d⊥ = 0.3 m. Find the average power and the torque.

  1. P = 100 J / 4 s = 25 W.
  2. τ = 5 N × 0.3 m = 1.5 N·m.
  3. Two different quantities, two units.

Together

Why can the same product-of-units, N·m, name a torque here and also appear in a joule, and still not let you call the torque a joule?

Hint 1: The jobs

The joule is work or energy; torque is a turning measure about a pivot.

Hint 2: The habit

Name the torque in newton-meters, even though the dimensions match.

Elective · Artistic anatomy: the arm (humerus, radius, and ulna)

40 min · Method: three long bones of the arm, named and placed, with a caution about the word radius

Learn

Key terms

humerus, radius, and ulna
Definition
The three long bones of the arm, as named in artistic anatomy: the humerus in the upper arm; the radius and the ulna in the forearm. In the anatomical position (palm forward), the radius is on the thumb side, the ulna on the little-finger side.
Example
A bent elbow is the meeting of the humerus with the two forearm bones. This lesson does not invent lengths, or a counted list of all the hand bones.
Not this
Not the mathematical radius of a circle (a length from a center), though the same English word appears. If a lesson says ‘radius’ without a figure, ask which one is meant.
Connects to
The neck and head studies this week sit above the shoulder; the arm begins at the shoulder and elbow already implied by a shoulder line. Today is a first map, not a full muscle plate.

A first map, for drawing and for naming:

  • Humerus: the long bone of the upper arm, from shoulder to elbow.
  • Ulna: the forearm bone on the little-finger side (in the palm-forward position).
  • Radius: the forearm bone on the thumb side (in that same position). When the palm turns, the radius crosses over the ulna — a fact to name, not a measured angle today.

A word caution. In mathematics this week, radius is a length (and a locus uses a radius). In anatomy, radius is a bone. The two are not the same term, even though they share a spelling.

No invented bone lengths, and no full list of carpal bones, in this lesson.

Remember

Humerus: upper arm. Ulna: little-finger side of the forearm (palm forward). Radius: thumb side. The anatomical radius is not the mathematical radius. No invented lengths.

Key terms: humerus, radius, and ulna.

Learn

Remember

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is a function different from a relation that can send one input to two outputs, and what does f(x) mean?
  2. A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. f(x) means the output of the rule f when the input is x, not “f times x.”
  3. A one-to-many rule is the near-miss: it can be a relation, but it is not a function as taught.
  4. A near-miss is to treat f(4) as multiplication, or to call any graph a function without the one-output test.
