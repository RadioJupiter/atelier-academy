Latin · The first- and second-person present of sequor, a first look

40 min · Method: the reserved present table of sequor, still a deponent, in original sentences

Learn

Key terms

first- and second-person present of sequor
Definition
Sequor is an already-taught deponent, I follow, still looking passive and translated as active. The first- and second-person present forms, reserved until today, are sequor, I follow; sequeris, you (s.) follow; sequimur, we follow; and sequiminī, you (pl.) follow. The already-used third persons remain sequitur and sequuntur.
Example
Ego sequor. Tū sequeris. Nōs sequimur. Puer sequitur.
Not this
Not the pluperfect locūtus eram, and not a new tense. Not a non-deponent present such as dūcō. The first persons of ēgredior wait until tomorrow.
Connects to
The already-taught deponent present, imperfect, perfect, and pluperfect of loquor and cōnor. Sentences are original, not copied from Ørberg.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXI: the unit circle, a first look

40 min · Method: a circle of radius 1; cos θ = x and sin θ = y at the point where the terminal ray meets the circle, in degrees

Learn

Key terms

unit circle
Definition
The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin of the coordinate plane. An angle in standard position is measured from the positive x-axis. If the terminal ray meets the unit circle at the point P(x, y), then, in degrees, cos θ = x and sin θ = y. This agrees, in the first quadrant, with the already-taught ratios in a right triangle: the adjacent side over the hypotenuse is x/1 = x, and the opposite side over the hypotenuse is y/1 = y.
Example
At 0°, P is (1, 0), so cos 0° = 1 and sin 0° = 0. At 90°, P is (0, 1), so cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1.
Not this
Not a circle of some other official radius treated as if it were already 1 without scaling. Not a first look at radians, which remain reserved. Not a replacement of last term’s right-triangle sine and cosine; those still hold in the first quadrant, and the unit circle extends them.
Connects to
Last term’s sine and cosine in a right triangle, in degrees. Tangent on the unit circle, and radians, remain reserved.

Last term, in a right triangle, sin θ was opposite over hypotenuse and cos θ was adjacent over hypotenuse, in degrees. Today a first look at the unit circle, which extends those ratios. The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. An angle θ in standard position is measured from the positive x-axis. The terminal ray meets the circle at a point P(x, y). Then cos θ = x and sin θ = y, because the hypotenuse is the radius 1, the adjacent side (in the first quadrant) is x, and the opposite side is y. At 0°, P is (1, 0): cos 0° = 1, sin 0° = 0. At 90°, P is (0, 1): cos 90° = 0, sin 90° = 1. Radians remain reserved. A first look at 30°, 45°, and 60° waits until later this week. Invent no official radius of a real wheel.

Writing · A narrative, a first look, after last week’s fable

40 min · Method: a short complete action, in order, with who, what, when, and where, and no stated moral

Learn

Key terms

narrative (progymnasmata)
Definition
A narrative, as the next exercise after last week’s fable, is a short account of a complete action, told in order, that makes clear who did what, when, and where. It does not end in a stated moral. The same invented event can be told as a fable (with a moral) or as a narrative (without one).
Example
An invented classroom narrative: At noon, a jar of ink stands open on a desk. A draft slides a loose sheet toward the rim. The sheet is pulled back, and the jar is then closed.
Not this
Not last week’s fable, which ends in a stated moral. Not a long short story, and not a précis of a history chapter. Not a mid-story lecture in place of the action.
Connects to
Last week’s fable (progymnasmata). Chreia and maxim remain reserved.

Last week’s first progymnasma was the fable: a short invented narrative ending in a stated moral. Today the next exercise is the narrative itself. Physics · Intensity of a wave, a first look, from OpenStax 16.11

40 min · Method: I = P/A and E = IAt; official Example 16.9, 700 W/m², E = 5.04 × 10^6 J

Learn

Key terms

intensity of a wave
Definition
In OpenStax 16.11, intensity I is power per unit area, I = P/A, where P is the power carried by the wave through area A. The SI unit is watts per square meter, W/m². A wave’s energy is directly proportional to its amplitude squared. Because power is energy per unit time, E = IAt when I, A, and t are constant.
Example
OpenStax Example 16.9 uses the official average intensity of sunlight on Earth’s surface, about 700 W/m², on a collector of area 0.500 m² in 4.00 h: E = IAt = 5.04 × 10^6 J.
Not this
Not last week’s amplitude itself, which is a displacement, not a power per unit area. Not an official living solar figure other than the book’s 700 W/m² and the book’s 1300 W/m² just above the atmosphere, if that second figure is named as the book’s. Decibels remain reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s superposition, and the already-taught idea that a wave carries a disturbance. Example 16.10, intensity and constructive interference, waits until Wednesday.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.11, “Energy in Waves: Intensity,” is this week’s physics source. Access for free at the OpenStax 16.11 page. All waves carry energy. The energy in a wave is directly proportional to its amplitude squared. Intensity I is power per unit area: I = P/A, in watts per square meter. When I, A, and t are constant, E = IAt, because P = E/t.

Example 16.9, official figures from the book: the average intensity of sunlight on Earth’s surface is about 700 W/m². For a collector of area 0.500 m² in 4.00 h, E = 700 × 0.500 × 4.00 × 3600 = 5.04 × 10^6 J. If the same sunlight is concentrated onto an area 200 times smaller, I′ = 200 × 700 = 1.40 × 10^5 W/m². The book also states that infrared and visible energy from the Sun impinge on Earth at an intensity of 1300 W/m² just above the atmosphere; that is the book’s figure, not a classroom invention. Decibels remain reserved. Invent no official living solar measurement beyond these book figures.

Remember

I = P/A, in W/m². Energy is proportional to amplitude squared. In Example 16.9, E = 5.04 × 10^6 J, and concentrated I′ = 1.40 × 10^5 W/m².

History · Inter Caetera, May 4, 1493: the line of a hundred leagues, a first look

40 min · Method: Davenport’s 1917 translation of the May 4 bull; the pole-to-pole line, not a modern official length

Learn

Key terms

Inter Caetera (May 4, 1493)
Definition
Inter Caetera of May 4, 1493, is a second bull of Alexander VI, used here in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 English translation, Document 07. Unlike the May 3 bull, it draws a line from the Arctic pole to the Antarctic pole, “distant one hundred leagues towards the west and south from any of the islands commonly known as the Azores and Cape Verde,” and grants islands and mainlands found and to be found towards the west and south of that line. The line is the bull’s wording, not an official modern longitude or an official length in kilometers.
Example
The grant is still to “you and your heirs and successors, kings of Castile and Leon, forever,” but now with that pole-to-pole line and the hundred leagues, which the May 3 text did not draw.
Not this
Not the May 3 Inter Caetera, last week’s document, which granted lands discovered and to be discovered with a proviso about a Christian owner, but without this line. Not the Treaty of Tordesillas, which remains reserved. Not a conversion of a hundred leagues into an official modern measurement.
Connects to
Last week’s May 3 bull. The first-voyage letter remains reserved. The proviso about possession up to the “birthday” from which 1493 begins is Wednesday’s close of the clause.

Last week’s set text was Inter Caetera of May 3, 1493, in Davenport’s 1917 translation. Today is the later bull of the same name, Inter Caetera of May 4, 1493, in the same translator’s Document 07, as posted on Wikisource. The May 3 grant did not draw a line of leagues. This May 4 text does.

give, grant, and assign to you and your heirs and successors, kings of Castile and Leon, forever, together with all their dominions, cities, camps, places, and villages, and all rights, jurisdictions, and appurtenances, all islands and mainlands found and to be found, discovered and to be discovered towards the west and south, by drawing and establishing a line from the Arctic pole, namely the north, to the Antarctic pole, namely the south, no matter whether the said mainlands and islands are found and to be found in the direction of India or towards any other quarter, the said line to be distant one hundred leagues towards the west and south from any of the islands commonly known as the Azores and Cape Verde.

The grant is still forever, to the kings of Castile and León and their heirs. What is new is the line: “from the Arctic pole, namely the north, to the Antarctic pole, namely the south,” “distant one hundred leagues towards the west and south from any of the islands commonly known as the Azores and Cape Verde,” and the lands granted are those “towards the west and south” of that line, found and to be found, “no matter whether the said mainlands and islands are found and to be found in the direction of India or towards any other quarter.” A hundred leagues is the bull’s figure. Do not convert it into an official modern kilometer or an official longitude; those later calculations remain reserved. The Treaty of Tordesillas remains reserved. The first-voyage letter remains reserved. The proviso about possession up to the “birthday” from which 1493 begins waits until Wednesday. Invent no official island count.

Remember

The May 4 bull draws a line from pole to pole, a hundred leagues west and south from any of the Azores and Cape Verde, and grants lands towards the west and south of that line. The May 3 bull did not draw this line. Do not convert a hundred leagues into an official modern measurement.

a mixed earth hue
Definition

Modern language · Spanish: irregular simple future of hacer, decir, and ir, a first look

40 min · Method: the same future endings on changed stems: haré, diré, iré

Learn

Key terms

irregular simple future (hacer, decir, ir)
Definition
Some Spanish verbs keep the same future endings already taught, -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án, but not the whole unchanged infinitive. Today’s first set: hacer → haré, harás, hará, haremos, haréis, harán; decir → diré, dirás, dirá, diremos, diréis, dirán; ir → iré, irás, irá, iremos, iréis, irán. The endings are the same as in hablaré; the stems are not the full infinitives.
Example
Mañana lo haré. Ella lo dirá. Nosotros iremos.
Not this

Seminar · Logic: complementary events, P(not A) = 1 − P(A), a first look

40 min · Method: P(not A) = 1 − P(A) on last week’s invented fair cube and spinner

Learn

Key terms

complementary events
Definition
Two events are complementary when one is exactly the cases in which the other does not happen, and one of the two must happen. If P(A) is the probability of A, then P(not A) = 1 − P(A), provided the outcomes are the same equally likely set already taught, and one of them must happen.
Example
On an invented fair six-faced cube, P(face 4) = 1/6, so P(not 4) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6. P(an even face) = 1/2, so P(not even) = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2.
Not this
Not an official probability of a real event, such as weather. Not a lesson in gambling. Not a claim that a real cube in the room has been tested as fair. Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved.

Last week’s logic lesson treated equally likely outcomes: if n outcomes are equally likely and one must happen, the probability of one named outcome is 1/n. Today a first look at complementary events. Two events are complementary when one is exactly the cases in which the other does not happen, and one of the two must happen. Then P(not A) = 1 − P(A). On an invented fair six-faced cube, P(face 4) = 1/6, so P(not 4) = 5/6. The even faces are 2, 4, and 6, so P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2, and P(not even) = 1/2. On an invented fair eight-sector spinner, P(one named sector) = 1/8, so P(not that sector) = 7/8. If two named sectors are taken together, P(that pair) = 2/8 = 1/4, and P(not that pair) = 3/4. Do not invent an official probability for the weather, or for a living person’s future. This is not a gambling lesson. Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved.

Remember
