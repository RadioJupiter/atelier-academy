Latin · Capitulum XIII: counting inclusively backward

25 minutes · Method: inclusive backward count from the next named day, after Allen and Greenough §631.d

Learn

Key terms

inclusive counting in Roman dates
Definition
Days other than the named day and the day before it are counted backward from the next Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs, and the starting point is counted as one of the days.
Example
The fourth day before the Ides of September, counted inclusively, is 10 September, as Allen and Greenough’s example IV. Īd. Sept. shows: 13 + 1 − 4 = 10.
Not this
Not ordinary English “four days before,” which would not count the Ides themselves as day 1, and not prīdiē, which is only the day immediately before.
Connects to
Allen and Greenough §631.d. The worked examples used in this lesson are the grammar’s own: IV. Nōn. Mār. = 4 March, and IV. Īd. Sept. = 10 September.

Other days of the month are counted backward from the next Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs, and the count is inclusive: the named day itself is counted as one of the days. That is why the day immediately before is not called “the second day before,” but prīdiē. The next day back is the third day before, and so on.

Allen and Greenough §631.d gives a rule and two examples this lesson uses as set checks, not as a table to memorize in full:

  • For Nones or Ides: add one to the numbered day on which they fall, then subtract the Roman day-number in the date. IV. Nōn. Mār. (the fourth day before the Nones of March): the Nones of March are the 7th, so 7 + 1 − 4 = 4 March.
  • IV. Īd. Sept. (the fourth day before the Ides of September): the Ides of September are the 13th, so 13 + 1 − 4 = 10 September.

In words, that September date may be written ante diem quartum Īdūs Septembrēs. The named day in this phrase is accusative, as it was after prīdiē. This lesson does not dump every cell of the calendar table; it teaches the inclusive method on the grammar’s own examples.

Word How to say it Meaning
ante diem quartum Īdūs Septembrēs AN-teh DI-em KWAR-tum EE-doos sep-TEM-bres on the 4th day before the Ides of September (10 September, inclusive)
ante diem quartum Nonās Mārtiās AN-teh DI-em KWAR-tum NO-nahs MAR-ti-ahs on the 4th day before the Nones of March (4 March, inclusive)

Remember

Days other than the named day and prīdiē are counted backward inclusively from the next Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs. For Nones or Ides, add one to the day they fall on, then subtract the Roman number. IV. Nōn. Mār. = 4 March; IV. Īd. Sept. = 10 September.

Key terms: inclusive counting in Roman dates.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXIII: an isosceles triangle, its height, and its area

40 minutes · Method: the altitude to the base bisects the base; find h from a² + b² = c²; then ½bh

Learn

Key terms

height of an isosceles triangle
Definition
In an isosceles triangle the altitude to the base meets the base at its midpoint and is perpendicular to it. That altitude can be found by the Pythagorean theorem, and the area is then ½ × base × that height.
Example
A triangle with sides 5, 5, and base 6 has the base split into 3 and 3. Then 3² + h² = 5², so h² = 16, h = 4 (the positive length), and the area is ½ × 6 × 4 = 12.
Not this
Not treating a slanted equal side as the height, and not splitting the base into unequal parts when the two sides are equal.
Connects to
The midpoint split uses the equal sides; the height uses last week’s theorem; the area uses Monday’s ½bh.

An isosceles triangle has two sides equal. The altitude from the apex to the base meets the base at a right angle and at its midpoint. That splits the isosceles triangle into two right triangles that you can work with.

The sides are 5, 5, and 6. The base 6 is split into 3 and 3. In one of the right triangles, the legs are 3 and h, and the hypotenuse is 5:

3² + h² = 5² 9 + h² = 25 h² = 16 h = 4

(the positive length). Then the area of the whole isosceles triangle is

A = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12.

The equal side 5 is not the height. The lengths are given measures in the figure. A scalene triangle whose height is not a midpoint split is not today’s first method; today’s job is this clean isosceles case.

Remember

In an isosceles triangle, the altitude to the base bisects the base and is perpendicular to it. Find h from the Pythagorean theorem, then A = ½bh. An equal slanted side is not h.

Key terms: height of an isosceles triangle.

Writing & Rhetoric · Ad hominem

40 minutes · Method: answer the claim, not a trait of the person in place of the claim

Learn

Key terms

ad hominem
Definition
A fallacy in which a claim is rejected (or accepted) because of a trait of the person who offers it, instead of an answer to the claim or to the reasons given for it.
Origin
Ellipsis of the Latin expression argumentum ad hominem, “argument at the person.”
Example
An invented example: “You are only a first-year student, so your check that 5² + 12² = 13² cannot be right.” The arithmetic stands or falls by the squares, not by how long the person has studied.
Not this
Not every mention of a person is this fallacy. A question about whether a witness was in a position to see an event can be a relevant challenge to testimony. The flaw is using a personal trait as if it settled a claim that needs a different kind of test.
Connects to
The name is from Latin argumentum ad hominem, “argument at the person.” It is taught here as an English name for this pattern, not as a new Latin construction.

Ad hominem is a fallacy in which a claim is rejected, or accepted, because of a trait of the person who offers it, instead of an answer to the claim or to the reasons given.

An invented example, labeled invented:

“You are only a first-year student, so your check that 5² + 12² = 13² cannot be right.”

The squares either add or they do not (25 + 144 = 169 = 13²). How long the person has studied does not settle the arithmetic.

Not every mention of a person is this fallacy. If someone reports a measurement they could not have seen, asking whether they were in a position to see it can be a relevant challenge to testimony. The flaw is using a personal trait as if it replaced the test the claim actually needs.

The classroom arguments in this week’s writing are invented examples, labeled invented. They are for practicing the method. No contemporary political case is used as the set example. A précis, already taught last week, is not retaught as a first look.

Remember

Ad hominem rejects or accepts a claim because of a trait of the person, instead of answering the claim. A relevant challenge to whether a witness could have seen an event is not automatically this fallacy. The Latin phrase means “at the person”; it is not a new Capitulum XIII construction.

Science · Water: dilute and concentrated solutions

40 minutes · Method: relatively little or much solute in a given amount of solution; no invented official percentages

Learn

Key terms

dilute solution
Definition
A solution in which the sum of the mole fractions of the solutes is small compared with unity, in the IUPAC Gold Book’s wording; in school language, a solution with relatively little solute in a given amount of solution.
Example
An invented comparison, labeled invented: two glasses of the same size, the second with much less salt in the same amount of water, is the more dilute of the two. No official percentage is named.
Not this
Not “containing no solute,” which would not be this week’s solution, and not a named official percentage. Saturated solutions remain reserved.
Connects to
The Gold Book’s solution entry (S05746) uses this name when the sum of the mole fractions of the solutes is small compared with unity. School language about “relatively little solute” is a first reading of that idea, not a second official scale.
concentrated solution
Definition
In this week’s school language, a solution with relatively much solute in a given amount of solution, compared with a more dilute one. No official percentage or molarity is named.
Example
In the same invented two-glass comparison, the glass with more salt in the same amount of water is the more concentrated of the two.
Not this
Not a synonym of “saturated,” which remains reserved, and not an official bank of percentages. “Concentrated” here is comparative: more solute in a given amount of solution than another solution of the same substances.
Connects to
The origin of the English word “concentration” was not used as a card origin, because the Wiktionary entry was too mixed to quote cleanly. The scientific contrast is with dilute, from the Gold Book note.

Yesterday’s names still hold: solute, solvent, solution, dissolution. Today compares two solutions of the same substances.

The IUPAC Gold Book’s solution entry says that when the sum of the mole fractions of the solutes is small compared with unity, the solution is called a dilute solution. In school language, that is a solution with relatively little solute in a given amount of solution.

A concentrated solution, in this week’s school language, has relatively much solute in a given amount of solution, compared with a more dilute one. The lesson does not invent an official percentage, a solubility, or a molarity, and it does not begin “saturated” as a first look (that remains reserved).

An invented comparison, labeled invented: two glasses of equal amount of solution, one with more salt than the other. The first is more concentrated; the second is more dilute. No number of grams is official here.

Definitions in this week’s science follow the IUPAC Gold Book, opened at https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05746.html (solution), https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05744.html (solute), and https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/D01806.html (dissolution). No official percentage, solubility, or molarity figure is invented. Surface tension from last week is not retaught as new. Saturated solutions remain reserved.

Remember

Dilute: relatively little solute in a given amount of solution (the Gold Book’s wording is a small mole-fraction of solute compared with unity). Concentrated, in this week’s language: relatively much solute in that same sense, compared with a more dilute solution. No official percentage is invented. Saturated remains reserved.

Key terms: dilute solution, concentrated solution.

Literature · Inferno I, lines 112–136: “I will be thy guide”

40 minutes · Method: the set Longfellow lines in which Virgilius offers to guide, and the speaker follows

Learn

Key terms

Virgilius as guide (Longfellow, Inferno I)
Definition
In Longfellow’s Inferno I, lines 112–136, Virgilius offers to be the speaker’s guide “through the eternal place,” and at the end “he moved on, and I behind him followed.”
Example
The set lines include: “Thou follow me, and I will be thy guide, / And lead thee hence through the eternal place.”
Not this
Not a claim that Virgilius will also lead the speaker into “his city” above: the same speech says that “through me none come into his city,” and that a soul “more worthy” will be left for that ascent. Those later stages are not begun as a new canticle today.
Connects to

Therefore I think and judge it for thy best
Thou follow me, and I will be thy guide,
And lead thee hence through the eternal place,
Where thou shalt hear the desperate lamentations,
Shalt see the ancient spirits disconsolate,
Who cry out each one for the second death;
And thou shalt see those who contented are
Within the fire, because they hope to come,
Whene’er it may be, to the blessed people;
To whom, then, if thou wishest to ascend,
A soul shall be for that than I more worthy;
With her at my departure I will leave thee;
Because that Emperor, who reigns above,
In that I was rebellious to his law,
Wills that through me none come into his city.
He governs everywhere, and there he reigns;
There is his city and his lofty throne;
O happy he whom thereto he elects!”
And I to him: “Poet, I thee entreat,
By that same God whom thou didst never know,
So that I may escape this woe and worse,
Thou wouldst conduct me there where thou hast said,
That I may see the portal of Saint Peter,
And those thou makest so disconsolate.”
Then he moved on, and I behind him followed.

The poem’s speaker entreats him, “By that same God whom thou didst never know,” to be conducted “there where thou hast said,” to “the portal of Saint Peter” and to “those thou makest so disconsolate.” The canto’s last line, in this translation, is: “Then he moved on, and I behind him followed.”

Atelier · Value on the mouth and the ear

90 minutes · Method: two masses of the mouth, and the ear on the side plane, in the same light

Learn

Key terms

value on the mouth and the ear
Definition
The already-placed mouth, as two masses, and the already-placed ear, on the side plane of the head, receive value from the same light: the groove or turn between the two masses of the mouth is often darker than the masses themselves; the ear, set back on the side plane, is often in half-tone rather than in the full light of the front of the face.
Example
If the light is from the front and slightly above, the upper and lower masses of the mouth each have a small turn, and the ear stays quieter in value than the front plane of the forehead.
Not this
Not a new placement of mouth or ear, not a line-drawing of every tooth or every fold of the ear, and not a full-light ear pasted onto a side plane that is itself in half-tone.
Connects to
Last week placed the mouth as two masses and the ear from the sockets and the nose. Today finishes the week’s value pass on those two forms, before Friday’s critique.

The mouth was placed last week as two masses. Today those masses receive value. The turn or groove between them often goes darker than the masses themselves. A line of teeth, or a fully drawn outline of each lip with no mass, is not the job.

The ear was placed on the side plane. That side plane is often already in half-tone, so the ear is usually quieter in value than the front of the forehead or the front plane of the nose. A fully lit ear pasted onto a turned-away side plane would break the light.

Keep the same light as the sockets and the nose. Do not move the features. Friday will be a critique of the whole pass.

This week is a value pass across the features already placed last week on the blocked-in head. It is not a new medium, not a first head, and not a first feature-pass. No identifying person is used as a model. Weeks 5–8 on light and planes, and last week’s sockets, nose, mouth, and ear, are used, not retaught as first looks.

Remember

The mouth, as two masses, has a darker turn between the masses. The ear, on the side plane, is often in half-tone, quieter than the front of the face. Neither feature is moved, and neither is replaced by a line-drawing of every small part.

Elective · Economics: the amount after simple interest

25 minutes · Method: A = P + I, with I = P × r × t, invented rates labeled

Learn

Key terms

amount after simple interest
Definition
The amount A after simple interest is the original principal plus the interest: A = P + I, where I = P × r × t, on the original principal only.
Example
An invented example, labeled invented: if P = 200, r = 0.05 per year, and t = 3 years, then I = 200 × 0.05 × 3 = 30, and A = 200 + 30 = 230.
Not this
Not compound interest, already taught and not retaught as new, and not an official rate at a named bank. Classroom rates remain invented and labeled invented.
