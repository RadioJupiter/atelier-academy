Latin · The pluperfect of sequor, a first look

40 min · Method: secūtus eram, the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum; original sentences, not Ørberg

Learn

Key terms

pluperfect of sequor
Definition
The pluperfect of sequor uses the already-taught pattern of a deponent pluperfect: the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum. The participle is secūtus. The six forms are secūtus eram, secūtus erās, secūtus erat, secūtī erāmus, secūtī erātis, secūtī erant: I had followed, you had followed, and so on. It is not the present sequor, and it is not locūtus eram, which is the already-taught pluperfect of loquor.
Example
Puer mē secūtus erat, tum tacēbat: he had followed me, then he was silent. The following was already completed before the later past action.
Not this
Not sequor, I follow, or I am following. Not secūtus sum, the perfect, I followed or I have followed. Not locūtus eram, I had spoken.
Connects to
Last week’s present of sequor, and the already-taught pluperfect of loquor, locūtus eram.

Last week the present of sequor was taught: sequor, sequeris, sequitur, sequimur, sequiminī, sequuntur. Today the reserved pluperfect of the same verb. The pattern is already known from locūtus eram: the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum. For sequor the participle is secūtus. The six forms are secūtus eram, I had followed; secūtus erās; secūtus erat; secūtī erāmus; secūtī erātis; secūtī erant. Ego tē secūtus eram, I had followed you, marks the following as already completed before another past moment. Sequor would be the present, and secūtus sum the perfect. Locūtus eram remains I had spoken, not a form of sequor. Ēgressus eram, the pluperfect of ēgredior, waits until tomorrow. Sentences are original, not copied from Ørberg.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXV: related angles on the unit circle, a first look

40 min · Method: sin(180° − θ) = sin θ and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ, read from last week’s unit circle; degrees only

Learn

Key terms

related angles on the unit circle
Definition
On the unit circle already taught, an angle and 180° minus that angle have the same sine and opposite cosines: sin(180° − θ) = sin θ, and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. An angle and 360° minus that angle have opposite sines and the same cosine: sin(360° − θ) = −sin θ, and cos(360° − θ) = cos θ. These are read from the coordinates, still in degrees. Radians remain reserved.
Example
150° is 180° − 30°, so sin 150° = sin 30° = 1/2, and cos 150° = −cos 30° = −√3/2, as last week. 330° is 360° − 30°, so sin 330° = −1/2, and cos 330° = √3/2.
Not this
Not a new radius, and not a claim that the two angles are the same angle. Not a conversion into radians, which remain reserved. Not the law of sines, which waits until later this week.
Connects to
Last week’s unit circle, exact values at 30°, 45°, and 60°, and signs in the four quadrants.

Last week, on the unit circle of radius 1, cos θ was the x-coordinate and sin θ the y-coordinate. The points at 150° and at 330° were already found from 30°. Today those facts are stated as relations that hold for a general angle θ, still in degrees. The point at 180° − θ is in the second quadrant when θ is acute: its y-coordinate is the same as at θ, and its x-coordinate is the opposite, so sin(180° − θ) = sin θ and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. The point at 360° − θ is in the fourth quadrant when θ is acute: sin(360° − θ) = −sin θ and cos(360° − θ) = cos θ. Check with last week’s values: sin 150° = sin 30° = 1/2, cos 150° = −√3/2; sin 330° = −1/2, cos 330° = √3/2. In the same way, 180° − 45° = 135°, so sin 135° = sin 45° = √2/2, and cos 135° = −√2/2. Radians remain reserved. The law of sines waits until later this week. Invent no official radius of a classroom wheel.

Remember

Sin(180° − θ) = sin θ and cos(180° − θ) = −cos θ. Sin(360° − θ) = −sin θ and cos(360° − θ) = cos θ. Still in degrees. Radians remain reserved.

Writing · A chreia, a first look, after last week’s narrative

40 min · Method: a short anecdote that attributes a saying to a named person; set example from Clough’s Plutarch

Learn

Key terms

chreia (progymnasmata)
Definition
A chreia, in this first look, is a short, useful anecdote that attributes a saying, a deed, or both to a named person. It is not a fable, which has a stated moral and often uses animals, and it is not last week’s narrative, which tells who, what, when, and where, in order, without a required famous saying. The set example is from Plutarch’s Life of Alexander, in the 1859 Clough edition of the Dryden translation, as posted on Wikisource.
Example
Alexander finds Diogenes of Sinope “lying along in the sun,” and “when he kindly asked him whether he wanted any thing, ‘Yes,’ said he, ‘I would have you stand from between me and the sun.’ ” That short scene, person and saying together, is a chreia in this sense.
Not this
Not last week’s narrative, which need not end in a famous person’s saying. Not a fable with a stated moral. Not a full eight-part elaboration, which remains reserved. Not an official biography of a living person.
Connects to
Last week’s narrative, and the week-before-last’s fable. A maxim, as a general saying without the anecdote, remains reserved.

Physics · Sound, a first look, from OpenStax 17.1

40 min · Method: sound as a longitudinal pressure wave in air, compressions and rarefactions, from OpenStax 17.1

Learn

Key terms

sound
Definition
In OpenStax 17.1, sound is a disturbance of matter that is transmitted from its source outward. Sound is a wave. In air and most fluids it is a longitudinal pressure wave: compressions, regions of slightly higher pressure, and rarefactions, regions of slightly lower pressure, move out from the source and have the same frequency as the source. Hearing is the perception of sound, as vision is the perception of visible light.
Example
A vibrating string compresses and expands the surrounding air. Those compressions and rarefactions move out as a sound wave of the same frequency as the string.
Not this
Not last week’s intensity I = P/A as if it already named sound, and not a transverse wave in air, which OpenStax sets aside because fluids have almost no shear strength. Sound waves in solids, which can be both transverse and longitudinal, remain beyond this first look. Decibels remain reserved.
Connects to
Last term’s longitudinal wave, and last week’s intensity. OpenStax 17.2, the speed of sound, waits until Wednesday.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 17.1, “Sound,” begins this week’s physics. Access for free at the OpenStax 17.1 page. “The physical phenomenon of sound is defined to be a disturbance of matter that is transmitted from its source outward. Sound is a wave.” Hearing is the perception of sound, “just as vision is the perception of visible light.” In air and most fluids, sound is a longitudinal pressure wave. A vibrating string “transfers energy to the air,” and “a small part of the string’s energy goes into compressing and expanding the surrounding air, creating slightly higher and lower local pressures. These compressions (high pressure regions) and rarefactions (low pressure regions) move out as longitudinal pressure waves having the same frequency as the string.” OpenStax notes that “sound waves in air and most fluids are longitudinal, because fluids have almost no shear strength,” and that “sound waves in solids can be both transverse and longitudinal,” which “are beyond the scope of our discussion.” The amplitude of a sound wave decreases with distance, because the energy is spread over a larger area and is also absorbed. Pressures “vary only slightly from atmospheric for ordinary sounds.” Decibels, and the numerical speed of sound, wait until later this week. Invent no official speed of sound today, and no official speed of light.

Remember

Sound is a disturbance of matter transmitted outward from a source, and is a wave. In air it is a longitudinal pressure wave: compressions and rarefactions of the same frequency as the source. Hearing is the perception of sound. Decibels remain reserved.

History · The Treaty of Tordesillas, June 7, 1494: the line of 370 leagues, a first look

40 min · Method: Davenport 1917, Document 09; 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, not last week’s 100; no official conversion

Learn

Key terms

Treaty of Tordesillas (June 7, 1494)
Definition
The Treaty of Tordesillas, concluded June 7, 1494, is used here in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 English translation, Document 09, as posted on Wikisource. It draws a pole-to-pole line “at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands,” farther west than last week’s Inter Caetera of May 4, which measured one hundred leagues from the Azores and Cape Verde. The 370 leagues are the treaty’s wording, not an official modern length in kilometers, and not an official longitude.
Example
The line is “drawn north and south, from pole to pole, on the said ocean sea, from the Arctic to the Antarctic pole,” at those three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands.
Not this
Not last week’s May 4 bull, which drew a line one hundred leagues west and south from the Azores and Cape Verde. Not an official conversion of 370 leagues into kilometers or into a modern longitude. The first-voyage letter remains reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s Inter Caetera of May 4, 1493. The assignment of the two sides of the line waits until Wednesday.

Last week’s Inter Caetera of May 4, 1493, in Davenport’s translation, drew a pole-to-pole line “distant one hundred leagues towards the west and south from any of the islands commonly known as the Azores and Cape Verde.” Today a later agreement, the Treaty of Tordesillas, concluded June 7, 1494, in the same 1917 translation, Document 09, as posted on Wikisource. The treaty text covenants “that a boundary or straight line be determined and drawn north and south, from pole to pole, on the said ocean sea, from the Arctic to the Antarctic pole. This boundary or line shall be drawn straight, as aforesaid, at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, being calculated by degrees, or by any other manner as may be considered the best and readiest, provided the distance shall be no greater than abovesaid.” The measure is 370 leagues, not last week’s 100, and it is taken from the Cape Verde Islands, not from “the Azores and Cape Verde” together. Do not convert the 370 leagues into an official modern length or longitude. Davenport’s introduction notes that Portugal had found the earlier hundred-league limit too narrow. The assignment of lands east and west of the new line waits until Wednesday. The first-voyage letter remains reserved.

Remember

The Treaty of Tordesillas, June 7, 1494, draws a pole-to-pole line “at a distance of three hundred and seventy leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands.” That is not last week’s hundred leagues, and it is not an official modern longitude.

Key terms

simple conditional of a regular verb
Definition
The simple conditional of a regular Spanish verb keeps the whole infinitive, as last week’s regular future did, and adds the endings -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían. Hablaría, I would speak; comerías, you would eat; viviríamos, we would live. The endings are not the future endings -é, -ás, -á. Irregular conditionals, such as haría and tendría, wait until later this week.

Not this

Seminar · Logic: mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), a first look

40 min · Method: add probabilities only when two events cannot both happen; invented fair cube; overlap left as a miss

Learn

Key terms

mutually exclusive events
Definition
Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen in the same outcome. If they are mutually exclusive, and the outcomes are the equally likely set already taught, then P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). If they can overlap, the simple sum is a miss, because the overlap would be counted twice.
Example
On an invented fair six-faced cube, the face 4 and the face 5 are mutually exclusive, so P(4 or 5) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3. An even face and the face 5 are mutually exclusive, so P(even or 5) = 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3.
Not this
Not a lesson in gambling, and not an official probability of a real event. Not last week’s complementary events as if “or” were the same as “not.” Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved. A full treatment of overlapping events, sometimes called inclusion-exclusion, remains reserved except as a miss: P(even or greater than 3) is not 1/2 + 1/2.

Last week’s logic lesson treated complementary events: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Today a first look at mutually exclusive events, still on invented classroom figures, not as a gambling lesson. Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen in the same outcome. Then P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). On an invented fair six-faced cube, the faces 4 and 5 cannot both occur, so P(4 or 5) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3. An even face and the face 5 are also mutually exclusive, so P(even or 5) = 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3. A miss: an even face (2, 4, 6) and a face greater than 3 (4, 5, 6) overlap at 4 and 6, so they are not mutually exclusive, and P(even or greater than 3) is 4/6 = 2/3, not 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. A full treatment of overlapping events remains reserved; today that sum is only a miss to avoid. Do not invent an official probability of the weather. Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved.

Remember

If A and B cannot both happen, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). On an invented fair cube, P(4 or 5) = 1/3, and P(even or 5) = 2/3. If they overlap, the simple sum is a miss.
