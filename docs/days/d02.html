Latin · Capitulum I, part 2

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. I · natural method

Learn

Key terms

agreement
Definition
The rule that an adjective takes the same gender and number (and, later, the same case) as the noun it describes.
Example
Īnsula magna est: īnsula is feminine, so the adjective is magna, not magnus.
Not this
Agreement is not ‘the adjective that sounds nicest’, and a masculine ending is not a default you can use for every noun.
Connects to
Day 1’s est/sunt already agreed in number with the subject. Today the adjective agrees in gender and number as well.
gender (Latin)
Definition
The class of a Latin noun — masculine, feminine, or neuter — which adjectives and some pronouns must follow. You cannot always guess it from the meaning.
Origin
From Middle English gendre, from Old French, from Latin genus, genere (‘type, kind’). A doublet of genre and genus.
Example
Fluvius (river) is masculine, so ‘large’ is magnus; īnsula (island) is feminine, so magna.
Not this
Grammatical gender is not the same as the sex of a person or animal, and it is not optional.
Connects to
Agreement is the rule; gender is one of the features the rule uses. Capitulum II will add case.

Adjectives in Latin agree with the noun they describe: they take the same gender and number (and, later, the same case). Masculine magnus, feminine magna, neuter magnum; plurals magnī, magnae, magna. You will see this in today's reading; learn the pattern here first.

Mathematics · Geometry I: definitions, axioms and the first proofs

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry (Givental's English translation), Chapter 1

Learn

Key terms

axiom
Definition
A starting statement accepted without proof, so that theorems can be proved from it.
Origin
From Middle French axiome, from Latin axiōma, from Ancient Greek ἀξίωμα, from ἄξιος, literally ‘weighing as much as; of like value’, hence ‘worthy’.
Example
In this treatment: through two distinct points there is exactly one line.
Not this
An axiom is not a theorem, and it is not ‘anything that looks obvious in a drawing’.
Connects to
Yesterday’s bar models were tools for seeing a relationship. An axiom is a starting claim in a proof, not a drawing.
theorem
Definition
A statement that is proved from definitions, axioms, and earlier theorems.
Origin
From Middle French théorème, from Latin theōrēma, from Ancient Greek θεώρημα, from θεωρέω (‘to look at, consider’).
Example
Vertical angles are equal — proved by showing each is the supplement of the same adjacent angle.
Not this
A theorem is not an axiom, and ‘it looks right in the figure’ is not a proof of one.
Connects to
A theorem depends on axioms. The converse of a theorem must be proved separately and may be false.
vertical angles ∠AOB and ∠COD
Definition
A pair of angles whose sides are the extensions of each other’s sides: the opposite angles formed when two lines meet.
Origin
Vertical here comes from Latin vertex (‘highest point, turning point’), used for the shared vertex, not from ‘upright’.
Example
If lines AC and BD meet at O and ∠AOB = 55°, the vertical angle ∠COD is also 55°.
Not this
Vertical angles are not simply ‘any two equal angles’, and they are not the adjacent angles on a straight line.
Connects to
They are a theorem, not an axiom: we prove they are equal from the fact that adjacent angles on a straight line are supplementary.

What geometry is doing. Kiselev's geometry is a chain: a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (axioms), and every other statement (theorem) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each justified by a definition, an axiom or a proved theorem. "It looks right in the figure" is never a justification.

Definitions to learn exactly. - Two angles are adjacent if they share a vertex and one side, and their other sides lie on opposite sides of the common side. - Two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line are supplementary: they add to 180°. - Vertical angles are two angles where the sides of one are the extensions of the sides of the other.

Theorem. Vertical angles are equal. Proof. Let lines AC and BD meet at O. Angles AOB and BOC are adjacent with OA and OC forming a straight line, so ∠AOB + ∠BOC = 180°. Likewise ∠BOC + ∠COD = 180°, since OB and OD form a straight line. Hence ∠AOB = 180° − ∠BOC = ∠COD. ∎

Converse. The converse of "if P then Q" is "if Q then P". It must be proved separately and is often false. Write the converse of every theorem you learn and decide whether it is true.

Remember

Undefined terms, axioms, definitions, then theorems. Every step in a proof needs a reason. Do not assume a picture proves the claim; the picture only helps you see it.

Key terms: axiom, theorem, vertical angles.

Watch me

Lines AC and BD meet at O, and ∠AOB = 55°. Find the vertical angle to ∠AOB, and an adjacent angle that forms a straight line with it. Give a reason for each.

  1. The vertical angle is ∠COD. Vertical angles are equal, so ∠COD = 55°.
  2. ∠AOB and ∠BOC are adjacent with OA and OC on a straight line, so they are supplementary: ∠BOC = 180° − 55° = 125°.
  3. (The other adjacent angle, ∠AOD, is likewise 125°, and the remaining vertical pair is 55° and 55°.)

Together

Hint 1: Converse

"If two angles are equal, then they are vertical."

Hint 2: True or false

False. Two equal angles can be corresponding angles of a parallel and a transversal, or two equal base angles of an isosceles triangle, without being vertical.

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: copywork and copia

45 min · Method: Renaissance grammar-school imitation (copying, analyzing and varying model sentences); Erasmus, De Copia

Learn

Key terms

imitatio
Definition
The Renaissance school practice of first copying an excellent passage exactly, noticing how it is built, then writing a new version that keeps the structure and changes the content.
Origin
Latin imitātiō (‘imitation’), from imitārī (‘to imitate, copy’).
Example
Copy ‘The night was quiet.’ Then write ‘The harbour was still.’ A richer variation keeps a because-clause if the model has one.
Not this
Imitatio is not plagiarism for display, and it is not ‘write whatever you like first’. The copy comes before the variation.
Connects to
The kernel and expansion of Day 1 are still in use: you are now borrowing a structure, not only adding phrases.
copia
Definition
Plenty of expression: many accurate ways of saying the same idea, produced on purpose.
Origin
Latin cōpia (‘abundance, plenty’), from con- + ops (‘wealth, resource’).
Example
The night was quiet. / The harbour was still. / The studio was silent. Three versions of one idea.
Not this
Copia is not padding, and it is not a list of synonyms that do not actually fit the sentence.
Connects to
Imitatio gives you a structure to vary; copia is the plenty you gain by varying it.

Renaissance schools taught writing by imitatio: copy an excellent passage exactly, notice how it is built, then write a new version that keeps the structure but changes the content. Erasmus's De Copia (1512) trains copia: many ways to say one idea. Today you copy, then vary.

Background. Renaissance schools taught writing by imitatio: students copied excellent passages exactly, analyzed how they worked, and then produced their own versions. Erasmus's textbook De Copia (first published in 1512) shows the method at its most extreme: in Book I, chapter 33, he varies one Latin sentence, Tuae literae me magnopere delectarunt ("Your letter pleased me greatly"), in 150 different ways. The goal is copia, a ready abundance of ways to say a thing, so that the writer can choose the best one.

Today's model. Benjamin Franklin describing how he taught himself to write (public domain, from his Autobiography):

About this time I met with an odd volume of the Spectator. It was the third. I had never before seen any of them. I bought it, read it over and over, and was much delighted with it. I thought the writing excellent, and wished, if possible, to imitate it. With this view I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again, by expressing each hinted sentiment at length, and as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that should come to hand. Then I compared my Spectator with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them.

Remember

Copy exactly first (spelling, punctuation, line breaks if asked). Then vary: same skeleton, new nouns and verbs. Do not paraphrase loosely until the copy is accurate.

Key terms: imitatio, copia.

Watch me

Kernel idea: The night was quiet. Copies of structure: The harbour was still. The studio was silent. A richer variation keeps a because-clause if the model had one: The studio was silent, because the street below had emptied.

Hint 2: Example

Science · Phenomenon: the candle under the jar

45 min · Method: Finnish phenomenon-based learning (start from a real phenomenon, cross subject lines) with Russian-olympiad-style estimation

Learn

Key terms

phenomenon
Definition
A real event or appearance that you can observe, and that you then try to explain with a model and a test.
Origin
From Late Latin phaenomenon (‘appearance’), from Ancient Greek φαινόμενον (‘thing appearing to view’), from φαίνω (‘I show’).
Example
A candle under a jar goes out, and water rises into the jar. That is the phenomenon to be explained.
Not this
A phenomenon is not already an explanation. ‘The flame uses up the oxygen, so the water rises a fifth of the way’ is a claim about the phenomenon, and it must be tested.
Connects to
This course starts from a phenomenon and only then uses facts from chemistry and from estimation.
model (scientific)
Definition
A reasoned picture of what is happening that makes a testable prediction. If the evidence disagrees, the model must change.
Origin
From Middle French, ultimately a diminutive of Latin modulus (‘measure, standard’).
Example
The heating-and-cooling model predicts that water rises mostly after the flame goes out, and that more candles give a larger rise in the same jar.
Not this
A model is not a guess you protect from the evidence, and it is not the same as a bar model in mathematics, though both are pictures that can be checked.
Connects to
The oxygen-use story is a model too; the experiment is how you decide between models.

The phenomenon. Stand a short candle in a shallow dish of water, light it, and lower a glass jar over it until the rim is under water. The flame goes out and water rises into the jar. Safety: do this with an adult present, on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can catch fire.

The common explanation is "the flame uses up the oxygen, which is about a fifth of the air, so water rises to replace it." Today you will test that claim.

Facts you need. Dry air is about 21% oxygen by volume. Burning wax (a hydrocarbon) consumes oxygen but produces carbon dioxide and water vapour. For a typical wax molecule, roughly two molecules of CO₂ are produced for every three of O₂ consumed, and much of the water vapour condenses. A candle also goes out long before all the oxygen is gone.

Procedure. 1. Do the experiment three times with one candle. Mark the water level each time with tape. Watch closely: when does the water rise: while the flame burns, or after it goes out? Are there bubbles as the jar goes down? 2. Repeat with two or three candles together (same jar). If the rise were caused only by oxygen use, would more candles make a larger rise or the same rise? 3. Repeat once with the jar first warmed in hot tap water, then dried.

Remember

The candle-under-the-jar phenomenon is not automatically explained by 'oxygen is a fifth of the air'. Burning wax consumes oxygen but produces CO₂ and water; a candle goes out before the oxygen is gone. Watch when the water rises, and test with more candles and a pre-warmed jar. Safety: do this with an adult present, on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can catch fire.

Key terms: phenomenon, model (scientific).

Watch me

Designing the test of the oxygen story, as a model (do the experiment yourself; these are the questions to watch for, not a substitute for your measurements).

  1. If the rise were only oxygen use, it should be about 21% of the jar, and more candles in the same jar should not change the fraction of oxygen, so they should not give a larger rise.
  2. If heating, escape of gas, and cooling dominate, the water should rise mostly after the flame goes out; bubbles may escape as the jar goes down; more candles should give a larger rise; a pre-warmed jar should change the result.
  3. Typical careful results (and studies such as Vera, Rivera & Núñez, 2011) support the heating-and-cooling model, not the 21% oxygen story.

Together

Air at 20 °C is 293 K. If the trapped air were at an average of 350 K when the jar sealed, and then cooled back to 293 K at constant pressure, by what fraction would its volume shrink? (V is proportional to T.)

Hint 1: Ratio

V₂/V₁ = 293/350 ≈ 0.837.

Hint 2: Shrink

The volume shrinks by about 16%, the same size as a typical observed rise, from cooling alone.

Literature · Homer, Iliad, Book 1

45 min · Method: great books read whole, with close reading and written answers · Read Book 1 before this lesson

Learn

Key terms

invocation
Definition
The opening move of an epic in which the poet asks the Muse to sing, and names the subject of the poem.
Origin
From Latin invocātiō, from invocāre (‘to call upon’), from in- + vocāre (‘to call’).
Example
The Iliad begins by asking the Muse to sing the wrath of Achilles.
Not this
An invocation is not a plot summary of the whole war, and it is not the poet speaking only in his own person with no Muse.
Connects to
The subject named in the invocation (wrath, not ‘the fall of Troy’) tells you what the poem is actually about.
in medias res
Definition
‘Into the middle of things’: the story begins not at the first event, but in the midst of an already ongoing action.
Origin
Latin in mediās rēs, ‘into the middle of things’. Horace uses the phrase in the Ars Poetica (line 148).
Example
The Iliad opens in the tenth year of the war, with the quarrel already about to start, not with the apple or the landing at Troy.
Not this
It is not ‘a flashback’, though flashbacks may follow, and it is not a failure to start at the beginning.
Connects to
The invocation names the subject; in medias res chooses the moment at which that subject is already at work.
epithet
Definition
A fixed descriptive phrase attached to a name, used and reused in oral poetry (‘swift-footed Achilles’, ‘rosy-fingered Dawn’).
Origin
From Middle French épithète, from Latin epitheton, from Ancient Greek ἐπίθετον (‘epithet, adjective’), from ἐπιτίθημι (‘to add on’), from ἐπι- (‘in addition’) + τίθημι (‘to put’).
Example
‘Swift-footed Achilles’ keeps the hero identifiable in the spoken line, even when he is sitting still.
Not this
An epithet is not a one-off metaphor invented for a single line, and it is not automatically ‘a personal insult’ (that is a later English sense).
Connects to
Along with the invocation and the simile, it is one of the epic conventions you watch for in Book 1.

Which translation. Use one verse translation consistently: Richmond Lattimore (1951) is the most line-by-line; Robert Fagles (1990) is the most fluent; Emily Wilson (2023) is the most recent and compressed. Samuel Butler's prose translation (1898) is in the public domain and free online.

Epic conventions to watch for. - The invocation: the poet asks the Muse (goddess) to sing, and names the subject in the first line: the wrath of Achilles. - In medias res: the story begins in the middle, in the tenth year of the war. - Epithets: fixed descriptive phrases ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn") that help an oral poet compose and help a listener follow. - Similes: extended comparisons, often taken from nature or ordinary work.

The plot of Book 1. Chryses, a priest of Apollo, offers ransom for his captured daughter; Agamemnon refuses him; Apollo sends a plague on the Greek camp. Forced to return the girl, Agamemnon takes Briseis, Achilles' prize, instead. Achilles withdraws from the fighting and asks his mother, the goddess Thetis, to persuade Zeus to let the Greeks suffer until they honor him. Zeus agrees.

Remember

An epic may open with an invocation (the Muse, and the subject: the wrath of Achilles) and start in medias res (the tenth year of the war). Watch for epithets and similes. Book 1: the quarrel over prizes, the plague, Achilles' withdrawal, Thetis and Zeus. Use one verse translation consistently and cite line numbers.

Atelier · Bargue plate: the block-in

90 min · Method: Bargue–Gérôme Drawing Course, Part I (plates after casts)

Learn

Key terms

block-in
Definition
The first stage of a Bargue copy: the largest shapes, the big angles, and the main proportions, all in light line, with no features yet.
Example
From the viewing point, measure the subject’s greatest height with a pencil at arm’s length, and mark that height lightly on the paper. Then mark the width and the main tilts.
Not this
A block-in is not a finished outline, and it is not the time for eyes, lips, or shading.
Connects to
Sight-size (Day 1) is how you compare those first lengths. Features wait until the large shapes are true.
proportion
Definition
The relation of one length or shape to another, and to the whole, as seen from the viewing point.
Origin
From Old French, from Latin prōportiō, from prō (‘for, according to’) + portiō (‘a share, a part’).
Example
The greatest width of the plate compared with its greatest height is a proportion you mark before any detail.
Not this
Proportion is not ‘making the drawing pretty’, and it is not the same as value (light and dark).
Connects to
The block-in exists in order to get proportions right while the line is still light and easy to change.

The block-in is the first stage of a Bargue copy: the largest shapes, the big angles, and the main proportions, all in light line. You are not drawing features yet. Charles Bargue's Cours de dessin (with Jean-Léon Gérôme, published by Goupil from the late 1860s) trains this by giving you a design to copy sight-size.

The plates. Charles Bargue's Cours de dessin, made with Jean-Léon Gérôme and published by Goupil in Paris from the late 1860s, has 197 lithographs in three parts: plates after plaster casts, plates after master drawings, and figure studies in charcoal. Students copied them before drawing from casts. The plates are in the public domain; scans are available from the Bibliothèque nationale de France (Gallica), and the complete course is reprinted in Gerald Ackerman's 2003 edition.

Choose one of the first plates of Part I: a single feature (an eye, nose, ear or mouth) shown in outline beside a finished version. Print or display it at a known size and fix it upright next to your paper at the same size (sight-size).

The block-in, in order. 1. Mark the top, bottom and the two widest points. Check the overall height-to-width ratio with your measuring stick before anything else. 2. Enclose the shape in an envelope of a few long straight lines. 3. Use the plumb line: what lies directly below what? Mark these alignments. 4. Break the envelope into smaller straight lines. Check each angle against the vertical or horizontal. 5. Check negative shapes (the spaces around and between parts); they are often easier to judge than the forms themselves. 6. Draw lightly. Step back to your marked spot after every few lines.

Remember

Find the tallest height and the widest width first. Block large shapes. Check angles with a pencil. No small features until the big ones sit right.

Key terms: block-in, proportion.

Watch me

  3. Drop a plumb line through a useful point and mark a light vertical on the drawing.
  4. Place the big interlocking shapes against that vertical, still light, and only then look at a smaller angle.

Modern language · Spanish: nouns, gender and articles

25 min

Learn

Key terms

gender (Spanish)
Definition
The class of a Spanish noun, shown by the article: el with many masculine nouns, la with many feminine nouns. The article must match.
Origin
From Middle English gendre, from Old French, from Latin genus, genere (‘type, kind’).
Example
El museo es grande. La casa es grande. The adjective idea is the same; the article changes.
Not this
Gender is not always predictable from the meaning (el lápiz is masculine), and you should not learn a noun as a bare word without its article.
Connects to
Latin gender (this week’s Latin lessons) is a related idea, but the endings and the articles are not the same language. Learn each system on its own.
article
Definition
The small word placed before a noun (el, la; later un, una) that must agree with the noun’s gender and number.
Origin
From Old French, from Latin articulus (‘a joint, a part, the article in grammar’), a diminutive of artus (‘joint’).
Example
La ciudad uses la, not el. El lápiz uses el, not la.
Not this
The article is not optional in these first sentences, and el is not a universal default.
Connects to
Gender is the class; the article is the daily sign of that class. Always learn them together.

Spanish nouns have gender: el with many masculine nouns, la with many feminine nouns. The article must match. Learn a noun with its article, never as a bare word.

Music theory · Pitch, staff and clefs

30 min · Seminar rotation: Music theory (Tuesdays)

Learn

Key terms

pitch
Definition
How high or low a note sounds: the perceived frequency of the sound.
Example
Middle C has a familiar pitch to many musicians; the next C above it is an octave higher.
Not this
Pitch is not the same as volume (how loud the note is), and it is not the name of a clef.
Connects to
The staff and the clefs are written pictures of pitch. Letter names A to G repeat in each octave.
clef
Definition
The sign at the start of a staff that fixes which line is a named pitch: the treble (G) clef or the bass (F) clef in this lesson.
Origin
From Middle French clef, from Latin clāvis (‘a key’).
Example
The treble clef curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F.
Not this
A clef is not a note, and the bass clef’s two dots do not sit around the same line as the treble clef’s curl.
Connects to
Once the clef is set, the staff’s lines and spaces have names. Middle C sits on a ledger line between the two staves.
staff
Definition
The five lines and four spaces on which notes are written.
Origin
From Old English stæf (‘staff, letter of the alphabet’); the musical sense is a later use of the same word.
Example
Without a clef, a staff is only five lines. With a treble clef, the bottom line is E.
Not this
A staff is not a scale, and it is not a bar in a bar model.
Connects to
Pitch is what you hear; the staff, with a clef, is how you write it.

Pitch names. Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in higher and lower octaves. On a keyboard, the white keys carry these names; C is the white key just left of each group of two black keys.

Half and whole steps. A half step (semitone) is the distance between two adjacent keys, black or white. A whole step is two half steps. Between the white keys E–F and B–C there is no black key, so those are half steps; every other pair of adjacent white keys is a whole step.

The staff and clefs. Five lines and four spaces. The treble clef (G clef) curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F; spaces: F A C E. The bass clef (F clef) places F below middle C on the fourth line, between its two dots. Bass lines from the bottom: G B D F A; spaces: A C E G. Middle C sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff, or just above the bass staff.

Remember

Letter names A–G repeat in each octave. A half step is the distance between adjacent keys; a whole step is two half steps. E–F and B–C are half steps on the white keys. Treble lines E G B D F, spaces F A C E. Bass lines G B D F A, spaces A C E G. Middle C sits on a ledger line between the staves.

Key terms: pitch, clef, staff.

Watch me

Naming notes from a clef, as a model.

  1. Treble clef: the curl fixes the second line as G. So line 1 (bottom) is E, line 3 is B, line 5 is F. Space 2 is A, space 4 is E.
  2. Bass clef: the two dots fix the fourth line as F. Line 1 is G, line 4 is F. Space 1 is A, space 3 is E.
  3. If you name a treble line as if it were a bass line, every note will be wrong. Read the clef first.
