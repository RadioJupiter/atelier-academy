Latin · Capitulum I, part 2

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. I · natural method

Learn

Adjectives in Latin **agree** with the noun they describe: they take the same gender and number (and, later, the same case). Masculine *magnus*, feminine *magna*, neuter *magnum*; plurals *magnī*, *magnae*, *magna*. You will see this in today's reading; learn the pattern here first.

Learn

**Review aloud** yesterday's section once. **Read aloud twice** the next third of Capitulum I. **Notice: adjectives agree.** An adjective takes the gender and number of its noun: *fluvius magn**us***, *insula magn**a***, *oppidum magn**um***; plural *fluviī magn**ī***, *insulae magn**ae***, *oppida magn**a***. Questions: **ubi?** (where?), **quid?** (what?), **-ne** (yes/no). Answers use *nōn* and *sed*: *Corsica nōn est fluvius, sed insula.* Always check a claim against the text and the map before writing it.

Remember

*Fluvius magnus*, *īnsula magna*, *oppidum magnum*. The ending of the adjective follows the noun's gender and number.

Watch me

Īnsula magna est. The island is large. Īnsula is feminine, so the adjective is magna, not magnus.

Together

How would you say 'the town is large' in Latin?

Hint 1: Gender *Oppidum* is neuter, so *magnum*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Oppidum magnum est.*

1. Which form of 'large' goes with *fluvius*? 2. Which form goes with *īnsula*?

  2. Answer in Latin with full sentences: (a) Quid est Nīlus? (b) Quid est Corsica? (c) Estne Tusculum oppidum magnum? (d) Ubi est Aegyptus?
  3. Find and correct the one error in each: (a) Sicilia et Sardinia insula sunt. (b) Nīlus fluvius magna est. (c) Oppida parvum sunt.

Mathematics · Geometry I: definitions, axioms and the first proofs

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry (Givental's English translation), Chapter 1

Learn

In Kiselev's geometry, a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (**axioms**), and every other statement (**theorem**) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each with a reason (a definition, an axiom, or a theorem already proved).

Learn

**What geometry is doing.** Kiselev's geometry is a chain: a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (**axioms**), and every other statement (**theorem**) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each justified by a definition, an axiom or a proved theorem. "It looks right in the figure" is never a justification. **Definitions to learn exactly.** - Two angles are **adjacent** if they share a vertex and one side, and their other sides lie on opposite sides of the common side. - Two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line are **supplementary**: they add to 180°. - **Vertical angles** are two angles where the sides of one are the extensions of the sides of the other. **Theorem.** Vertical angles are equal. *Proof.* Let lines AC and BD meet at O. Angles AOB and BOC are adjacent with OA and OC forming a straight line, so ∠AOB + ∠BOC = 180°. Likewise ∠BOC + ∠COD = 180°, since OB and OD form a straight line. Hence ∠AOB = 180° − ∠BOC = ∠COD. ∎ **Converse.** The converse of "if P then Q" is "if Q then P". It must be proved separately and is often false. Write the converse of every theorem you learn and decide whether it is true.

Remember

Undefined terms, axioms, definitions, then theorems. Every step in a proof needs a reason. Do not assume a picture proves the claim; the picture only helps you see it.

Watch me

A first proof-style argument: through two distinct points there is exactly one line (an axiom in this treatment). If two lines share two distinct points, they cannot be different lines, because that would contradict the axiom. So two distinct lines meet in at most one point.

Together

What is the difference between an axiom and a theorem, in one sentence each?

Hint 1: Axiom A statement we accept without proof, as a starting point.
Hint 2: Theorem A statement we prove from axioms, definitions and earlier theorems.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Name two things a step in a proof may use as its reason. 2. Does a careful drawing, by itself, count as a proof?

Exercises

  1. One of the four angles formed by two intersecting lines is 40° greater than another. Find all four angles.
  2. Prove that the bisectors of two adjacent supplementary angles are perpendicular.
  3. Prove that the bisectors of two vertical angles lie on one straight line.
  4. Three lines pass through one point. How many pairs of vertical angles are formed? (Count only angles smaller than 180°.)
  5. State the converse of "vertical angles are equal". Is it true? Give a proof or a counterexample.
  6. Prove: if two angles are equal, their supplements are equal.

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: copywork and copia

45 min · Method: Renaissance grammar-school imitation (copying, analysing and varying model sentences); Erasmus, De Copia

Learn

Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: copy an excellent passage exactly, notice how it is built, then write a new version that keeps the structure but changes the content. Erasmus's *De Copia* (1512) trains **copia**: many ways to say one idea. Today you copy, then vary.

Learn

**Background.** Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: students copied excellent passages exactly, analysed how they worked, and then produced their own versions. Erasmus's textbook *De Copia* (first published in 1512) shows the method at its most extreme: in Book I, chapter 33, he varies one Latin sentence, *Tuae literae me magnopere delectarunt* ("Your letter pleased me greatly"), in 150 different ways. The goal is *copia*, a ready abundance of ways to say a thing, so that the writer can choose the best one. **Today's model.** Benjamin Franklin describing how he taught himself to write (public domain, from his *Autobiography*): > About this time I met with an odd volume of the *Spectator*. It was the third. I had never before seen any of them. I bought it, read it over and over, and was much delighted with it. I thought the writing excellent, and wished, if possible, to imitate it. With this view I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again, by expressing each hinted sentiment at length, and as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that should come to hand. Then I compared my *Spectator* with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them.

Remember

Copy exactly first (spelling, punctuation, line breaks if asked). Then vary: same skeleton, new nouns and verbs. Do not paraphrase loosely until the copy is accurate.

Watch me

Kernel idea: The night was quiet. Copies of structure: The harbour was still. The studio was silent. A richer variation keeps a because-clause if the model had one: The studio was silent, because the street below had emptied.

Together

Take the skeleton The ___ was ___, because ___ and write one new sentence about a workshop.

Hint 1: Fill Choose a subject, an adjective or state, and a reason.
Hint 2: Example *The workshop was warm, because the stove had been lit since dawn.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What do you do first in *imitatio*: copy or vary? 2. What is *copia*, in one sentence?

Exercises

  1. Copy the passage by hand, exactly, including the 18th-century spellings try'd and compleat. Then proofread letter by letter against the original. Count your errors.
  2. Analyse. (a) The first four sentences are short; the fifth is long. What does that change of rhythm do? (b) In the long sentence, list the actions in order. How does Franklin keep a long sentence clear?
  3. Imitate. Write your own sentence with the same structure as the long sentence ("With this view I took …, and, making …, laid …, and then, without …, tried to …, by …"), describing how you would learn a skill of your own choice.
  4. Copia. Write ten different English sentences that mean "Your letter pleased me greatly". Vary vocabulary, voice (active/passive), sentence type (question, exclamation) and figures of speech.

Science · Phenomenon: the candle under the jar

45 min · Method: Finnish phenomenon-based learning (start from a real phenomenon, cross subject lines) with Russian-olympiad-style estimation

The phenomenon. Stand a short candle in a shallow dish of water, light it, and lower a glass jar over it until the rim is under water. The flame goes out and water rises into the jar. Safety: do this with an adult present, on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can catch fire.

The common explanation is "the flame uses up the oxygen, which is about a fifth of the air, so water rises to replace it." Today you will test that claim.

Facts you need. Dry air is about 21% oxygen by volume. Burning wax (a hydrocarbon) consumes oxygen but produces carbon dioxide and water vapour. For a typical wax molecule, roughly two molecules of CO₂ are produced for every three of O₂ consumed, and much of the water vapour condenses. A candle also goes out long before all the oxygen is gone.

Procedure. 1. Do the experiment three times with one candle. Mark the water level each time with tape. Watch closely: when does the water rise: while the flame burns, or after it goes out? Are there bubbles as the jar goes down? 2. Repeat with two or three candles together (same jar). If the rise were caused only by oxygen use, would more candles make a larger rise or the same rise? 3. Repeat once with the jar first warmed in hot tap water, then dried.

Questions

  1. Record your three heights as a fraction of the jar's height. Compare with 21%.
  2. Using the observations from steps 1–3, decide which explanation the evidence supports: (a) oxygen is removed and nothing replaces it; (b) the gas is heated and expands (some escapes as bubbles), then cools and contracts after the flame dies. Give two observations that support your choice.
  3. (Estimation, olympiad style.) Air at 20 °C is 293 K. If the trapped air were at an average of 350 K when the jar sealed, and then cooled back to 293 K at constant pressure, by what fraction would its volume shrink? (Use V ∝ T at constant pressure.)
  4. Write one paragraph, using because, but and so at least once, explaining the phenomenon to someone who believes the oxygen explanation.

Literature · Homer, Iliad, Book 1

45 min · Method: great books read whole, with close reading and written answers · Read Book 1 before this lesson

Which translation. Use one verse translation consistently: Richmond Lattimore (1951) is the most line-by-line; Robert Fagles (1990) is the most fluent; Emily Wilson (2023) is the most recent and compressed. Samuel Butler's prose translation (1898) is in the public domain and free online.

Epic conventions to watch for. - The invocation: the poet asks the Muse (goddess) to sing, and names the subject in the first line: the wrath of Achilles. - In medias res: the story begins in the middle, in the tenth year of the war. - Epithets: fixed descriptive phrases ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn") that help an oral poet compose and help a listener follow. - Similes: extended comparisons, often taken from nature or ordinary work.

The plot of Book 1. Chryses, a priest of Apollo, offers ransom for his captured daughter; Agamemnon refuses him; Apollo sends a plague on the Greek camp. Forced to return the girl, Agamemnon takes Briseis, Achilles' prize, instead. Achilles withdraws from the fighting and asks his mother, the goddess Thetis, to persuade Zeus to let the Greeks suffer until they honour him. Zeus agrees.

Questions (answer in full paragraphs; cite line numbers from your translation)

  1. What is the subject announced in the first line, and why is it a surprising subject for a war poem?
  2. Who is more at fault in the quarrel, Agamemnon or Achilles? Argue one side, then state the strongest point for the other.
  3. Find one simile or one repeated epithet in Book 1. Copy it, and explain what it adds.
  4. How does Book 1 connect the world of the gods with the world of the humans? Give two examples.

Atelier · Bargue plate: the block-in

90 min · Method: Bargue–Gérôme Drawing Course, Part I (plates after casts)

Learn

The **block-in** is the first stage of a Bargue copy: the largest shapes, the big angles, and the main proportions, all in light line. You are not drawing features yet. Charles Bargue's *Cours de dessin* (with Jean-Léon Gérôme, published by Goupil from the late 1860s) trains this by giving you a design to copy sight-size.

Learn

**The plates.** Charles Bargue's *Cours de dessin*, made with Jean-Léon Gérôme and published by Goupil in Paris from the late 1860s, has 197 lithographs in three parts: plates after plaster casts, plates after master drawings, and figure studies in charcoal. Students copied them before drawing from casts. The plates are in the public domain; scans are available from the Bibliothèque nationale de France (Gallica), and the complete course is reprinted in Gerald Ackerman's 2003 edition. **Choose** one of the first plates of Part I: a single feature (an eye, nose, ear or mouth) shown in outline beside a finished version. Print or display it at a known size and fix it upright next to your paper at the same size (sight-size). **The block-in, in order.** 1. Mark the top, bottom and the two widest points. Check the overall height-to-width ratio with your measuring stick before anything else. 2. Enclose the shape in an **envelope** of a few long straight lines. 3. Use the **plumb line**: what lies directly below what? Mark these alignments. 4. Break the envelope into smaller straight lines. Check each **angle** against the vertical or horizontal. 5. Check **negative shapes** (the spaces around and between parts); they are often easier to judge than the forms themselves. 6. Draw lightly. Step back to your marked spot after every few lines.

Remember

Find the tallest height and the widest width first. Block large shapes. Check angles with a pencil. No small features until the big ones sit right.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. From the viewing point, measure the subject's greatest height with a pencil at arm's length, and mark that height lightly on the paper.
  2. Measure the greatest width as a fraction of that height (for example, 'a little more than half') and mark it.
  3. Drop a plumb line through a useful point and mark a light vertical on the drawing.
  4. Place the big interlocking shapes against that vertical, still light, and only then look at a smaller angle.

Together

If a small feature already looks interesting, when may you draw it?

Hint 1: Rule After the large height, width, and main shapes have been checked.
Hint 2: Why A correct nose on a wrong head is still a wrong drawing.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is a block-in for? 2. What two measurements should you take first?

Exercises

  1. Complete the envelope and block-in of your chosen plate in straight lines only (no curves yet). Stop at 75 minutes.
  2. Check (15 min): measure five distances on the plate and the same five on your drawing; record the error as a percentage of the total height. Mark the largest error in the margin for tomorrow.

Modern language · Spanish: nouns, gender and articles

25 min

Learn

Spanish nouns have **gender**: *el* with many masculine nouns, *la* with many feminine nouns. The article must match. Learn a noun with its article, never as a bare word.
WordHow to say itMeaning
el libroel LEE-brohthe book
la casalah KAH-sahthe house
el museoel moo-SEH-ohthe museum
la ciudadlah syoo-DAHDthe city
el lápizel LAH-peeth or LAH-peesthe pencil
la mesalah MEH-sahthe table
unoona (masculine)
unaOO-naha (feminine)

Learn

Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine. Most nouns ending in *-o* are masculine (*el libro*), and most ending in *-a* are feminine (*la mesa*). Nouns ending in *-ción*, *-sión* and *-dad* are feminine (*la canción, la ciudad*). Learn every noun **with its article**, because there are exceptions: *el día, el mapa, el problema, la mano*, and *la foto* (short for *la fotografía*). | | masculine | feminine | |---|---|---| | the (singular) | el | la | | the (plural) | los | las | | a / some | un / unos | una / unas | **Plurals:** add *-s* after a vowel (*libros*); add *-es* after a consonant (*ciudades*); *-z* becomes *-ces* (*el lápiz → los lápices*).

Remember

Learn *el libro*, not just *libro*. *Un* / *una* mean 'a' and follow the same gender. Plural: *los* / *las*, and the noun usually adds *-s* or *-es*.

Watch me

El museo es grande. La casa es grande. Same adjective idea, different article, because museo takes el and casa takes la.

Together

How do you say 'a book' and 'a house'?

Hint 1: Book *un libro* (*el libro* → *un*).
Hint 2: House *una casa*.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Which article goes with *ciudad*? 2. What does *el lápiz* mean?

Exercises

  1. Give el or la: libro, mesa, día, canción, mapa, ciudad, mano, problema, ventana, cuaderno.
  2. Make plural with the article: el lápiz, la ciudad, el papel, la canción, el día.
  3. Translate: a pencil; some windows; the maps; a song; the hand.

Music theory · Pitch, staff and clefs

30 min · Seminar rotation: Music theory (Tuesdays)

Pitch names. Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in higher and lower octaves. On a keyboard, the white keys carry these names; C is the white key just left of each group of two black keys.

Half and whole steps. A half step (semitone) is the distance between two adjacent keys, black or white. A whole step is two half steps. Between the white keys E–F and B–C there is no black key, so those are half steps; every other pair of adjacent white keys is a whole step.

The staff and clefs. Five lines and four spaces. The treble clef (G clef) curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F; spaces: F A C E. The bass clef (F clef) places F below middle C on the fourth line, between its two dots. Bass lines from the bottom: G B D F A; spaces: A C E G. Middle C sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff, or just above the bass staff.

Exercises

  1. Name the treble-clef note on: line 1, line 3, line 5, space 2, space 4.
  2. Name the bass-clef note on: line 1, line 4, space 1, space 3.
  3. Whole step or half step? E–F, F–G, B–C, A–B, C–D.
  4. Starting on D, go up a whole step, then a half step. Which white keys do you land on?
