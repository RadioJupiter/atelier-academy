Latin · The pluperfect of a deponent, a first look

40 min · Method: a first look at locūtus eram, contrasted with the already-taught perfect and imperfect

Learn

Key terms

pluperfect of a deponent
Definition
The pluperfect of a deponent uses the already-taught perfect participle with the imperfect of sum, and it still looks passive and is translated as active: locūtus eram, locūtus erās, locūtus erat, locūtī erāmus, locūtī erātis, locūtī erant. The meaning is I had spoken, you had spoken, and the rest. The action is presented as already completed before another past moment.
Origin
From Latin plus quam perfectum, more than completed.
Example
Ego locūtus eram. Puer locūtus erat. Puerī locūtī erant.
Not this
Not the perfect locūtus sum, I spoke or I have spoken, which uses the same participle with the present of sum. Not the imperfect loquēbar, I was speaking or I used to speak, which has no participle. Not the already-taught absolute puerō locūtō, which has no sum and is not a main-clause tense.
Connects to
The already-taught deponent present, imperfect, and perfect, and the two times of the ablative absolute.

Last term the deponent had three taught tenses: loquor, I speak; loquēbar, I was speaking or I used to speak; and locūtus sum, I spoke or I have spoken. Today a first look at a fourth: the pluperfect. The same participle locūtus is used, but the helping verb is the already-taught imperfect of sum: eram, erās, erat, erāmus, erātis, erant. Locūtus eram means I had spoken: the speaking is already completed before some other past moment. The forms still look passive and are translated as active, as with the earlier deponent tenses.

Word How to say it Meaning

locūtus erat lo-KOO-toos EH-raht he or she had spoken
locūtī erāmus lo-KOO-tee eh-RAH-moos we had spoken
locūtī erātis lo-KOO-tee eh-RAH-tis you (pl.) had spoken

A short original sentence, not copied from Ørberg: Puer locūtus erat, et tum tacēbat. Puer locūtus erat means he had spoken, and then he was silent. The pluperfect marks the speaking as already done before the silence. The first persons of sequor and ēgredior remain reserved.

Remember

The pluperfect of a deponent is the perfect participle plus the imperfect of sum: locūtus eram, I had spoken. Locūtus sum is the perfect. Loquēbar is the imperfect, with no participle.

Watch me

How is locūtus eram formed, and how does it differ from locūtus sum and from loquēbar?

  1. Start from the already-taught participle locūtus, the same one used in locūtus sum.
  2. Replace sum with eram: locūtus eram, I had spoken. The six forms are locūtus eram, locūtus erās, locūtus erat, locūtī erāmus, locūtī erātis, locūtī erant.
  3. Locūtus sum is the perfect: I spoke, or I have spoken. Loquēbar is the imperfect: I was speaking, or I used to speak. It has no participle and no sum.
  4. The absolute puerō locūtō is not a fourth main-clause tense, and it has no sum.

Hint 2: The sentence

Locūtus erat, he had spoken, is already completed before the silence. Locūtus est would be the perfect, he spoke or has spoken, not marked as prior to another past moment.

Mathematics · Geometry LXVII: a line and a plane in space, a first look

40 min · Method: three relations of a line and a plane, shown on an invented classroom box

Learn

Key terms

a line and a plane in space
Definition
In space, a line and a plane have three possible relations: the line may lie in the plane, meet the plane at exactly one point, or be parallel to the plane and never meet it. These are relations in three dimensions, not a new kind of plane drawn as a value plane in a head study.
Example
On an invented classroom box, the front bottom edge AB lies in the floor plane; the vertical edge DA meets the floor at the single point A; the top front edge DC is parallel to the floor and does not meet it.
Not this
Not a line drawn on a sheet of paper as if the paper were the only plane in the world. Not the already-taught value planes of a form in the atelier. Not a claim about an official room measurement; the box is an invented model.
Connects to
Already-taught lines and planes in a plane figure, now extended into space. A line perpendicular to a plane waits until tomorrow.

Term 3 mathematics begins in space. A plane is still flat, as already taught, but now it sits in three dimensions, like the floor of an invented classroom box. A line may lie in that plane, as one of the floor’s own edges does. A line may meet the plane at exactly one point, as a vertical edge meets the floor at a corner. Or a line may be parallel to the plane and never meet it, as a top edge of the box stays a constant height above the floor. The box is an invented model, not a measured room; invent no official length for a wall.

The already-taught tangent of an acute angle, and the reserved first look at the unit circle and at radians, are not today’s job. A line perpendicular to a plane waits until tomorrow.

A line may lie in a plane, meet it at one point, or be parallel to it. The invented box is a model, not a measured room.

Writing · A fable, a first look: Townsend’s Crow and the Pitcher

40 min · Method: a short narrative plus a stated moral, from Townsend 1887

Learn

Key terms

fable (progymnasmata)
Definition
A fable, as the first of the classical progymnasmata, is a short invented narrative, often with animals that act and speak, that ends in a stated moral. The narrative is short and complete; the moral is named after the action, not lectured in the middle of it.
Example
Townsend’s 1887 wording of “The Crow and the Pitcher,” which ends, “Necessity is the mother of invention.”
Not this
Not a long short story, and not a précis of a history chapter. Not a moral announced before any action has happened. Not a new official proverb claimed as a law of nature.
Connects to
Term 3 writing begins with the progymnasmata. Narrative, chreia, and maxim wait until later exercises. Franklin’s method remains available, but it is not today’s first look.

Term 3 writing begins with the classical progymnasmata, a sequence of graded exercises. The first is the fable. A fable is a short invented narrative, often with animals that act, that ends in a stated moral. Today the set text is George Fyler Townsend’s 1887 wording of “The Crow and the Pitcher,” from Three Hundred Æsop’s Fables, as posted on Wikisource. The wording is:

A Crow perishing with thirst saw a pitcher, and, hoping to find water, flew to it with great delight. When he reached it, he discovered to his grief that it contained so little water that he could not possibly get at it. He tried everything he could think of to reach the water, but all his efforts were in vain. At last he collected as many stones as he could carry, and dropped them one by one with his beak into the pitcher, until he brought the water within his reach, and thus saved his life. Necessity is the mother of invention.

The action is short: thirst, a pitcher with too little water, failed efforts, stones dropped one by one, the water brought within reach, the life saved. The moral is stated after the action, not inside it. The Wikisource page appears to lack a stop after “delight”; the stop is restored here as ordinary punctuation, not as a change of wording. Do not add an official natural-history claim about crows, and do not treat the moral as a law of physics.

Remember

A fable is a short invented narrative that ends in a stated moral. Townsend’s crow drops stones until the water can be reached. The moral is “Necessity is the mother of invention.”

  4. The moral is not argued in the middle of the dropping of the stones, and it is not an official law of nature.

Physics · Superposition, and constructive and destructive interference, a first look

40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.10, amplitudes add; two pure cases, in phase and out of phase

Learn

Key terms

superposition
Definition
When two or more waves arrive at the same point, their disturbances are superimposed: the resulting disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. If the disturbances are along the same line, the amplitudes add, with sign.
Origin
From French superposition, from Late Latin superpositio, from superponere, to place over (Etymonline; Wiktionary).
Example
Two identical waves that arrive in phase, crest with crest, produce a wave of twice the amplitude and the same wavelength, which OpenStax 16.10 calls pure constructive interference.
Not this
Not a replacement of one wave by the other, and not a claim that energy is created. Not the reserved first look at beats, and not an official speed of sound or of light invented today.
Connects to
Last term’s wave, wavelength, and wave velocity, from OpenStax 16.9. Constructive and destructive interference are the two pure cases of this sum.
constructive interference
Definition
Pure constructive interference, in OpenStax 16.10, is the superposition of two identical waves that arrive exactly in phase, crest aligned with crest and trough with trough. The result is a wave of twice the amplitude and the same wavelength.
Example
If each of two identical waves has amplitude X, the pure constructive result has amplitude 2X and the same wavelength as either wave.
Not this
Not destructive interference, in which crest lines up with trough and the resulting amplitude is zero. Not a change of wavelength. Not an official loudness of a real stereo, which the text uses only as an illustration of mixed interference.
Connects to
Superposition, and the already-taught amplitude and wavelength.
destructive interference
Definition
Pure destructive interference, in OpenStax 16.10, is the superposition of two identical waves that arrive exactly out of phase, crest aligned with trough. The disturbances are in opposite directions, and the resulting amplitude is zero: the waves cancel.
Example
Two identical waves of amplitude X, exactly out of phase, produce zero amplitude at that point.
Not this
Not constructive interference, which doubles the amplitude. Not a claim that the two waves have ceased to exist elsewhere, or that energy has been destroyed as a new official law. The reserved standing wave and beats are later in the week.
Connects to
Superposition, and constructive interference as the opposite pure case.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.10, “Superposition and Interference,” is this week’s physics source. Access for free at the OpenStax College Physics 2e book URL. Most waves look complicated because several simple waves add. When two or more waves arrive at the same point, their disturbances are superimposed: the resulting disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. If the disturbances are along the same line, the amplitudes add.

Two pure cases are taught today. If two identical waves arrive exactly in phase, crest with crest, the result is pure constructive interference: twice the amplitude, same wavelength. If they arrive exactly out of phase, crest with trough, the result is pure destructive interference: the resulting amplitude is zero. Most real superpositions are mixed, and can vary from place to place. Standing waves and beats wait until later this week. Invent no official speed of sound or of light.

Remember

Disturbances add. In phase, two identical waves give twice the amplitude and the same wavelength. Out of phase, they cancel.

Watch me

  2. The wavelength is unchanged: it is the same as that of either wave.
  3. Out of phase, crest lines up with trough, the disturbances are opposite, and the resulting amplitude is zero.
  4. Do not change the wavelength in either pure case, and do not invent an official speed of sound.

Together

Hint 1: Constructive

The amplitudes add: 3 cm + 3 cm.

Hint 2: Destructive

The disturbances cancel: the resulting amplitude is zero.

History · Inter Caetera, May 3, 1493: the recital of the voyage, a first look

40 min · Method: Davenport’s 1917 translation of the May 3 bull; a claim in a grant, not a journal

Learn

Key terms

Inter Caetera (May 3, 1493)
Definition
Inter Caetera of May 3, 1493, is a papal bull of Alexander VI, used here in Frances Gardiner Davenport’s 1917 English translation. It addresses the sovereigns of Castile and León, recounts a westward voyage under Christopher Columbus after the recovery of Granada, and later in the week grants lands “hitherto discovered” and “to be discovered hereafter,” with a proviso about Christian owners. The wording is a claim in a legal and religious document, not a map and not a voyage journal.
Example
The bull says that Columbus and his company, “sailing in the ocean sea, through western waters, as is said, toward the Indians, discovered certain very remote islands and even mainlands, that hitherto had not been discovered by others,” and that “therein dwell very many peoples.”
Not this
Not the later May 4 bull, and not a line of demarcation, both of which remain reserved. Not the first-voyage letter, which also remains reserved. Not an official count of islands or an official quantity of gold.
Connects to
Term 3 history begins with exploration and empires, from a primary source. Dewing, Buildings I.3, is taken up in this week’s art-history seminar, not as a new history chapter.

Term 3 history begins with exploration and empires, from a primary source. Today’s set text is the bull Inter Caetera of May 3, 1493, issued in the name of Alexander VI, in the 1917 English translation by Frances Gardiner Davenport, in European Treaties bearing on the History of the United States and its Dependencies to 1648, as posted on Wikisource. The bull is dated “in Rome, at St. Peter’s, on the third day of May in the year one thousand four hundred and ninety-three.” It is a papal grant, not a voyage journal. The first-voyage letter, and the later May 4 bull with any line of leagues, remain reserved. Invent no official island count and no official quantity of gold.

The opening recital says that, after “the siege and recovery of the kingdom itself of Granada,” the addressees chose “our beloved son Christopher Columbus,” furnished with ships and men, “to make diligent quest for these remote and unknown countries through the sea, where hitherto no one had sailed.” It then claims that they, “sailing in the ocean sea, through western waters, as is said, toward the Indians, discovered certain very remote islands and even mainlands, that hitherto had not been discovered by others; and therein dwell very many peoples living in peace, and, as reported, going unclothed, and not eating flesh.” Two points must be kept together: the bull claims lands “not hitherto discovered by others,” and in the same sentence it says that many peoples already live there. Those peoples’ presence is in the document. The claim of first discovery is also in the document, and it is a claim, not a fact you are asked to certify today. The grant clause itself waits until Wednesday.

limited palette
Definition

Modern language · Spanish: the simple future of a regular -ar verb, a first look

40 min · Method: keep the infinitive, add -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án; model hablaré

Learn

Key terms

simple future of a regular verb
Definition
The simple future of a regular Spanish verb keeps the whole infinitive and adds the endings -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án. So hablar becomes hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablaréis, hablarán: I will speak, you will speak, and the rest. The same endings will be used tomorrow with a regular -er or -ir infinitive.
Example
Mañana hablaré. Ella hablará. Nosotros hablaremos.
Not this
Not the already-taught present hablo, I speak, or the near future that uses ir a. Not an irregular future such as haré or iré, which remains reserved. Not a voice code written in the meaning column of a wordlist.
Connects to

Seminar · Logic: equally likely outcomes, a first look

40 min · Method: probability 1/n when n outcomes are equally likely; invented fair cube and spinner

Learn

Key terms

equally likely outcomes
Definition
Outcomes are equally likely when each of the distinct possible results of a chance process has the same chance. If there are n such outcomes, and one of them is named, its probability is 1/n, provided the outcomes really are equally likely and one of them must happen.
Example
An invented fair six-faced cube, with faces labeled 1 through 6, has six equally likely outcomes. The probability of a named single face, such as 4, is 1/6. The probability of an even face is 3/6, which is 1/2.
Not this
Not an official probability of a real event, such as weather or a living person’s future, invented without evidence. Not a lesson in gambling. Not a claim that a real cube in the room has been tested as fair.
Connects to
Term 3 logic begins probability and evidence with this first look at a fair chance. Later work on evidence, and on outcomes that are not equally likely, remains reserved.

Term 3 logic begins probability and evidence. Today a first look at equally likely outcomes, on invented classroom figures, not as a gambling lesson, and not as an official probability of a real event. If a chance process has n distinct outcomes, all equally likely, and one of them must happen, then the probability of one named outcome is 1/n. An invented fair six-faced cube, faces 1 through 6, has six equally likely outcomes. The probability of the face 4 is 1/6. The probability of an even face (2, 4, or 6) is 3/6, which is 1/2. An invented fair spinner with eight equal sectors has probability 1/8 for one named sector. Do not invent an official probability for weather, or for a living person’s future. A process whose outcomes are not equally likely remains reserved.

Remember

If n outcomes are equally likely, and one must happen, the probability of one named outcome is 1/n. An invented fair six-faced cube gives 1/6 for a single face, and 3/6 = 1/2 for an even face.
