Latin · Capitulum XV: domī, and a close of the chapter’s new grammar

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV closes its new grammar. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

domī
Definition
Domī means at home, with no preposition. It is a locative form of domus. The locative case itself was already taught in Capitulum VI for the names of towns (Rōmae, at Rome). Domī is one of a small set of common words that also keep a locative, and it is the first look at this particular form.
Example
Puer domī est. — The boy is at home. Discipulī nōn domī sunt; in lūdō sunt.
Not this
Not a first look at the locative as a case, and not a first look at Rōmae. Not in plus the ablative (in casā), which is a different construction. Domī does not take a preposition in this use.
Connects to
Capitulum VI taught the locative for towns. Capitulum XV adds domī as the everyday locative “at home.”

The locative, already taught, answers ubi? for the names of towns: Rōmae, at Rome. A few other common words also keep a locative. The one taught today is domī, at home.

Mathematics · Geometry XLIII: a right prism, volume as base area times height

45 min · Method: the same packing idea, now written V = B × h, where B is the area of the base

Learn

Key terms

volume of a right prism
Definition
A right prism has two parallel, congruent bases, and its side edges are perpendicular to the bases. Its volume is V = B × h, where B is the area of one base and h is the perpendicular height between the bases. A right rectangular prism is the special case B = ℓ × w, so V = ℓ × w × h, already taught yesterday.
Example
A classroom right triangular prism whose base is a right triangle of legs 6 and 8, and whose height is 5, has B = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24, so V = 24 × 5 = 120 cubic units.
Not this
Not a first look at the area of a triangle or a rectangle, already taught, and not a first look at yesterday’s box formula. Not a cylinder, which is tomorrow’s job. Not a slanted prism, whose volume is not taught today.
Connects to
The packing idea is the same: each layer has B unit cubes (or a fraction of them that still totals B), and there are h layers.

Yesterday’s box is a right prism whose base is a rectangle. The same packing works for other right prisms: two parallel, congruent bases, with sides standing perpendicular to those bases.

If B is the area of the base, already taught as an area in earlier weeks, then

V = B × h.

For a rectangular base, B = ℓ × w, so V = ℓ × w × h, as yesterday. For a triangular base, B is the triangle’s area. A classroom right triangle of legs 6 and 8 has B = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24. If the prism’s height is 5, V = 24 × 5 = 120 cubic units.

The height h is perpendicular to the base, just as in the area formulas already taught. A slanted edge is not h.

Remember

A right prism has V = B × h, with B the area of the base and h perpendicular to the base. Yesterday’s V = ℓ × w × h is the rectangular case. Triangle area ½bh is not a first look; it is used as B.

Key terms: volume of a right prism.

Watch me

  2. The height of the prism is 5, so V = 24 × 5 = 120 cubic units.
  3. If someone treats 6 × 8 × 5 as the volume and forgets the ½, the answer 240 is twice too large, because the base is a triangle, not a 6-by-8 rectangle.

Science · The enthalpy of fusion of ice, from the Harvey/NIST table

40 min · Method: ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g, from the same Harvey/NIST table already used last week for density, with invented masses labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

enthalpy of fusion of ice
Definition
The enthalpy of fusion of ice, written ΔfusH in the Harvey/NIST table, is the energy needed to melt ice at the transition, per gram, without a change of temperature. At 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the table gives ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g. The same quantity is often called the latent heat of fusion in school physics.
Example
An invented 100 g of ice, already at 0 °C, needs 100 × 333.4 = 3.334 × 10⁴ J, or 33.34 kJ, to melt at 0 °C. The mass is invented; the 333.4 J/g is Harvey’s.
Not this
Not a first look at melting at 1 atm, already taught when the Water module opened, and not a first look at last week’s densities. Not a temperature change, and not a boiling figure. Not an official liquid-water specific heat, which is not taken from a table row this week.
Connects to
Harvey’s heading is “Phase Transition Properties,” and the symbol is ΔfusH. This lesson uses both the table’s name and the school name latent heat of fusion, without treating them as two different quantities here.

Last week used the Harvey/NIST table for densities at 0 °C. The same table, under phase-transition properties, gives

ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g

at 101.325 kPa. That is the energy needed to melt one gram of ice at 0 °C, without raising its temperature. In school language this is also the latent heat of fusion. The temperature stays at 0 °C while the ice melts; the energy goes into the change of state.

For an invented 100 g of ice already at 0 °C, the energy to melt it at 0 °C is 100 × 333.4 = 33,340 J, or 33.34 kJ. The mass is invented and labeled invented; the 333.4 J/g is Harvey’s.

Last week’s densities (ice Ih 0.9167 g/cm³, liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³ at 0 °C) explain why ice floats. They are not a second first look today, and they are not a substitute for ΔfusH. A specific heat of ice, and a comparison of melting with warming, wait until Thursday. An official liquid-water cp is not invented, and is not copied from a table row that was not opened as a definite fetched figure this week.

Numerical figures are from A. H. Harvey, “Properties of Ice and Supercooled Water,” a NIST update to a CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics table, at standard atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water (PDF: https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=926353). ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g. The specific heat of ice Ih at 0 °C is cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Last week’s NIST/Harvey densities at 0 °C (ice Ih 0.9167 g/cm³; liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³) remain available as contrasts, not as a new first look. A liquid-water official cp is not taken from a table that was not opened as a definite fetched row for this week, and is not invented.

Literature · The gate of Inferno III (Longfellow, lines 1–21)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Inferno III.1–21 in Longfellow’s 1867 translation; typography as in the set text

Learn

Key terms

the gate of Inferno
Definition
The gate of Inferno, in Longfellow’s Inferno III.1–11, bears an inscription that begins “Through me the way is to the city dolent” and ends “All hope abandon, ye who enter in!” The speaker beheld “These words in sombre color … Written upon the summit of a gate.”
Example
The nine lines name a dolent city, eternal dole, and the people lost; Justice, divine Omnipotence, the highest Wisdom, and the primal Love; and that before the gate there were no created things, “Only eterne, and I eternal last.”
Not this
Not a first look at Inferno, or at last week’s close of Canto II (“the deep and savage way”). Not a modernized wording in place of “city dolent,” “sombre color,” or “ye who enter in.” Not a later commentator’s list imported as if it were in the set lines.
Connects to
Longfellow, Inferno III.1–21, Wikisource posting of the 1867 text, as in the source note.

Dante, Inferno III, translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867, as posted at Wikisource: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_3 (page text from the linked scan pages). Spelling and punctuation are quoted as in that text, including “sombre color,” “city dolent,” and “withouten.” This course does not add later identifications beyond what the set wording itself names. Charon, the river, and the rest of the canto after line 69 remain reserved.

Canto II ended on “the deep and savage way.” Canto III opens with an inscription:

Through me the way is to the city dolent;
Through me the way is to eternal dole;
Through me the way among the people lost.
Justice incited my sublime Creator;
Created me divine Omnipotence,
The highest Wisdom and the primal Love.
Before me there were no created things,
Only eterne, and I eternal last.
All hope abandon, ye who enter in!

Then: “These words in sombre color I beheld / Written upon the summit of a gate; / Whence I: ‘Their sense is, Master, hard to me!’”

The guide answers that suspicion and cowardice must here be abandoned, and that they have come to the place where the speaker will “behold the people dolorous / Who have foregone the good of intellect.” He lays his hand on the speaker’s, “with joyful mien,” and “led me in among the secret things.”

Keep Longfellow’s wording as it stands. The neutrals of lines 22–69 remain for Thursday. Charon and the river remain reserved. Writing’s Franklin work on lines 1–9 is a different job; today’s literature job is a close reading through line 21.

Key terms

family check after light modeling
Definition
A family check after light modeling is a pause, at a distance, to see whether the light family still reads as one lighter family, whether the shadow still reads as one darker family, and whether yesterday’s halftones have flattened the light or crossed the terminator.
Example
If a halftone has slipped into the shadow’s value, it should be lightened or wiped until the families separate again at a distance.
Not this
Not a first look at a family check, already used last week after the lay-in, and not a first look at modeling in the light, taught yesterday. Not a new pass of heavy modeling, and not full color.
Connects to
Last week’s check asked whether the lay-in still read. Today’s check asks the same of both families after the light has been modeled a little.

Stand at your usual viewing distance. Do the two families still read? Is the light still lighter than the shadow, as one family? Has a halftone flattened the breadth of the light, or crossed into the shadow? Has the terminator become a line?

Correct only what the check names: soften a crowded light, lift a mark that has grown too dark, rest a terminator that has turned into an outline. Do not start a finish in the features, and do not open full color. If you are in a drawing rehearsal, the same check holds.

Elective · Music: contrasting and parallel periods

40 min · Method: a period already taught is now named contrasting or parallel, by whether the antecedent and consequent begin alike. Cadences already taught are used, not retaught as first looks

Learn

Key terms

contrasting period
Definition
A contrasting period, in this lesson’s school sense, is a period whose consequent does not begin like the antecedent. The two phrases still form one period, with cadences already taught: often a weaker cadence at the end of the antecedent and a stronger one at the end of the consequent.
Example
If the first phrase begins with a rising three-note figure and the second phrase begins with a different, falling figure, and they still answer as antecedent and consequent, the period is contrasting.
Not this
Not a first look at a period, or at antecedent and consequent, taught last week. Not a first look at a half cadence or an authentic cadence. Not a parallel period, which is today’s other name.
Connects to
Last week named the period. Today adds two ways the phrases may be related at their openings.
parallel period
Definition
A parallel period, in this lesson’s school sense, is a period whose consequent begins like the antecedent, usually with the same opening figure, before it continues to a stronger cadence.
Example
If both phrases begin with the same rising three-note figure, and the second phrase then turns to close more strongly, the period is parallel.
Not this
Not a first look at a period, and not a contrasting period, which begins differently. Not a claim about every later textbook’s extra types, which are reserved.
Connects to
The names contrast and parallel refer to the openings of the two phrases, not to whether the cadences differ — the cadences of a period already often differ, as last week taught.

Last week’s period was an antecedent followed by a consequent, often a weaker cadence then a stronger one. Those cadence names are already taught and are not first looks today.

Today names two common relations between the openings of those phrases:

A parallel period has a consequent that begins like the antecedent, usually with the same opening figure, before it goes on to its cadence.

A contrasting period has a consequent that does not begin like the antecedent. The two phrases still belong together as a period; their openings simply differ.

The names refer to the openings, not to the mere fact that the two cadences differ. A parallel period may still end its first phrase more weakly and its second more strongly, as last week already allowed.

Remember

A parallel period’s consequent begins like the antecedent. A contrasting period’s consequent begins differently. Both are periods already taught. Cadences already taught are used, not retaught as first looks.
