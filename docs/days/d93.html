Latin · Capitulum XVI: the ablative absolute with a deponent participle

30 min · Latin · The same construction, now with a present participle from a deponent already taught in the third person. Perfect-participle absolutes remain reserved.

Learn

Key terms

deponent present participle in the absolute
Definition
A deponent verb, already taught in the third person and the infinitive, also has a present participle that is active in meaning, as deponents are. That participle may stand in an ablative absolute: Puerō loquente, magister tacet. — With the boy speaking, the schoolmaster is silent.
Example
Discipulīs ēgredientibus, canis sequitur. — With the pupils going out, the dog follows.
Not this
Not a first look at the ablative absolute, taught Monday, and not a first look at loquitur or ēgreditur. Not a perfect-participle absolute. The participle loquente means “speaking,” not “being spoken.”
Connects to
Last week’s deponents were third person and infinitive. Today adds only their present participle in the construction already taught.

Monday’s absolute used participles such as recitante, from an ordinary active verb. Deponents already taught — loquitur, ēgreditur, sequitur, cōnātur — also have present participles, still active in meaning:

Word How to say it Meaning
loquente, loquentibus loh-KWEN-teh, loh-KWEN-tih-boos speaking (singular, plural)
ēgrediente, ēgredientibus ay-greh-dee-EN-teh, ay-greh-dee-EN-tih-boos going out
sequente, sequentibus seh-KWEN-teh, seh-KWEN-tih-boos following

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Puerō loquente, magister tacet. Discipulīs ēgredientibus, canis sequitur.

The participle is translated as active, just as loquitur is “he speaks,” not “he is spoken.” First- and second-person deponent forms, and the perfect-participle absolute, remain reserved.

Remember

A deponent’s present participle may stand in an ablative absolute and is translated as active: Puerō loquente, magister tacet. This is not a first look at the absolute, or at the deponent in the third person. Perfect-participle absolutes remain reserved.

Key terms: deponent present participle in the absolute.

Key terms

volume of a sphere
Definition
The volume of a sphere of radius r is V = (4/3)πr³, in cubic units. OpenStax, College Physics 2e, Appendix C, “Useful Information,” Table C9, lists this formula (CC BY). Exact answers stay in terms of π.
Example
A classroom sphere of radius 3 has V = (4/3)π × 27 = 36π cubic units. The length 3 is invented; the formula is OpenStax’s.
Not this

Connects to

A sphere is the solid of a single radius r, every point on the surface at that distance from the center. OpenStax, College Physics 2e, Appendix C, Table C9 (CC BY), lists the volume as

Physics · The maximum speed in simple harmonic motion, vmax = X√(k/m)

40 min · Method: vmax = X√(k/m) from OpenStax 16.5, with sourced Example 16.6 and invented classroom checks

Learn

Key terms

maximum speed in SHM
Definition
In undamped simple harmonic motion, the maximum speed occurs at x = 0, and OpenStax 16.5 gives vmax = X√(k/m). It is proportional to the amplitude X and to the square root of k, and inversely proportional to the square root of m.
Example
OpenStax Example 16.6: a 900 kg car, k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m, amplitude 0.100 m, no damping, vmax = 0.852 m/s. Those figures are OpenStax’s, not invented.
Not this
Not a first look at Monday’s conservation equation, or at last week’s ½kx² = ½mv² as a one-step conversion. Not a period, and not a claim that a real car’s shock absorbers are undamped (OpenStax assumes no damping in the example).
Connects to
The formula follows from ½m vmax² = ½kX², which is Monday’s statement at x = 0.

Monday’s conservation, at x = 0, is ½m vmax² = ½kX². Solving for the speed gives OpenStax 16.5’s result:

vmax = X√(k/m).

OpenStax Example 16.6 uses a car of mass 900 kg, a suspension with k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m, and amplitude 0.100 m, with no damping:

vmax = 0.100 × √(6.53 × 10⁴ / 900) = 0.852 m/s.

Those figures are OpenStax’s. Invented Together figures, labeled invented: k = 200 N/m, m = 0.50 kg, X = 0.10 m, so vmax = 0.10 × √(200/0.50) = 0.10 × 20 = 2.0 m/s. A check: ½mv² = ½ × 0.50 × 4.0 = 1.0 J, which matches Monday’s ½kX² = 1.0 J.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.5, “Energy and the Simple Harmonic Oscillator” (CC BY): https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-5-energy-and-the-simple-harmonic-oscillator The conservation statement ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX², the result vmax = X√(k/m), and Example 16.6 (a 900 kg car, k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m, amplitude 0.100 m, vmax = 0.852 m/s, no damping) are in that section. Last week’s PE_el = ½kx² and the conversion ½kx² = ½mv², from section 16.1 Example 16.2, are not first looks. Other classroom masses, amplitudes, and constants are invented and labeled invented.

Remember

vmax = X√(k/m), at x = 0, if damping is neglected. OpenStax Example 16.6: 0.852 m/s for the sourced car. Invented classroom: 2.0 m/s for k = 200 N/m, m = 0.50 kg, X = 0.10 m. This is not a first look at Monday’s conservation statement.

Key terms

di
Definition
Di is the first-person singular preterite of dar, to give. The forms are di, diste, dio, dimos, disteis, dieron. There is no accent on di or dio in the ordinary school spelling taught here. Di is not last week’s dije, and dio is not dijo.
Example
Ayer di el libro. — Yesterday I gave the book. (Invented.) Ellos dieron las cartas. — They gave the letters. (Invented.)
Not this
Not a first look at vi, and not a first look at dije. Not the present doy. Not a spelling dió, and not the miss *dije used as if it were dar.
Connects to
This lesson teaches the forms fully. The school sense is a completed giving on that occasion.

Dar, to give, has an irregular preterite:

Word How to say it Meaning
di dee I gave
diste DEES-teh you (singular, informal) gave
dio DEE-oh he, she, or you (formal) gave
dimos DEE-mohs we gave
disteis DEES-tayss you (plural, informal, Spain) gave
dieron DYEH-rohn they, or you (plural) gave

The first-person form is di, not dije. Dije remains last week’s form of decir. Dio is not dijo. The they-form is dieron, beside vieron, dijeron, and trajeron as already-taught they-forms of other verbs.

Remember

Dar in the preterite: di, diste, dio, dimos, disteis, dieron. Di is not dije; dio is not dijo. The school sense is a completed giving. Vi is a contrast, not a first look today.

Key terms: di.

Watch me

Watch the full table beside a di / dije contrast, then dio / dijo.

  2. Ayer di el libro is a giving. Ayer dije la verdad is a saying. Different verbs.
  3. Dio, not dijo, for dar; dijo remains “said / told.”

Elective · Art history: Dewing’s meadow of stones, and the sight new each time

40 min · Method: a rereading of Dewing I.1.58–63, already opened in history this week, for the colors and the repeated visit. No invented counts

Learn

Key terms

meadow, colors, and the sight new each time (Dewing)
Definition
This elective gathers two claims already read in history this week. In I.1.59–60 the columns and stones are a meadow in full bloom, with purple, green, crimson, flashing white, and Nature varying “the most contrasting colours.” In I.1.62 the experience of entering to pray comes again “on each successive occasion, as though the sight were new each time.”
Example
The two claims belong to one stretch, but they are not the same claim: one is about the stones’ appearance; the other is about a repeated visit. Keep “colours” inside quotations when you quote.
Not this
Not a first look at this week’s history extract, and not a first look at last week’s gold and prevailing light. Not an invented column count.

Dewing I.1.58–63, as in the source note.

History this week has already read I.1.58–63. This elective holds the meadow of stones and the sight new each time side by side, without adding a count.

The stones: a meadow in full bloom, with Dewing’s named colors. The visit: not only the first time, but each successive occasion, as though the sight were new. A near-miss is to treat the colors as a modern paint list, or to say visitors soon tire of the church, which reverses I.1.63’s “no surfeit.”

Remember

Dewing’s stones are a meadow of named colors. The experience comes again on later visits, as though the sight were new, and no one has had a surfeit. Both claims are already in this week’s history reading. No invented counts.

Key terms: meadow, colors, and the sight new each time (Dewing).

Watch me
