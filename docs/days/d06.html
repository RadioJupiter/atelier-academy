Latin · Capitulum III, part 1: the accusative

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. III "Puer improbus"

Learn

Key terms

accusative
Definition
The case that often marks the direct object: the person or thing the verb acts on.
Origin
From Latin accūsātīvus, a mistranslation of the Greek grammatical term αἰτιατική (‘expressing an effect’), as if it came from accūsāre (‘to blame’).
Example
If a subject ‘sees a town’, the town will be in the accusative, not the form you have used for the subject.
Not this
The accusative is not ‘the past tense’, and it is not the genitive (‘whose?’).
Connects to
The nominative (the subject form you have been using since Day 1) and the accusative are two cases of the same noun.
direct object
Definition
The person or thing that receives the action of the verb. In Latin it is typically in the accusative.
Example
In a sentence meaning ‘The boy sees the town,’ the town is the direct object.
Not this
The direct object is not the subject, and it is not an ‘of’ possessor (that was the genitive).
Connects to
Case is the system: nominative for the subject, genitive for the possessor, accusative for the object.

The accusative is the form used for the direct object — the person or thing the verb acts on. In the early chapters, many masculine and feminine nouns take -m in the singular accusative (puellam, fluvium). The subject stays in the nominative (puella, fluvius).

Notice: the accusative marks the object of an action. Singular endings: -um (masculine and neuter in -us/-um) and -am (feminine in -a).

nominative (subject) accusative (object)
servus servum
ancilla ancillam
puer puerum

Puer puellam pulsat. Puella puerum pulsat. The endings, not the word order, show who does what. Latin word order is free; the usual order puts the verb last.

Verbs in this chapter are third person singular: -at (vocat, pulsat), -et (videt, rīdet), -it (venit, dormit).

Remember

Who does the action: nominative. Who or what is acted on: accusative (often -m in these words). Puella fluvium videt — the girl sees the river.

Key terms: accusative, direct object.

Mathematics · Geometry III: parallel lines and the angle sum

60 min · Kiselev, Planimetry, parallel lines

Learn

Key terms

transversal
Definition
A line that crosses two or more other lines, forming corresponding, alternate interior, and same-side interior angles.
Origin
From Middle French transversal, from Medieval Latin trānsversālis, from Latin trānsversus (‘turned across’).
Example
When the two lines are parallel, alternate interior angles are equal.
Not this
A transversal is not itself required to be perpendicular, and it is not ‘any third line in the plane’ if it does not cut the other two.
Connects to
Equal alternate interior angles (or equal corresponding angles) are the test that the two lines are parallel.
exterior angle
Definition
An angle formed by one side of a triangle and the extension of an adjacent side. It equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
Example
If ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 60°, the exterior angle at C is 110°.
Not this
It is not the same as an adjacent interior angle, and it is not ‘any angle outside the triangle’ with no side in common.
Connects to
The corollary follows from the theorem that the angles of a triangle add to 180°, which itself uses a parallel and a transversal.

When a line t crosses two other lines, it is a transversal. If those two lines are parallel, corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and consecutive interior angles add to 180°. Conversely, if (for example) corresponding angles are equal, the lines are parallel. These facts are the engine of many later proofs.

The parallel axiom. Through a point not on a line, there is at most one line parallel to the given line. (Kiselev proves that at least one exists; the axiom supplies uniqueness.)

Angles formed by a transversal. When a line crosses two lines, it forms corresponding, alternate interior and same-side interior angles. Theorem: two lines are parallel if and only if alternate interior angles are equal (equivalently, if corresponding angles are equal, or same-side interior angles add to 180°).

Theorem. The angles of a triangle add to 180°. Proof. Through vertex B of triangle ABC draw the line parallel to AC. The two angles it makes with BA and BC are equal to ∠A and ∠C (alternate interior angles), and together with ∠B they form a straight angle. ∎

Corollary (exterior angle). An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles not adjacent to it.

Remember

Parallel + transversal ⇒ corresponding angles equal; alternate interior equal; consecutive interior supplementary. Those equalities also let you prove lines are parallel.

Key terms: transversal, exterior angle.

Watch me

In triangle ABC, ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 60°. Find ∠C, and find the exterior angle at C. Give a reason for each.

  1. The angles of a triangle add to 180°, so ∠C = 180° − 50° − 60° = 70°.
  2. An exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles not adjacent to it, so the exterior angle at C is 50° + 60° = 110°.
  3. Check: the exterior angle and ∠C are supplementary: 110° + 70° = 180°. ✓

Writing & Rhetoric · The single-paragraph outline

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, single-paragraph outline (SPO)

Learn

Key terms

single-paragraph outline
Definition
A plan for one paragraph: a topic sentence (a claim), then a short list of the points or evidence in order, written before the draft.
Example
Claim: The first line of the Iliad names wrath, not victory. Evidence: the opening word; the deaths that follow; a contrast with a war-chronicle.
Not this
It is not a first draft in complete sentences, and it is not a list of topics without a claim.
Connects to
Franklin’s hints (Day 4) recalled someone else’s structure. This outline plans a paragraph of your own, using claims from Day 5.
topic sentence
Definition
The claim that opens a paragraph and that the rest of the paragraph must support.
Example
‘Hector’s meeting with Andromache shows what the war will cost, in a way the battle scenes do not.’
Not this
A topic sentence is not ‘In this paragraph I will discuss…’, and it is not a quotation.
Connects to
The analytical essay on Day 9 will need one of these at the start of each body paragraph.

A single paragraph is one unit of thought: a topic sentence that states the claim, then sentences that support it with reason or evidence, then (often) a close that does not merely repeat the first sentence. Hochman's method still applies: write tight sentences first, then arrange them. One paragraph, one job.

Plan before drafting. An SPO has: a topic sentence (written in full); three or four supporting details written as brief notes, not sentences (abbreviations and symbols welcome, so you cannot copy them straight into the draft); and a concluding sentence (written in full) that restates the point in a new way. The notes force you to compose sentences when drafting, using the strategies from Days 1–3.

Topic sentence types: a statement, a question, an "although" sentence, or an appositive sentence.

Example SPO. T.S.: Although Franklin had little schooling, he trained himself into a fine writer by a deliberate method. 1. Spectator vol. → read repeatedly, admired 2. short hints → put away → rebuilt from memory 3. compared w/ original → corrected faults 4. prose → verse → prose = stock of words C.S.: His method shows that good style can be built step by step, by anyone willing to compare work against a master.

Remember

Topic sentence first. Every later sentence must earn its place. End without a hollow restatement. Prefer because/but/so links to a list of unconnected facts.

Key terms: single-paragraph outline, topic sentence.

Physics · Newton's first law: inertia

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Key terms

inertia
Definition
The tendency of a body to keep its velocity — to stay at rest, or to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed — unless a net force acts on it. This is Newton’s first law.
Origin
From Latin inertia (‘inactivity, lack of skill’), from iners, from in- (‘without’) + ars (‘skill, art’). A sense close to the modern physics one was first used in New Latin by Johannes Kepler.
Example
A puck on smooth ice keeps sliding at nearly constant velocity because the net force is almost zero.
Not this
Inertia is not a force, and it is not ‘a force that keeps things moving’. No net force is needed to keep a constant velocity.
Connects to
A constant velocity (Day 1) is what inertia maintains. Acceleration (Day 3) is what happens when a net force breaks that maintenance.
net force Fnet
Definition
The vector sum of all forces on a body. Newton’s first law says that if the net force is zero, the velocity does not change.
Origin
Force is from Old French force, from Latin fortia, from fortis (‘strong’).
Example
Two equal opposite pushes cancel: Fnet = 0, so the object does not accelerate.
Not this
A net force of zero does not mean ‘no forces act’. Forces can be present and cancel.
Connects to
Inertia describes the motion when Fnet = 0. Day 8 will relate a nonzero net force to acceleration.

Newton's first law: a body keeps its velocity (including remaining at rest) unless a net force acts on it. 'Net' matters: several forces can cancel, and then the velocity does not change. Inertia is this tendency to keep the present velocity. A force is not needed to keep a body moving at a steady speed in a straight line (in this model); a force is needed to change that velocity.

Pre-reading. An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a net external force acts on it. Motion does not need a force; change of motion does. On Earth, moving things slow down because friction and air resistance act on them, which is why the law was not obvious before Galileo and Newton. Net force is the vector sum of all forces. Forces are measured in newtons (N).

ConcepTests

  1. A hockey puck slides across nearly frictionless ice at constant velocity. The net force on it is: (A) in the direction of motion (B) zero (C) opposite to the motion (D) it depends on its speed.
  2. A passenger in a bus that brakes suddenly lurches forward. Why? (A) A forward force pushes the passenger (B) The passenger tends to keep moving forward while the bus slows (C) Gravity increases (D) The seat pushes the passenger forward.
  3. A ball on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle, and the string breaks. The ball then moves: (A) in a spiral outward (B) straight outward from the center (C) along the tangent to the circle at the moment of release (D) in a curve that continues around.

Problems

  1. A 2 kg book rests on a table. List the forces acting on it, and give the net force.
  2. A car moves at a steady 25 m/s on a straight level road. The engine's driving force is 600 N. What is the total resistive force?
  3. Demonstration. Put a card on top of a glass and a coin on the card. Flick the card away sharply. Explain the coin's behavior with the first law.

Remember

No net force ⇒ velocity stays the same (rest or uniform straight-line motion). A net force is required to change speed or direction.

Key terms: inertia, net force.

Watch me

A puck slides on a nearly frictionless surface at 3 m/s and no sideways force acts. After 2 s it is still moving at 3 m/s in the same direction. The net force was (approximately) zero, so the first law says the velocity does not change. If a constant force then acts against the motion, the puck slows: that is a change of velocity, so a net force is now present.

Together

A ball flies through the air. After it has left the hand, is a force needed to keep it moving forward in Newton's first law? (Ignore air for a moment, then put air back.)

Hint 1: Ideal

No forward force is needed to keep a steady velocity.

Hint 2: Gravity and air

Gravity changes the vertical velocity; air may slow it. Those are net forces. There is still no need for a 'forward push' to explain the motion itself.

History · Athens: Pericles' Funeral Oration

45 min · Primary source: Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.37 (Richard Crawley translation, 1874, public domain)

Learn

Key terms

democracy
Definition
In Pericles’ usage (as Thucydides reports it), a constitution whose administration ‘favors the many instead of the few’. In Athens this meant many citizens, not all adults.
Origin
From Middle French, from Medieval Latin dēmocratia, from Ancient Greek δημοκρατία, from δῆμος (‘the people’) + κράτος (‘power, rule’).
Example
Pericles claims equal justice in private disputes and office by merit, not class, for citizens.
Not this
‘The many’ in this speech is not women, slaves, or resident foreigners (metics), who were excluded from citizenship. It is not modern universal suffrage.
Connects to
Day 1’s sourcing still applies: the words are Thucydides’ version of Pericles (see 1.22), not a transcript.
citizenship
Definition
Membership in the political community, with its rights and duties. In classical Athens it was a restricted legal status, not a synonym for ‘everyone who lived in the city’.
Origin
From Middle English, from Anglo-Norman, ultimately from Old French cité + -ien, from Latin cīvitās, from cīvis (‘citizen’).
Example
Advancement in public life, in Pericles’ claim, is open to a poor citizen of merit — but only to a citizen.
Not this
Citizenship is not ‘living in the city’, and it is not the same in every society or century.
Connects to
Compare Day 3: Hammurabi’s laws treat people by rank. Pericles claims equality among citizens. Both are self-portraits in a source.

Context. In the winter after the first year of the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta (431 BCE), Athens held a public funeral for its war dead. Pericles, the leading Athenian statesman, gave the speech. Thucydides, an Athenian who fought in the war and wrote its history, reports it. But read his warning about speeches (1.22):

… it was in all cases difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory, so my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said.

The source (2.37, opening):

Our constitution does not copy the laws of neighbouring states; we are rather a pattern to others than imitators ourselves. Its administration favors the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy. If we look to the laws, they afford equal justice to all in their private differences; if no social standing, advancement in public life falls to reputation for capacity, class considerations not being allowed to interfere with merit; nor again does poverty bar the way, if a man is able to serve the state, he is not hindered by the obscurity of his condition.

Remember

Thucydides 1.22 warns that the speeches are not transcripts: he keeps the general sense and makes the speakers say what the occasion demanded. In 2.37 Pericles (as reported) claims Athens is a model, a democracy of 'the many', with equal justice and office by merit. 'The many' means many citizens — not women, slaves, or metics.

Key terms: democracy, citizenship.

Watch me

Sourcing the Funeral Oration, as a model.

  1. Whose words? Thucydides' version of Pericles, written after the fact, 'adhering as closely as possible to the general sense' but not word for word (1.22). Treat them as reliable for themes and for Thucydides' view, not as a tape recording.
  2. Three claims in 2.37: Athens is original, a pattern for others; administration favors the many (this is why it is called a democracy); equal justice in private disputes, and office by merit rather than class or poverty.
  3. Context still required: citizenship excluded most adults. 'The many' is relative to oligarchy, not a modern electorate.

Together

Hint 1: Who is counted

'The many' means many citizens, a minority of the adult population.

Hint 2: Still a contrast

The claim is still a contrast with oligarchy (power of the few), but it is not equality in the modern sense.

Atelier · Light on form: the egg

90 min

Learn

Key terms

core shadow
Definition
The band of darker value on a form, where the surface turns away from the light and before the reflected light on the shadow side begins.
Example
On an egg lit from one side, the core shadow is the darker belt that shows the egg turning, not a flat half-moon of gray.
Not this
The core shadow is not the cast shadow (the shadow the egg throws on the table), and it is not the highlight.
Connects to
Modeling (Day 5) is the general skill. On the egg you separate highlight, light, halftone, core shadow, reflected light, and cast shadow.
cast shadow
Definition
The shadow a form throws onto another surface when it blocks the light.
Example
The egg throws a cast shadow on the cloth; that shadow has an edge and a value of its own, darker usually than the reflected light on the egg.
Not this
A cast shadow is not the core shadow on the egg itself, and it is not automatically the darkest value in the drawing.
Connects to
Light on form is a family of values. Mixing up core and cast shadow flattens the egg.

An egg is a simple form with a single curved surface, used to learn light on form. One main light makes a highlight, a light family, a terminator, a shadow family, and a reflected light in the shadow, plus a cast shadow on the cloth. Keep the families simple; do not invent extra lights.

The structure of light on a curved form (one light source): - Light family: highlight → light → halftones, getting darker as the surface turns away. - Terminator / core shadow: where the form turns away from the light completely; the darkest part of the form shadow is usually just inside it. - Shadow family: form shadow, softened by reflected light bouncing in from nearby surfaces. Reflected light is always darker than the halftones in the light. Keeping the families separate is what makes a form look solid. - Cast shadow: the shadow the object throws on the surface, darkest and sharpest where the object touches it (the occlusion shadow).

Set-up. A white egg on a white or light-gray cloth, a single lamp above and to one side at about 45°, everything else dark. View from your marked spot.

Remember

One main light. Map light family, terminator, shadow family, reflected light, and cast shadow. The highlight is small. Values from the scale, not from invention.

Key terms: core shadow, cast shadow.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Set one lamp to the side and slightly in front; turn off competing room lights as far as you can.
  2. From the viewing point, squint and outline the cast shadow and the terminator on a light block-in of the egg.
  3. Lay in the shadow family as one gray (a step from your scale), leaving a slightly lighter strip for reflected light if you truly see it.
  4. Lay in the light family, reserving a small highlight, and check both families by squinting at the egg and the drawing together.

Hint 1: Lights

Hint 2: Action

Modern language · Spanish: questions

25 min

Learn

Key terms

question (Spanish)
Definition
A sentence that asks, marked in writing by ¿ at the start and ? at the end, and often begun with a question word such as qué, dónde, or cómo.
Example
¿Dónde está el libro? uses estar because it asks about place, and it opens with ¿.
Not this
Leaving off the opening ¿ is an English habit, and using ser for a place question is the Day 4 contrast forgotten.
Connects to
Estar (Day 4) and the present of -ar verbs (Day 5) appear inside these questions.
question word
Definition
A word such as qué (what), dónde (where), or cómo (how) that opens many questions and is written with an accent in these forms.
Example
¿Cómo estás? ¿Qué es esto?
Not this
The accent is not optional in these question words, and dónde is not the same word as donde without the accent.
Connects to
Each question word must be heard with a Spanish vowel, then used in a full question, not as a bare vocabulary item.

Questions in Spanish. For a yes/no question you can invert, or keep the order and use question marks ¿ … ?. Question words: qué (what), dónde (where), quién (who), cuándo (when), por qué (why), cómo (how).

Word How to say it Meaning
qué keh what
dónde DOHN-deh where
quién kyen who
cuándo KWAHN-doh when
por qué por keh why
cómo KOH-moh how
¿dónde está? DOHN-deh es-TAH where is he/she/it?

Question words always carry a written accent: qué (what), quién / quiénes (who), dónde (where), cuándo (when), cómo (how), cuánto / cuánta / cuántos / cuántas (how much, how many), por qué (why), cuál / cuáles (which). The answer porque (because) is one word without an accent. Written questions open with ¿ and close with ?.

Remember

Written questions open with ¿ and close with ?. ¿Dónde está el museo? ¿Qué es esto? Por qué is 'why'; porque (one word, no accent) is 'because'.

Key terms: question (Spanish), question word.

Logic · Validity, soundness and two classic fallacies

30 min · Seminar rotation: Logic

Learn

Key terms

validity
Definition
A property of an argument’s form: the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are true. Validity does not require the premises to be actually true.
Origin
From valid + -ity, from Middle French validité, from Latin validitās, from validus, from valēre (‘to be strong’).
Example
‘All fish can fly; sharks are fish; so sharks can fly’ is valid (and unsound). If the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be true.
Not this
Valid does not mean ‘true’, and it does not mean ‘persuasive’. A valid argument can have a false conclusion if a premise is false.
Connects to
Day 1’s conditionals are the forms being tested: modus ponens and modus tollens are valid; affirming the consequent is not.
soundness
Definition
The property of an argument that is valid and whose premises are actually true. Only then must the conclusion be true in the world.
Origin
From sound in the sense ‘whole, reliable’ + -ness. (Not the same word as musical sound.)
Example
‘If a triangle is equilateral, it is isosceles. This triangle is not isosceles. So it is not equilateral’ can be sound, because the form is modus tollens and the first premise is true.
Not this
Sound is not a synonym of valid. ‘All birds can fly; penguins are birds; so penguins can fly’ is valid but unsound.
Connects to
Validity is about form (Day 1’s truth tables). Soundness adds a check on the facts.
fallacy
Definition
A tempting but invalid form of argument. Two classics: affirming the consequent, and denying the antecedent.
Origin
From Latin fallācia, from fallere (‘to deceive, to be mistaken’).
Example
‘If the plate is accurate, the proportions match. The proportions match. So the plate is accurate.’ That is affirming the consequent, and it is invalid.
Not this
A fallacy is not ‘any false conclusion’, and a true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacy.
Connects to
The contrapositive (Day 1) is safe. Affirming the consequent looks similar and is not.

An argument is valid if its conclusion must be true whenever its premises are true. It is sound if it is valid and its premises are actually true. Validity depends only on form.

form pattern valid?
modus ponens p → q; p; therefore q valid
modus tollens p → q; ¬q; therefore ¬p valid
affirming the consequent p → q; q; therefore p invalid
denying the antecedent p → q; ¬p; therefore ¬q invalid

Remember

An argument is valid if the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are. It is sound if it is valid and the premises are actually true. Valid forms: modus ponens, modus tollens. Invalid: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent. Validity is about form, not about whether you like the conclusion.

Key terms: validity, soundness, fallacy.

Watch me

Naming a form, as a model.

  1. 'If the plate is accurate, the proportions match. The proportions match. So the plate is accurate.' That is affirming the consequent (p → q; q; therefore p), and it is invalid: the proportions might match by chance in a drawing that is wrong elsewhere.
  2. 'If a triangle is equilateral, it is isosceles. This triangle is not isosceles. So it is not equilateral.' That is modus tollens, and it is valid.
  3. Valid does not yet mean sound: you still have to check the premises.
