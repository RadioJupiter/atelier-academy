Hint 2: Ēgredior

Mathematics · Friday mix: the law of cosines, with last week’s law of sines as a check

40 min · Friday mix · invented figures labeled invented; degrees only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the law of cosines — Day 136
  • the law of sines — Day 133

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday mixes the law of cosines in both directions, and uses last week’s law of sines only as a check when a side and its opposite angle are both known. Recovering the Pythagorean theorem when C is 90° is part of the mix, not a new first look. Radians and the ambiguous case remain reserved. Invented figures stay labeled invented.

Remember

c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, and cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab). When C is 90°, the first formula is the Pythagorean theorem.

Watch me

On an invented 30°-30°-120° triangle with equal sides 4, find the side opposite 120° by the law of cosines, and check it with the law of sines.

  1. By cosines: c² = 16 + 16 − 2·4·4·(−1/2) = 48, so c = 4√3.
  2. By sines: 4 / sin 30° = c / sin 120°. Sin 30° = 1/2 and sin 120° = √3/2, so 4 / (1/2) = c / (√3/2), hence c = 4√3.
  3. The two laws agree. The triangle is invented.

Together

On an invented triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, what is the angle opposite 5, and why is that the Pythagorean theorem read in the other direction?

Hint 1: The cosine

cos C = (9 + 16 − 25)/24 = 0, so C = 90°.

Hint 2: The special case

c² = a² + b² is the same statement as a 90° included or opposite angle.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. State the law of cosines, and say what it becomes when C is 90°.
  2. On an invented triangle with sides 4 and 4 and included angle 120°, what is the third side? Check with the law of sines.
  3. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 5, and 7, what is the angle opposite 7?

Exercises

  1. On an invented triangle with sides 5 and 5 and included angle 60°, what is the third side?
  2. On an invented 3-4-5 triangle, what is the angle opposite 5?
  3. What remains reserved?

Writing · Friday mix: a maxim distinguished from a chreia, a narrative, and a fable

40 min · Friday mix · set 1733 precept, plus a short invented maxim

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • maxim (progymnasmata) — Day 136
  • chreia (progymnasmata) — Day 131

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday distinguishes the four progymnasmata already taught. A fable (week 25) is a short story, often with animals, that ends with a stated moral. A narrative (week 26) tells a complete event in order, without a required moral or precept. A chreia (week 27) needs a person, an occasion, and a saying or act, with a short reason tied to that occasion. A maxim is a short general precept, not tied to one person on one occasion. The set example remains “Eat to live, and not live to eat.” from Poor Richard, 1733. An original maxim stays labeled invented. A full later elaboration remains reserved.

Remember

A maxim is a general precept; a chreia needs a person and an occasion; a narrative tells an event in order; a fable carries a moral, often after an animal story.

Watch me

Sort four short items: the 1733 line “Eat to live, and not live to eat.”; Diogenes’ saying to Alexander; a retelling of Townsend’s Crow and the Pitcher with its moral; and a short account of a walk told in order with no moral.

  1. The 1733 line is a maxim. Diogenes’ saying, on that occasion, is a chreia. The Crow and the Pitcher with its moral is a fable. The walk in order with no moral is a narrative.
  2. Do not call the 1733 line a chreia about Franklin.
  3. Do not call the walk a maxim only because it might suggest a lesson; a maxim states the precept.

Together

Why is an invented maxim still not a chreia, even if it is good advice?

Hint 1: What it lacks

It has no named person and no occasion.

Hint 2: The label

It is also invented classroom work, not a sourced saying on a day.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Quote the set maxim in the 1733 wording, and say what makes it a maxim.
  2. Distinguish a maxim from a chreia, a narrative, and a fable’s moral.
  3. Write one short invented maxim, labeled invented, and say what it must not become.

Exercises

  1. Which of the four already-taught exercises would you use for a general precept with no speaker and no day?
  2. Why must “mite,” “ancle,” and the 1733 capitals stay inside quotations when those wordings are used?
  3. What remains reserved?

Friday mixes the first-voyage letter, in Major’s 1870 wording, with last week’s Treaty of Tordesillas as a later contrast. The letter is a voyage report to Luis de Santangel: “in thirty-three days' time I reached the Indies”; the first island is named San Salvador, “the Indians call it Guanaham”; Juana is at first taken for “the mainland,—the province of Cathay,” then treated as an island; a further island is named “La Española,” at “eighteen leagues” from a cape after “one hundred and seven leagues” eastward. Neither set of figures is converted into an official modern length or longitude, and Major’s footnotes are not taught as official identifications. Inter Caetera remains earlier bulls, already taught, not today’s first look.

Remember

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: Gray 1918, the seventh cervical vertebra, a first look

40 min · Method: Gray 1918 via Theodora; vertebra prominens; no official lengths; not a diagnosis

Learn

Key terms

the seventh cervical vertebra (vertebra prominens)
Definition
In Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 1918, “The Cervical Vertebrae,” the seventh cervical vertebra is distinguished by “a long and prominent spinous process, hence the name vertebra prominens.” The process “is thick, nearly horizontal in direction, not bifurcated, but terminating in a tubercle to which the lower end of the ligamentum nuchæ is attached.” Official lengths remain reserved.
Example
Gray notes that the foramen transversarium “is generally smaller on one or both sides; occasionally it is double, sometimes it is absent,” and that “the usual arrangement is for both artery and vein to pass in front of the transverse process, and not through the foramen.”
Not this
Not the atlas, which has no body and no spinous process in the ordinary sense already taught. Not the axis, whose distinctive mark is the odontoid process. Not a medical diagnosis, and not a body described from life. Not an official length.
Connects to
The already-taught cervical vertebra and foramen transversarium, the atlas, and the axis, all from Gray 1918 as posted at Theodora. Official lengths remain reserved.

Henry Gray, Anatomy of the Human Body, 20th ed., ed. Warren H. Lewis, 1918, “The Cervical Vertebrae,” as posted at https://www.theodora.com/anatomy/the_cervical_vertebrae.html, has already supplied a typical cervical vertebra, the atlas, and the axis. Today the seventh. “The most distinctive characteristic of this vertebra is the existence of a long and prominent spinous process, hence the name vertebra prominens.” “This process is thick, nearly horizontal in direction, not bifurcated, but terminating in a tubercle to which the lower end of the ligamentum nuchæ is attached.” “Ligamentum nuchæ” is Gray’s wording and stays as the source names it.

“The transverse processes are of considerable size, their posterior roots are large and prominent, while the anterior are small and faintly marked.” “The foramen transversarium may be as large as that in the other cervical vertebræ, but is generally smaller on one or both sides; occasionally it is double, sometimes it is absent.” “The usual arrangement is for both artery and vein to pass in front of the transverse process, and not through the foramen.” Gray’s “sometimes” and “occasionally” stay inside the record; they are not a diagnosis, and no body is being described from life. “Sometimes the anterior root of the transverse process attains a large size and exists as a separate bone, which is known as a cervical rib,” is likewise Gray’s “sometimes,” not a classroom diagnosis. Official lengths remain reserved.

Remember

The seventh cervical vertebra is named vertebra prominens for its long, prominent, not bifurcated spinous process, in Gray 1918. Official lengths remain reserved. The lesson is not a diagnosis, and no body is being described from life.
