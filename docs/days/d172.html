Latin · A tighter mix, with ēgredior as well as sequor

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today tightens the same already-taught jobs, now with both sequor and ēgredior. No new construction. Keep ēgrediātur distinct from ēgrediētur, and sequātur from sequētur. Original classroom sentences only.

Word How to say it Meaning
ut ēgrediātur oot ay-GREH-dee-ah-toor in order that he go out, or so that he goes out, according to the job
nē ēgrediātur nay ay-GREH-dee-ah-toor in order that he not go out

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

The four jobs do not change when the verb is ēgredior. The present subjunctive is ēgrediātur, not the future ēgrediētur.

Watch me

  1. The first is a negative purpose: he sits in order that he not go out.
  2. The second is a negative result: he rises so slowly that he does not go out.
  3. Both use ēgrediātur, not ēgrediētur.

Mathematics · The derivative at a point, a first look

40 min · Method: f'(a) = lim h→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h, when the limit exists; invented f(x) = x², f'(3) = 6 and f'(2) = 4; the derivative function reserved until tomorrow

Learn

Key terms

the derivative at a point, a first look
Definition
The derivative of a function f at a number a, written f'(a), is, in this first look, the limit of the already-taught difference quotient as h approaches 0, when that limit exists: f'(a) = lim h→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h. On the invented function f(x) = x², the difference quotient at 3 simplifies to 6 + h, so f'(3) = lim h→0 (6 + h) = 6. At 2, the difference quotient simplifies to 4 + h, so f'(2) = 4. The English noun derivative is from Middle French dérivatif, from Latin dērīvātus, the perfect passive participle of dērīvō, “to derive,” as given on Wiktionary. The function and the chosen numbers are invented. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. The derivative as a function of x waits until tomorrow.
Origin
From Middle French dérivatif, from Latin dērīvātus, the perfect passive participle of dērīvō, “to derive,” as given on Wiktionary, https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/derivative.
Example
On invented f(x) = x², f'(3) = 6, and f'(2) = 4. Those values happen to be twice the chosen number, but the general rule f'(x) = 2x is tomorrow’s job, after the same calculation is done at a general x.
Not this
Not yesterday’s difference quotient as if the limit had already been taken. Not a formal epsilon argument. Not ∞ treated as a number to substitute. Not the general derivative function, which waits until tomorrow. Not a claim about official data.
Connects to
Yesterday’s difference quotient on invented f(x) = x². Tomorrow takes the same limit at a general x, and names the derivative function.

Yesterday’s difference quotient of invented f(x) = x², at 3, simplified to 6 + h, for h ≠ 0. Today’s first look takes the limit of that quotient as h approaches 0, when the limit exists, and names the result the derivative of f at a, written f'(a). So f'(a) = lim h→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h. On the invented function, f'(3) = lim h→0 (6 + h) = 6. At 2, yesterday’s arithmetic already gave the quotient 4 + h, so f'(2) = lim h→0 (4 + h) = 4. Those two values happen to be twice the chosen number, but the general rule for every x waits until tomorrow, when the same calculation is done at a general x. Remember

Definition
OpenStax official Example 20.8 uses a book example rate of 12 cents per kWh, not a named utility rate, and asks for the total cost of using a 60-W incandescent bulb for 1000 hours if the bulb cost 25 cents. The official arithmetic is E = P t = (60 W)(1000 h) = 60,000 W·h = 60.0 kWh, then cost = (60.0 kWh)($0.12/kWh) = $7.20, and the total, including the 25-cent bulb, is $7.45 for 1000 hours. Those figures are official book example figures, not a real named utility bill. Part (b), on a compact fluorescent light, waits until Thursday.
