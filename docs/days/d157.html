Latin · A purpose clause with ut and ēgredior, a first look

40 min · Method: ut + ēgrediātur, contrasted with ēgrediētur and with ut sequātur; original sentences; nē reserved

Learn

Key terms

a purpose clause with ut and ēgredior
Definition
A purpose clause may also use the already-taught present subjunctive of ēgredior: ut ēgrediātur, in order that he go out, or in order that she go out. It is not the future ēgrediētur, and not yesterday’s ut sequātur, a different verb.
Example
In an original sentence, Puer surgit ut ēgrediātur: he rises in order that he go out. The verb in the ut-clause is ēgrediātur, not ēgrediētur, and not sequātur.
Not this
Not ēgrediētur, he will go out, or she will go out. Not ut sequātur, yesterday’s purpose clause with sequor. Not a negative purpose with nē, which remains reserved.
Connects to
Yesterday’s purpose clause with ut and sequor, and last week’s present subjunctive of ēgredior.

Yesterday’s purpose clause used ut and sequātur. Today the same job is done with ēgredior: ut ēgrediātur, in order that he go out, or in order that she go out. Puer surgit ut ēgrediātur: he rises in order that he go out. The ending is -iātur, not the future -iētur, and the verb is not sequor. The sentences are original. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the gloss. A negative purpose with nē remains reserved.

Mathematics · Algebra / analysis: a limit where the function is not defined at the point, a first look

40 min · Method: (x² − 4)/(x − 2) approaches 4 as x approaches 2, though it is not defined at 2; invented table; continuity reserved

Learn

Key terms

a limit where the function is not defined at the point
Definition
A limit may exist at a number a even when f(a) itself is not defined. For the invented function f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2), with x ≠ 2, the expression simplifies to x + 2, so as x approaches 2 the values approach 4, and lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4, even though the original expression is not defined at x = 2.
Example
On an invented table, x = 1.9, 1.99, 2.01, and 2.1 give f(x) = 3.9, 3.99, 4.01, and 4.1, which approach 4. The function is not defined at 2, because the denominator would be zero. The table is invented and labeled invented.
Not this
Not a claim that the function is defined at 2. Not a first look at continuity, which remains reserved. Not a formal epsilon argument, which remains reserved. Not an official limit of measured real data.
Connects to
Yesterday’s limit as approaching, and already-taught factoring of a difference of squares.

Yesterday’s limit was a value approached, even when substitution happened to agree. Today a limit may exist at a number a even when f(a) is not defined. Take the invented function f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2), for x ≠ 2. The numerator is a difference of squares, already taught, so (x − 2)(x + 2)/(x − 2) = x + 2 when x ≠ 2. As x approaches 2, x + 2 approaches 4. So lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4, even though the original expression is not defined at x = 2, where the denominator would be zero. On an invented table, x = 1.9, 1.99, 2.01, and 2.1 give 3.9, 3.99, 4.01, and 4.1, which approach 4. The table is invented and labeled invented. Continuity remains reserved. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved.

Remember

A limit may exist at a even when f(a) is not defined. For the invented f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2), x ≠ 2, the values approach 4 as x approaches 2, so the limit is 4. The function is not defined at 2.

Hint 1: The job

Last week’s first look was duple time, one-two, one-two. Binary form is a piece in two parts, A and B.

Hint 2: The score

The figure is original classroom notes. A copyrighted score is not copied, and is not the source of this invented example.

Use today's key terms (binary form on an invented figure). For each term, close the card and then: (1) explain it in your own words; (2) give a new example, not the one on the card; (3) name a near-miss — something it is easily confused with — and say how you would tell them apart.

Hint 1: Own words

Start from the definition, but do not copy it. If you can only repeat the card, close it and try again from the example you invent.

Hint 2: Near-miss

The "Not this" line on the card names a common mix-up. Explain why that mix-up would change an answer in this lesson.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. What is binary form in this lesson, and how is the invented figure arranged?
  2. Why is last week’s march in duple time a miss if the job is binary form as a first look?
  3. Why is a copyrighted score a miss?
  4. For each of today's key terms (binary form on an invented figure), write one sentence in your own words and one near-miss you must not confuse it with. Then apply one of the terms to a fresh case of your own (not the example on the card).

Exercises

  1. On the invented figure, what does each part need in order to be a part of a binary form, and what remains reserved?
  2. Could an invented march later be written in binary form, and would that make last week’s lesson already this first look?
  3. Why is an official analysis of a real published work a miss?
