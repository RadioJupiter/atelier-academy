Latin · Capitulum VI, part 1: prepositions with the accusative

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VI · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms

preposition (with the accusative)
Definition
In Capitulum VI a new closed list of prepositions takes the accusative: ad (to, toward), ante (in front of, before), post (behind, after), inter (between, among), prope (near), circum (around), apud (at the house of, with), per (through).
Example
ad vīllam (to the country house); prope Rōmam (near Rome); inter oppidum et vīllam (between the town and the country house).
Not this
These are not interchangeable with yesterday's ablative prepositions. ad takes the accusative; ab takes the ablative. English ‘to’ and ‘from’ are a useful pair, but the Latin case is fixed by the preposition.
Connects to
Capitulum V: in, ex, ab, cum, sine + ablative. Capitulum VI: this new list + accusative. The two lists face each other.
ad and ab
Definition
A pair of opposites: ad + accusative means ‘to, toward’; ab + accusative is wrong — ab takes the ablative and means ‘away from’.
Example
Servus ab oppidō ad vīllam ambulat. The slave walks from the town to the country house.
Not this
ad vīllā and ab vīllam are both ungrammatical. The preposition, not the English word, chooses the case.
Connects to
The same opposition will later be even sharper with town names, which drop the preposition altogether (Day 16).

Capitulum VI moves the family onto the road. New prepositions take the accusative (the case you already know from Capitulum III).

Mathematics · Geometry VII: the parallelogram

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry, quadrilaterals

Learn

Key terms

parallelogram
Definition
A quadrilateral whose opposite sides are parallel (both pairs). In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal, opposite angles are equal, consecutive angles are supplementary, and the diagonals bisect each other.
Origin
From Latin parallelogrammum, from Ancient Greek παράλληλος (‘parallel’) + γραμμή (‘line’).
Example
If ∠A = 70° in parallelogram ABCD, then ∠C = 70° and ∠B = ∠D = 110°.
Not this
A parallelogram is not ‘any slanted rectangle’, and a quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides is a trapezoid in this course, not a parallelogram.
Connects to
Day 6 (parallels and a transversal) is what proves the angle facts. The diagonal of a parallelogram cuts it into two congruent triangles.
diagonal
Definition
A segment joining two vertices of a polygon that are not already joined by a side. In a parallelogram the two diagonals bisect each other: they meet at their midpoints.
Origin
From Middle French diagonal, from Latin diagōnālis, from Ancient Greek διαγώνιος (‘from angle to angle’), from διά (‘across’) + γωνία (‘angle’).
Example
If the diagonals of parallelogram ABCD meet at O and AO = 5, BO = 7, then AC = 10 and BD = 14.
Not this
A diagonal is not a side, and it is not true that the diagonals of every parallelogram are equal (that happens in a rectangle).
Connects to
The two triangles made by one diagonal can be proved congruent by ASA or AAS, using alternate interior angles from Day 6.

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel.

Theorems (to be used, and proved in the exercises). In a parallelogram: 1. Opposite sides are equal, and opposite angles are equal. 2. Consecutive angles are supplementary (they add to 180°), because they are consecutive interior angles on a transversal of parallels. 3. Each diagonal divides the parallelogram into two congruent triangles. 4. The diagonals bisect each other.

A converse worth having. If the diagonals of a quadrilateral bisect each other, the quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

Draw first. Label the figure, mark the equal sides or the equal angles you actually know, and only then write an equation.

Remember

Parallelogram: opposite sides parallel and equal; opposite angles equal; consecutive angles supplementary; diagonals bisect each other. A rectangle is the special case with a right angle (tomorrow).

Key terms: parallelogram, diagonal.

Watch me

In parallelogram ABCD, ∠A = 70°. Find the other three angles, with a reason for each.

  1. AB ∥ DC, and AD is a transversal, so consecutive interior angles add to 180°. Thus ∠D = 180° − 70° = 110°.
  2. Opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal, so ∠C = ∠A = 70°, and ∠B = ∠D = 110°.
  3. Check: 70 + 110 + 70 + 110 = 360. ✓

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method: hints from a new model

45 min · Method: Benjamin Franklin's reconstruction exercise; model: the opening of Samuel Johnson, The History of Rasselas, Prince of Abyssinia, chapter 1 (1759, public domain)

Learn

Key terms

hint (for reconstruction)
Definition
A brief cue — a word or a short phrase — written down in order, after you have read a model and turned it over, so that you can later rebuild the passage from the cues rather than from memory of the exact wording.
Example
From a sentence about a prince who is confined to a pleasant valley, the hints might be: prince — valley — every pleasure — still restless.
Not this
A hint is not a complete outline, and it is not a quotation. If the hint is itself a copied clause, the exercise collapses into memorization.
Connects to
Day 4 used hints on Addison. The method is the same; the model is new. Reconstruction itself is Friday's work (Day 17).
period (a long constructed sentence)
Definition
A sentence that holds several related clauses in a designed order, often delaying the main point until the thought is complete. Johnson frequently writes in periods.
Example
A Johnson sentence may name a condition, add a second condition, and only then state what follows from both.
Not this
A period is not ‘any long sentence’. A sentence that is long because it rambles, or because commas splice new thoughts, is not a period.
Connects to
When you write hints, mark the main verb of the period so the reconstruction has a spine, not just a bag of nouns.

Read the first paragraph of Chapter 1 of Johnson’s Rasselas (1759; public domain). Then turn the book over.

Make hints, in order. For each sentence, write a short row of cues: the main actors, the main verb, and one or two necessary circumstances. Do not copy clauses.

Notice the periods. Johnson often builds a sentence that does not end at the first pause. In the margin, mark where the main verb arrives.

Do not reconstruct yet. Reconstruction from these hints is the work of Day 17. Today the skill is to make hints you could actually rebuild from.

Remember

Read the model; turn it over; write ordered hints (actors, main verb, necessary circumstance) that are not copied clauses. Mark the spine of any long period. Save the hints for Day 17.

Science · Autoxidation and the oil film

45 min · Method: from the phenomenon to a chemical model, with a check against the smears

Learn

Key terms

autoxidation
Definition
A reaction in which a substance combines with oxygen in the air, without being set on fire. In a drying oil, oxygen attacks the unsaturated parts of the oil’s molecules and begins a process that links those molecules together.
Origin
An English formation from auto- (here, ‘self’ or ‘spontaneous’) + oxidation.
Example
A thin linseed-oil film, open to the air, thickens and becomes firm; the same oil in a tightly closed bottle stays liquid much longer.
Not this
Autoxidation is not burning with a flame, and it is not evaporation. It can be sped up by heat, by light, and (historically) by certain metal compounds used as ‘driers’, but those are aids, not a different process.
Connects to
Yesterday’s inference — that the film needs the air and does not shrink away — is what this reaction explains.
polymerization
Definition
A process in which small molecules join into much larger ones (polymers). In a drying oil, after oxygen has started to react with the oil, the molecules become cross-linked and the liquid becomes a solid network, the paint film.
Origin
From polymerize + -ation. Polymer is from Greek πολύς (‘many’) + μέρος (‘part’).
Example
The firm oil smear is not a pile of the original liquid molecules with the water gone; it is a new, linked solid.
Not this
Polymerization here is not the same as the making of modern plastics in a factory, though the word is the same, and it is not ‘drying’ in the sense of wetness leaving. The everyday word ‘dry’ is the painter’s word; the chemical word is this one.
Connects to
The module title ‘why oil paint dries’ is now answered: the oil reacts with oxygen and the molecules link into a film.

The oil film does not leave as a vapor. It reacts with oxygen in the air: autoxidation. The oil’s molecules have places (unsaturated bonds in fatty-acid chains) that can take up oxygen. Those reactions then allow the molecules to link together (polymerization, with cross-links), and the liquid becomes a solid network — the film.

This is a model, not a complete industrial chemistry course. What you need to use:

  1. The film needs oxygen, so a thick lump stays wet inside longer than a thin smear (oxygen has farther to go).
  2. Heat and light can speed the reaction; a closed bottle slows it.
  3. Some historical ‘driers’ (for example, certain lead compounds, now avoided because they are toxic) were added to speed the same process, not to make the oil evaporate.
  4. A watercolor film is still mainly evaporation; do not collapse the two.

Check the model against this week’s smears: the oil smear should still be present, and more firm, if enough time has passed. If it is not yet firm, record that honestly; a thin film in a cool room can be slow.

Remember

A drying oil forms a film by autoxidation (reaction with oxygen) followed by polymerization (the molecules link into a solid network). That is not evaporation. A thick film dries more slowly inside because oxygen has farther to go.

Key terms: autoxidation, polymerization.

Watch me

Why does a thick lump of oil paint often stay soft inside after the surface is already firm?

  1. The reaction needs oxygen from the air.
  2. Oxygen reaches the surface first, so the surface can form a film while the interior is still short of oxygen.
  3. The model predicts a firm skin and a softer interior in a thick lump — which is what painters actually meet. Evaporation alone does not explain a skin of this kind as cleanly.

Literature · Iliad, Books 11–16: Patroclus

45 min · Method: close reading · Read Books 11–16 before this lesson (Book 10, the night raid, may be read, but today’s work is the wounding of the chiefs, the wall, and Patroclus)

Learn

Key terms

aristeia
Definition
A hero’s run of excellence in battle, a stretch of the poem in which one fighter dominates the action. The word is Greek for ‘excellence’ or ‘best moment’ (you met it as a name in Week 2’s summary of Book 5, Diomedes).
Example
In these books, several Greeks have such a run and then are wounded or stopped; Patroclus has his own aristeia in Book 16, which ends in his death.
Not this
An aristeia is not ‘the whole war’, and it is not a guarantee of survival. Patroclus’s best moment is also the path to his death.
Connects to
Diomedes in Book 5 was an aristeia that did not end the poem. Patroclus’s aristeia will force Achilles back, but not yet in this lesson.
Patroclus in Achilles’ armor
Definition
The decision, in Book 16, that Patroclus will put on Achilles’ armor, lead the Myrmidons, and drive the Trojans from the ships, but go no further — a command Achilles gives and Patroclus does not keep.
Example
The armor works as a scare and as a disguise at a distance; it does not make Patroclus into Achilles, and Hector will later strip it from the body.
Not this
This is not a ‘costume change’ for variety, and it is not Achilles returning. The poem makes the difference tragic: the friend is not the hero, and the limit is spoken and then crossed.
Connects to
Book 9 left Achilles withdrawn. The ships on fire (Book 15–16) are the pressure that makes him yield this much, and no more.

Read Books 11–16. (Book 10, the night raid of Odysseus and Diomedes, is part of the tradition of the Iliad; some readers treat it as distinctive in tone. Today’s discussion can set it to one side if your reading time was short, but do not invent a reason to skip 11–16.)

The turn. Book 11 wounds the great Greek chiefs. The wall and the ships come under heavier pressure. Achilles, watching, yields not himself but Patroclus, in Achilles’ armor, with a limit: save the ships, do not go on to the city. Patroclus drives the Trojans back, kills Sarpedon, and goes on. Hector, with Apollo’s help, kills him. The book ends with a fight already starting over the body.

Books 11–16: the chiefs are wounded, the ships are in danger, Patroclus goes out in Achilles’ armor with a limit he does not keep, and Hector kills him. An aristeia is a hero’s run of excellence; it does not guarantee survival. The armor is not Achilles’ return.

Atelier · Cross-hatching and control of value

45 min · Method: a second layer of hatch; the solids already begun, or a new study of one solid · After Day 13

Learn

Key terms

cross-hatching
Definition
A second (or later) layer of hatch laid across the first, at a new angle, to deepen value or to tighten form. The new strokes still follow the surface, or they cross the first layer at a shallow angle; they do not cancel the form-following of the first layer.
Example
In a core shadow that is still too light, a second hatch, crossing the first at a slight angle and still curving with the sphere or running with a plane, can darken the belt without a smear.
Not this
Cross-hatching is not a grid drawn as a decoration, and it is not a way to hide a wrong contour. If the first layer is careless, a second layer will not invent the form.
Connects to
Day 13’s single hatch is still the foundation. Today you add a layer only where the value must go down, or where the form needs a firmer turn.
control of value
Definition
The skill of making a patch as light or as dark as you intend, and of keeping a family even where the form is even. On a nine-step scale (Day 4), you should be able to place a hatch at a chosen step and not overshoot into a black that you cannot lift.
Example
A mid-gray halftone stays mid-gray, even after a second hatch; the core shadow is darker, but still a form, not a hole; the reflected light stays lighter than the core.
Not this
Control is not ‘as dark as the pencil can go’, and it is not a single formula of three fixed layers. You stop when the relation is right.
Connects to
The light and shadow families (Day 8) are the test: if cross-hatching has broken the families, you have gone too far.

Yesterday’s hatch was a single layer. Today you add cross-hatching only where you need it, and you practice control.

A shallow angle. The second layer should not sit at a loud 90° grid unless the form really supports a crossing that still feels like surface. A shallow cross, still running with the form, usually sits more quietly.

Where to add a layer. In the core shadow; in a cast shadow that is too weak; in a halftone that has not yet turned enough. Not in the highlight. Not as a blanket over the whole drawing.

The scale as a check. Put a small nine-step strip in the margin (Day 4). Before you darken a passage, say which step you are aiming at, lay the hatch, and compare. If you overshoot, you can sometimes lighten by careful lifting, but it is better to approach the step in two modest layers than in one heavy one.

The solid. Continue yesterday’s sphere, or make a new study of the cube or the cylinder, using hatch on planes (almost straight, in the plane) and on curves (turning). The cube’s plane may take a fairly even hatch; a cross on a plane should still lie on that plane.

A common error. A wire fence of crossings that sits in front of the form, or a black core with no reflected light left.

Remember

Cross-hatching is a second form-following layer, at a shallow angle, used only where value must go down. Control means hitting an intended step on the value scale and keeping the families. Do not cover a highlight, and do not hide a bad contour.

Key terms: cross-hatching, control of value.

Watch me

Modern language · Spanish: direct-object pronouns

30 min · Method: explicit placement rule, then substitution drills · Instruction in English

Learn

Key terms

direct-object pronoun
Definition
A small word that stands in for a direct object already known: me, te, lo, la, nos, os, los, las. In a simple present sentence it stands just before the conjugated verb.
Origin
Pronoun is from pro- + noun, modeled on Middle French pronom, from Latin pronomen, a calque of Ancient Greek ἀντωνυμία.
Example
¿Ves el libro? Sí, lo veo. ¿Tienes la carta? La tengo. Te veo. Nos entienden.
Not this
A direct-object pronoun is not the subject pronoun (yo, tú, él). *Lo veo* is not ‘he sees’; it is ‘I see him/it’, with yo understood or added: *Yo lo veo*.
Connects to
You have been using direct objects as nouns since the first week (*Veo la casa*). Today the noun can be replaced once it is known.
placement of the direct-object pronoun
Definition
Before a conjugated verb: Lo veo. Attached to an infinitive, or placed before the conjugated verb that governs the infinitive: Voy a verlo or Lo voy a ver. Both of those last two are correct.
Example
Quiero leer el libro. → Quiero leerlo. or Lo quiero leer.
Not this
Do not leave a gap and do not put the pronoun after a plain conjugated verb (*Veo lo* is wrong in this course).
Connects to
Day 9’s ir a + infinitive now has a choice of two correct places for the pronoun.

A direct object is what the verb acts on. When that object is already known, Spanish often replaces it with a pronoun.

The forms. me, te, lo/la, nos, os, los/las.

Before a conjugated verb. Veo el libro. → Lo veo. No veo las casas. → No las veo. (The no stays first.) ¿Tienes la pluma? Sí, la tengo.

With an infinitive. The pronoun may attach to the infinitive or stand before the conjugated verb: Voy a verlo or Lo voy a ver. Quiero comprarlo or Lo quiero comprar.

Match lo/los to a masculine noun and la/las to a feminine noun. El libro → lo; la lección → la; las cartas → las.

Word How to say it Meaning
me meh me (direct object)
te teh you, informal (direct object)
lo loh him / it (masculine)
la lah her / it (feminine)
nos nohs us
los lohs them (masculine or mixed)
las lahs them (feminine)

Subject pronouns are still optional: Yo lo veo and Lo veo can both be right. Do not confuse lo with él.

Remember

me, te, lo/la, nos, os, los/las. Place the pronoun before a conjugated verb (Lo veo). With an infinitive: Verlo or Lo voy a ver. Gender and number match the noun replaced.

Economics & personal finance · Markets and prices

30 min · Method: explicit definitions, then classroom examples clearly labeled as examples · After Day 4 (scarcity and opportunity cost)

Learn

Key terms

market
Definition
A meeting of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service, in which they can trade. It need not be a single building or a single hour; it is the arrangement that lets offers to buy and offers to sell meet.
Origin
From Middle English market, from late Old English and Anglo-Norman, ultimately from Latin mercātus (‘trade, market’), from mercor (‘to trade, to buy’), from merx (‘wares’).
Example
The market for pencils in a town includes shops, an online listing, and a school store, if those are places where pencils are actually offered and bought.
Not this
A market is not only a covered hall, and it is not ‘the economy’ as a whole. There is a market for a good, not one single market for everything.
Connects to
Day 4’s scarcity is why a market has work to do: goods are limited, and people have alternative uses for their time and money.
price (as a signal)
Definition
The amount of money (or other payment) asked or agreed for a good or service, which also serves as a signal: a high price discourages some buying and encourages some selling and production; a low price does the reverse, other things equal.
Origin
From Middle English price, from Old French pris, from Latin pretium (‘worth, price, reward’).
Example
If a storm ruins a fruit crop, the price of that fruit tends to rise. Buyers are pushed to use less or to switch; sellers are pushed to bring more of what remains to market, and later to plant more, if they can.
Not this
A price is not a moral grade (‘expensive means better’), and it is not by itself a proof of a bargain. It is information, mixed with other information.
Connects to
This lesson does not yet draw supply and demand curves (Day 19). It asks what a price does in a market.

A market is where buyers and sellers of a good or service meet, not only a building with a sign.

Trade. A trade happens when a buyer and a seller agree on a price and exchange. If they cannot agree, the trade does not happen. That fact is already an economic event: the good stays with the seller, and the buyer keeps the money for something else (opportunity cost, Day 4).

Price as a signal. Other things equal, a higher price tends to mean: buyers take less, sellers are willing to offer more. A lower price tends to mean the reverse. The phrase other things equal matters: if tastes, income, or the quality of the good also change, the story is not about the price alone.

Classroom example (invented, not a news claim). A school canteen sells the same sandwich every day. One week the price is raised. Some students buy fewer sandwiches and bring food from home; the canteen, if it can, prepares more only if the higher price still leaves them buyers — the full version of that thought is Day 19. Today, write what the new price is telling buyers, and what it is telling the canteen.

What this lesson does not do. It does not claim that every market is fair, or that price is the only thing that matters (rules, information, and power also shape a market). It teaches the tool first.

Remember

A market is the meeting of buyers and sellers of a good, not only a building. A price is a payment and a signal: other things equal, a higher price discourages some buying and encourages some selling. ‘Other things equal’ is part of the claim.

Key terms: market, price (as a signal).
