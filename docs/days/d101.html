Latin · Deponents in the first and second persons, present tense

40 min · Method: a first look at first- and second-person present deponents (loquor, loqueris). The third person and the infinitive are already taught

Learn

Key terms

first- and second-person present deponents
Definition
A deponent verb, already taught in the third person and the infinitive, also has first- and second-person present forms that look passive and are translated as active. For loquor, to speak: loquor, loqueris, loquitur, loquimur, loquiminī, loquuntur. The endings are the ordinary present passive endings already met on the page as forms, now used as the deponent’s whole present table.
Example
Ego loquor Lātīnē. Tū loqueris clārē. Nōs loquimur; vōs loquiminī. Cōnor, cōnāris, cōnātur follow the same first-conjugation deponent pattern: I try, you try, he or she tries.
Not this
Not a first look at loquitur, cōnātur, sequitur, or ēgreditur, already taught in the third person. Not a first look at the perfect of a deponent (locūtus sum), which is reserved. Classroom sentences only, not copied from Ørberg.
Connects to
Week 18’s third-person deponents and infinitives, and last week’s deponent participles in the absolute.

For two weeks, deponents have been loquitur, cōnātur, sequitur, ēgreditur, and their participles. The missing persons of the present are taught today.

Loquor, loqueris, loquitur, loquimur, loquiminī, loquuntur. The forms look passive, as deponents do, and are translated as active: I speak, you speak, he or she speaks, we speak, you (plural) speak, they speak.

Cōnor, cōnāris, cōnātur, cōnāmur, cōnāminī, cōnantur: I try, you try, and so on. The first conjugation keeps -ā- before these endings, as cōnātur already showed.

Classroom sentences only. The perfect, locūtus sum and the rest, remains reserved.

Mathematics · Geometry LV: the volume of a frustum of a right cone

45 min · Method: a frustum is what remains when a smaller similar cone is cut from a larger one; its volume is the difference of the two cone volumes, which is also V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²)

Learn

Key terms

volume of a frustum of a right cone
Definition
A frustum of a right cone is the solid that remains when a smaller cone, similar to the original, is cut away by a plane parallel to the base. Its volume is the large cone’s volume minus the small cone’s volume, both already taught as V = (1/3)πr²h. If the frustum has lower radius R, upper radius r, and height h, that difference is also V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²). The small cone is similar to the large one, so their heights and radii are in the same ratio, from the already-taught scale factor.
Example
A classroom large cone has radius 6 and height 8, invented lengths. A similar small cone of height 4 then has radius 3. The frustum between them has height 4, volume (1/3)π×36×8 minus (1/3)π×9×4, which is 96π − 12π = 84π cubic units. The formula gives the same: (1/3)π×4×(36 + 18 + 9) = 84π.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s cone volume, or at similar triangles and a scale factor, already taught. Not today’s later job, the surface of a frustum. Classroom lengths are invented. Exact answers stay in terms of π. No official monument’s volume is invented.
Connects to
Last week’s V = (1/3)πr²h, Day 61’s similar triangles, and Day 63’s scale factor.

Last week’s cone can have a smaller similar cone cut from the top, by a plane parallel to the base. What remains is a frustum.

A classroom large cone has radius R = 6 and height 8, invented lengths. A similar cone has sides in the same ratio, already taught as a scale factor. If the small cone has height 4, half of 8, its radius is half of 6, so r = 3. The frustum has height 4.

Volume of the large cone: (1/3)π × 36 × 8 = 96π. Volume of the small cone: (1/3)π × 9 × 4 = 12π. The frustum is the difference: 84π cubic units, left in terms of π.

The same volume is V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²) = (1/3)π × 4 × (36 + 18 + 9) = 84π. Use either the difference of two cones, or this formula, once the similar heights and radii are in place. Do not invent an official monument’s volume.

Remember

A frustum of a right cone is a large cone minus a smaller similar cone. V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²), with h the height of the frustum. Classroom lengths are invented. Exact answers stay in terms of π.

Key terms: volume of a frustum of a right cone.

Physics · Damped harmonic motion: underdamped, critical, and overdamped

40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.7 — a damping force removes mechanical energy; underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped motion distinguished

Learn

Key terms

damped harmonic motion
Definition
OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.7, treats damped harmonic motion: a non-conservative damping force removes mechanical energy, usually as thermal energy. The work of that force is Wnc = Δ(KE + PE), and for damping Wnc is negative. Completely undamped motion is rare. Last week’s undamped SHM, in which ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX², is the contrast in which those losses were neglected.
Example
For a small amount of damping, OpenStax says the period and frequency are nearly the same as in simple harmonic motion, but the amplitude gradually decreases. A guitar string that stops after being plucked, and a swing that must be pushed, are OpenStax’s opening illustrations of the kind of motion, not official measured periods in this lesson.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s undamped energy equation or at T = 2π√(m/k), except as the contrast when damping is neglected. Not today’s later names, underdamped, critical, and overdamped, except as they will be taught next in this same lesson. Forced oscillations and resonance, OpenStax 16.8, are reserved.
Connects to
Last week’s undamped SHM, Day 28’s first model of mechanical energy when friction does no work, and OpenStax 16.7, https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-7-damped-harmonic-motion (CC BY).
underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped
Definition
OpenStax 16.7 distinguishes three amounts of damping. An underdamped system returns toward equilibrium but overshoots and crosses one or more times; with small damping, the period is nearly unchanged while the amplitude falls. Critical damping is the condition in which the oscillator returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible; it may overshoot once, but if it does, it will do so only once. An overdamped system also moves toward equilibrium without a train of oscillations, but it takes longer than critical damping. OpenStax’s check: an overdamped system moves slowly toward equilibrium; an underdamped system moves quickly but oscillates about equilibrium as it does so; a critically damped system moves as quickly as possible toward equilibrium without oscillating about it.
Example
OpenStax names a car’s suspension as a case where critical damping is often wanted, and a bathroom-scale needle that moves to its new reading without oscillating. Those are illustrations of the kind of motion, not official measured times in this lesson.
Not this
Not a first look at Monday’s energy-removal equation, except as already named. Not a first look at Example 16.7’s figures, which wait until Wednesday. Not forced oscillations, section 16.8, which remain reserved.
Connects to
Today’s damped harmonic motion, and OpenStax 16.7, including the section’s “Check Your Understanding.”

Last week’s SHM neglected damping, so that ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX². OpenStax 16.7 now takes the losses seriously. Completely undamped motion is rare.

A non-conservative damping force removes mechanical energy, usually as thermal energy. The work of that force is Wnc = Δ(KE + PE), and for damping Wnc is negative.

With a small amount of damping, the period and frequency are nearly the same as in undamped SHM, but the amplitude gradually decreases, as in OpenStax Figure 16.20.

If the damping is increased, the period begins to be affected. If the damping is very large, the system does not even oscillate; it slowly moves toward equilibrium. Critical damping is the condition in which it returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible. It may overshoot once, but if it does, only once. Less than that is underdamped: it overshoots and crosses one or more times. More than that is overdamped: it reaches equilibrium over a longer time than critical damping, without a train of oscillations.

OpenStax’s own short contrast: an overdamped system moves slowly toward equilibrium; an underdamped system moves quickly but oscillates about equilibrium as it does so; a critically damped system moves as quickly as possible toward equilibrium without oscillating about it.

History · The south and north arches, and the columns planed by flakes (Dewing I.1.74–76)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.74–76. Last week’s eastern arch is the finished contrast Dewing himself names

Learn

Key terms

the south and north arches (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.74–76, the eastern arch has been completed, but “in the process of building the other arches, indeed, those namely which are turned toward the south and the north, the following chanced to take place.” The so-called lōri “had been raised up, carrying the masonry of the church, but everything underneath was labouring under their load, making the columns (kiones) which stood there throw off tiny flakes, as if they had been planed.” The master-builders, “dismayed” once more, reported the problem to the Emperor.

Modern language · Spanish: the imperfect of regular -ar verbs, a first look

40 min · Method: a first look at the imperfect of regular -ar verbs (hablaba). The preterite, already taught, is a contrast, not a first look. Instruction remains in English

Learn

Key terms

imperfect of regular -ar verbs
Definition
The imperfect of a regular -ar verb uses the stem and the endings -aba, -abas, -aba, -ábamos, -abais, -aban. For hablar: hablaba, hablabas, hablaba, hablábamos, hablabais, hablaban. In this first look, the imperfect is used for an ongoing or habitual action in the past: Cada mañana yo caminaba. The already-taught preterite, hablé, is used for a completed event: Ayer yo caminé. The two tenses are not interchangeable.
Example
Cuando yo leía, ella hablaba. Todos los días practicábamos. The first-person and third-person singular have the same form, hablaba; the subject, or the ending of a longer sentence, tells them apart.
Not this
Not a first look at the preterite of regular -ar verbs, already taught. Not tomorrow’s -er and -ir imperfect. Not the irregular imperfect of ir, ser, or ver, which remains reserved. Instruction remains in English. Classroom sentences are invented and do not identify a living person.
Connects to
The preterite of regular -ar verbs (Day 61), as a contrast, and today’s new endings.

The preterite of regular -ar verbs is already taught: hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablasteis, hablaron, for a completed event. Today a second past tense begins, the imperfect, for an action that was going on, or that happened habitually, in the past.

Hablaba, hablabas, hablaba, hablábamos, hablabais, hablaban. The stem is the same as the infinitive without -ar. The first-person and third-person singular look the same: yo hablaba, ella hablaba.

Cada mañana yo caminaba. Ayer, at a finished time already taught, yo caminé. The time words help: cada mañana, todos los días, or mientras, often go with the imperfect in this first look; ayer with a finished event often goes with the preterite. Seminar · Logic: Felapton, a first look at a third-figure particular negative

40 min · Method: Felapton (EAO-3). No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. Last week’s Darapti is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

Felapton
Definition
Felapton is a valid third-figure mood, EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. The middle term is the subject of both premises, as in last week’s Darapti. The conclusion is particular and negative: some S are not P, not “no S are P,” and not “all S are P.” The premises together require that some S exist, because all M are S and there is at least one M in the universal negative, in the traditional reading used here.
Example
No island-built temples are wooden; all island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. The sentence is invented, so that the figure can be seen; it is not a historical claim about a named building.
Not this
Not last week’s Darapti (AAI-3: all M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P), which has a particular affirmative conclusion. Not last week’s second-figure Baroco. Other third-figure moods remain reserved. Invented terms are classroom terms, not official classifications.
Connects to
The third figure, already taught, and last week’s Darapti, as a contrast.

Last week’s Darapti, still in the third figure, had two universal affirmatives and a particular affirmative conclusion: some S are P. Today a second third-figure mood begins, Felapton, whose conclusion is particular and negative.

No M are P. All M are S. Therefore some S are not P. The middle term is still the subject of both premises. The conclusion is not “no S are P,” because the premises do not say that every S is among the M, only that the M, which are not P, are among the S. So some of the S, at least those that are M, are not P.

An invented classroom example, not a historical claim: no island-built temples are wooden; all island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden.

Remember

Felapton is EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. The conclusion is particular and negative, not “no S are P,” and not Darapti’s “some S are P.” The middle term is the subject of both premises. Invented examples are classroom terms, not official claims.
