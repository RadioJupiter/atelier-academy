Poetry 5 was Keats’s 1817 sonnet. Today is Poetry 6: William Wordsworth, “I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud,” first published 1807. The text used here is the familiar four-stanza public-domain text as printed by the Poetry Foundation (with “golden daffodils” and the “inward eye”). This lesson does not date that exact wording to a later revision year, because a source for that later year has not been opened here.

The set text (Wordsworth, as given on the Poetry Foundation page used for this lesson):

I wandered lonely as a cloud That floats on high o'er vales and hills, When all at once I saw a crowd, A host, of golden daffodils; Beside the lake, beneath the trees, Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Continuous as the stars that shine And twinkle on the milky way, They stretched in never-ending line Along the margin of a bay: Ten thousand saw I at a glance, Tossing their heads in sprightly dance.

The waves beside them danced; but they Out-did the sparkling waves in glee: A poet could not but be gay, In such a jocund company: I gazed—and gazed—but little thought What wealth the show to me had brought:

For oft, when on my couch I lie In vacant or in pensive mood, They flash upon that inward eye Which is the bliss of solitude; And then my heart with pleasure fills, And dances with the daffodils.

What to notice. The poem is not a sonnet, so it has no volta of the kind already taught in a fourteen-line poem. The last stanza turns to memory: the scene returns to the “inward eye.” “Ten thousand saw I at a glance” is a figure in the poem, not a counted census this lesson will treat as a measurement. The poem is not a botany class, and not a science lesson on the eye (this week’s How we see stays where it was taught).

Attribute the lines to Wordsworth, and to the 1807 first publication of the poem, without inventing a diary of a walk this sitting has not sourced.

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is a quadratic inequality read from the graph of y = (x − 2)(x − 6), and how is that different from Euclid III.36?
  2. The parabola opens upward. y < 0 between the roots: 2 < x < 6. y > 0 outside the roots.
  3. III.36 is a different picture: from an exterior point, PT² = PA × PB. The 2 and the 6 in the inequality are roots, not automatically a tangent and a secant.
  4. A near-miss is to merge the two pictures, or to treat PT² = PA × PB as a sum, or to invent a complex solution for a square that is never negative.

Together

In your own words, what is g on Mars as taught this week, and how was it obtained?

Hint 1: The formula

The already-taught g = G M / R², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².

Hint 2: The figures

NASA Planetary Fact Sheet figures: M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 6,792 km so R = 3,396 km. The computation is about 3.71 m/s², reported as about 3.7 m/s², matching the fact sheet’s listed 3.7. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5. No invented orbital speed.
