Latin · Capitulum VI, part 5: a first look at the passive

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VI · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms

passive
Definition
A verb form in which the subject is the person or thing that the action happens to, not the one who does it. In this chapter you meet third-person forms such as portātur (‘is carried’) and portantur (‘are carried’).
Example
Saccus ā servō portātur. The sack is carried by the slave. The subject is saccus; the doer is named by ā/ab + ablative.
Not this
The passive is not a past tense. portātur is still a present-tense form. And ā servō (by the slave) is not the same as cum servō (with the slave).
Connects to
You already know the active portat / portant (he carries / they carry) from earlier chapters. The new endings −tur and −ntur flip who the subject is.
ablative of agent
Definition
The construction ā or ab + ablative that names the person who does the action of a passive verb.
Example
Saccus ā servō portātur (the sack is carried by the slave). Saccī ā servīs portantur (the sacks are carried by the slaves).
Not this
This is not motion from, even though it uses the same preposition ab. In a passive sentence ā servō names the doer, not a place left behind.
Connects to
cum + ablative remains accompaniment (with). Agent is only for the doer of a passive verb.

Capitulum VI also introduces the first passive forms. Notice them in the book, then practice on original sentences.

Word How to say it Meaning
portat POR-tat carries (active)
portātur por-TAH-tur is carried (passive)
portant POR-tant they carry (active)
portantur por-TAN-tur they are carried (passive)
saccus SAK-kus sack, bag
ā / ab ah / ahb by (agent of a passive verb), with the ablative

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The pattern. Active: Servus saccum portat. Passive: Saccus ā servō portātur. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive; the old subject is kept, if you need it, as ā/ab + ablative.

Original sentences: Saccus ā servō portātur. Saccī ā servīs portantur. Puer ā dominō nōn portātur — puer ambulat.

Remember

Passive present, 3rd person: −tur (singular), −ntur (plural). The subject receives the action. The doer, if named, is ā/ab + ablative (agent), not cum. portātur is not a past tense.

Key terms: passive, ablative of agent.

Mathematics · Geometry X: tangents to a circle

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry, tangents

Learn

Key terms

tangent to a circle
Definition
A line that meets a circle at exactly one point, called the point of contact. The radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin tangentem, accusative of tangēns (‘touching’), in the phrase līnea tangēns (‘a touching line’), the present participle of tangere (‘to touch’).
Example
If a tangent from P touches a circle of radius 5 at A, and OP = 13, then PA = 12, because △OAP is right-angled at A.
Not this
A tangent is not a chord (a chord meets the circle twice), and a line that ‘just misses’ the circle on the page is not a tangent.
Connects to
Yesterday's right triangle used a half-chord. Today's uses the radius to the point of contact, which is perpendicular to the tangent.
two tangents from a point
Definition
From a point outside a circle, the two tangent segments to the points of contact are equal. The two radii to those points, the two tangents, and the line from the center to the outside point make two congruent right triangles.
Example
If both tangents from P have length 8, the two points of contact are the same distance from P, and OP bisects angle APB and also bisects the angle AOB at the center.
Not this
This is not ‘any two tangents of the same circle are equal’ — only the two drawn from the same outside point. Parallel tangents on opposite sides are a different figure.
Connects to
The right angle at each point of contact is today's first theorem; congruence of the two right triangles gives the equal lengths.

A line that meets a circle at exactly one point is a tangent, and that point is the point of contact.

Theorem. The radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent.

Theorem. The two tangent segments from an outside point to the points of contact are equal.

If O is the center, A the point of contact, and P a point on the tangent, then △OAP is right-angled at A, so

OA² + AP² = OP².

A 5-12-13 triangle, or a 6-8-10, will often appear, the same triples you already know.

Remember

A tangent meets the circle once, and is perpendicular to the radius at that point. The two tangent segments from an outside point are equal. OA² + AP² = OP².

Key terms: tangent to a circle, two tangents from a point.

Watch me

A tangent from P touches a circle of radius 5 at A, and OP = 13. Find PA.

  1. The radius OA is perpendicular to the tangent at A, so △OAP is right-angled at A.
  2. OA = 5, OP = 13, so PA = √(13² − 5²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12.
  3. Check: 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13². ✓

Writing & Rhetoric · Concession in a two-paragraph argument

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, the although-sentence used at the level of a paragraph

Learn

Key terms

concession
Definition
A real point on the other side that you state fairly, before you show why your thesis still stands. In this course it often takes the form of an although-sentence (Day 9), and in a two-paragraph essay it often opens the second paragraph or appears in the concluding sentence.
Origin
From late Middle English, from Middle French concession, from Latin concessiō (‘a grant, a conceding’), from concēdere.
Example
Although the Karnak text is a royal inscription and not a neutral minutes-of-the-meeting, it can still be compared, clause by clause, with the Hattusa tablet.
Not this
A concession is not a fake weakness (‘although some people may disagree’), and it is not a surrender of the thesis. If you ignore the point after stating it, it was only decoration.
Connects to
Day 9 taught the although-sentence at sentence level. Today that sentence has a job in the two-paragraph outline from Day 16.
limit of a claim
Definition
A precise statement of what the thesis does not try to prove, so that the argument stays as strong as the evidence. A limit is not a concession of a contrary fact; it is a boundary around your own claim.
Example
The thesis that the two copies of the treaty should be read together does not, by itself, prove who ‘won’ the earlier battle of Kadesh; that question is beyond the present evidence.
Not this
A limit is not timidity, and it is not ‘we can never know anything’. It is a way of not claiming more than the sources will bear (Day 1’s sourcing habit).
Connects to
A strong concluding sentence often carries a limit, a concession, or both, and then returns to what the evidence *does* support.

A two-paragraph argument is stronger when it concedes a real point and when it limits its own claim.

Where to put them. A concession often opens paragraph 2 (‘Although…, …’). A limit often belongs in the concluding sentence, so the close is exact rather than inflated.

A test for a fake concession. If the conceded point is ‘some people may disagree’, or if you never use it, it is decoration. Name a specific fact or a specific reading that a fair opponent would actually use.

Today. Take the Day 16 outline and add one although-sentence and one limit. Then check that the thesis still stands, in new words, after both have been admitted.

Remember

A concession fairly states a real contrary point and then shows why the thesis still stands (often an although-sentence). A limit marks what the thesis does not claim. Neither is decoration, and neither is a surrender.

Key terms: concession, limit of a claim.

Science · The oil-paint module: a quantitative check and a short synthesis

45 min · Method: check the model against a measurement, then write a short synthesis

Learn

Key terms

mass check (of a film)
Definition
A measurement that can distinguish evaporation from autoxidation: a film that is mainly losing a liquid to the air should lose mass; a drying-oil film can gain mass as it takes up oxygen (before any later, slower losses). The check is the direction of the change, not a promised percentage.
Example
A labeled smear of linseed oil, weighed on a day it is still liquid and again when it is tacky, may be a little heavier if oxygen has been taken up. A watercolor smear should be lighter once the water has gone.
Not this
A mass check is not a license to invent a number you did not measure, and a single weighing on a coarse kitchen scale may not be sensitive enough. If the scale cannot show the change, say so; do not fake a gain.
Connects to
The model of Days 12–14 predicts a possible gain for the oil and a loss for the watercolor. The observation has to be real.
model of oil-paint drying
Definition
The short account this module has built: a drying-oil binder, open to the air, undergoes autoxidation and then polymerization, forming a solid film that holds the pigment; a solvent, if used, may evaporate, but that is not what makes the oil itself into a film; watercolor is a different system, with water leaving mainly by evaporation.
Example
A thick oil lump skins over because oxygen reaches the surface first; a closed bottle of oil stays liquid; a watercolor wash can be dry in minutes.
Not this
The model is not a complete chemistry of every additive, and it is not a rule about a fixed number of hours. It is a model: it explains the smears, the skin on a lump, and a possible mass gain, and it has limits.
Connects to
Day 2’s scientific model (the candle under the jar) already used this habit: a model is judged by what it explains and what it does not.

Two closing jobs: a measurement, if you have a scale that can sense a small change, and a synthesis of the model.

The mass check. Weigh a labeled support with a thin linseed-oil smear, and, if you can, a watercolor smear, on day one and again when the watercolor is dry and the oil is at least tacky. Record the masses. The model’s prediction: the watercolor should lose mass (water leaves); the oil film may gain mass (oxygen is taken up), or the change may be too small for the scale. Write what you actually got. If you have no suitable scale, write a designed procedure and the predicted directions of change, and say that the measurement was not made.

The synthesis, in a two-paragraph outline or a short paragraph. Answer: Why does oil paint ‘dry’, and how is that different from watercolor? Use the terms drying oil, autoxidation, polymerization, pigment, and binder. Include one limit (Day 19 of writing: what the model does not claim).

Safety. No lead driers, no solvents near a flame, no tasting of oils or pigments.

Remember

If a scale is sensitive enough, a watercolor film should lose mass and an oil film may gain mass. If it is not, say so. Then write a short synthesis of the model, with one limit, using this week’s terms.

Key terms: mass check (of a film), model of oil-paint drying.

  2. You should say that the measurement could not be made with this scale, and you may still state the predicted directions.
  3. You should not invent a percentage gain or a ‘measured’ change the scale could not show.

Literature · Iliad, Books 23–24: the funeral, the ransom, a last written response

45 min · Method: close reading and a short argued response · Read Books 23–24 before this lesson

Learn

Key terms

supplication (Priam)
Definition
The scene in Book 24 in which Priam comes by night to Achilles’ shelter, kisses his hands, and asks for Hector’s body, appealing to Achilles’ own father, Peleus. It is a formal and dangerous asking, not a casual request.
Origin
From Middle English, from Old French, from Latin supplicatio, from supplicare (‘to supplicate’).
Example
Priam: ‘Remember your father.’ The poem puts the two enemies in one space, and Achilles, after the first shock, weeps and will in the end give the body back.
Not this
Supplication is not ‘Priam apologizes for the war’, and it is not a conversion of Achilles into a gentle hero. He is still dangerous; the poem also shows pity and a shared grief.
Connects to
You have seen gifts fail in Book 9. The ransom in Book 24 is not the same situation, and it is not only a payment. The appeal to the father is part of the force.
ransom of Hector
Definition
The return of Hector’s body to Priam, for burial, after the supplication and the giving of gifts, with a pause in the fighting agreed for the funeral. It is the close of the Iliad; the fall of Troy is not narrated in this poem.
Example
The poem ends with Hector’s funeral, not with Achilles’ death and not with the wooden horse. Those stories belong to the wider cycle; this poem stops here.
Not this
The ransom is not a happy ending in a modern sense, and it is not a cancellation of Book 22. The body, which was dragged, is washed and burned, and the women lament.
Connects to
A written response today should not ‘finish the story’ by adding the fall of Troy as if Homer had written it in Book 24.

Read Books 23–24. Book 23: the funeral of Patroclus and the funeral games. Book 24: the gods object to the continued mistreatment of Hector’s body; Thetis is sent to Achilles; Priam, with Hermes’ help, crosses the camp and supplicates Achilles; the body is returned; Troy buries Hector. The Iliad ends.

What the poem does not do. It does not narrate Achilles’ death, the wooden horse, or the fall of the city. A response that ‘completes’ those events as if they were the last pages of this book has left the Iliad.

Close reading. Sit with the moment Priam and Achilles weep together, and with the later meal, the return of the body, and the laments of the women (Andromache, Hecuba, Helen). Notice who speaks last over the body.

A short written response (today’s check, begun here). In two paragraphs (you have the form from writing this week): What does Book 24 give us that Book 22, by itself, would have left undone? Use at least one specific scene, sourced to a book, and a limit (the poem does not forgive everything, and it does not end the war forever).

Remember

Books 23–24: funeral games for Patroclus; Priam’s supplication; the ransom and funeral of Hector. The Iliad ends with that funeral, not with the fall of Troy. A response should stay inside this poem and use a specific scene.

Key terms: supplication (Priam), ransom of Hector.

Watch me

  2. The gifts of Book 9 came from Agamemnon through envoys, in the dispute about honor and withdrawal, and they failed.
  3. Book 24 is a father in the same room as the killer, after the death of the friend and of the son. The poem makes them weep for their dead, not sign a treaty of friendship.

Atelier · Accents and a point of focus

45 min · Method: finish a study already in progress; a few accents, one focus · After value control and edges

Learn

Key terms

accent
Definition
A small, especially dark (or, more rarely, especially sharp or especially light) touch that sits at a point of contact, a deep hole of shadow, or a decisive edge. Accents are few. They are placed after the large families are right, not as a first sprinkle of black.
Example
The contact of a plaster nose with the table, or the deepest part of a nostril, or the meeting of two forms in a still life, may take a small, dark accent. The rest of the shadow family stays a family, not a field of accents.
Not this
An accent is not ‘shade everything darker’, and it is not a black outline. A drawing full of accents has no accent.
Connects to
Relative value (Day 16) and edge quality (Day 18) tell you whether a passage is already dark enough, or already sharp enough, before you add a touch.
point of focus
Definition
The place in the drawing you want the eye to arrive at first: usually the most particular passage of form, with a slightly stronger contrast or a slightly sharper found edge, while the surroundings are relatively quieter.
Example
In a plaster-nose study, the focus might be the plane break at the tip or the wing, not the cloth at the edge of the paper. In a group of solids, it might be the overlap where the sphere meets the cube.
Not this
A focus is not a spotlight invented after the fact, and it is not the same as a highlight (the highlight may or may not be at the focus). It is a decision about where the drawing is most specific.
Connects to
Lost edges and quieter values away from the focus help, but they must still be true to the set-up; you do not invent fog to force a focus.

A study is ready for accents and a point of focus only when the large forms, the families, and the edges already read.

Accents are few. Walk the drawing and choose two or three places — not ten — where the darkest dark or the sharpest small edge actually sits in the set-up: a contact with the table, a deep aperture, a tight overlap. Place them as small, decided touches. Then stop.

Focus. Choose one passage to be the most particular. It may already be so. If not, you may slightly strengthen a contrast or an edge there, and you may slightly quiet (not falsify) a competing edge at the margin. The test is still the set-up: you are selecting, not decorating.

What you do not do. You do not start a new drawing from scratch today unless yesterday’s is unusable; in that case, a shorter, large-form study plus one focused passage is better than a rushed complete copy. You do not add a black frame of accents around the whole object.

A common error. Every shadow is taken to the same black, so nothing is an accent; or a random sharp mark, away from the form, that the eye goes to instead.

Finish the plaster feature or the still life: a few accents, one focus, then a stand-back.

Remember

Accents are a few small, decided dark (or sharp) touches at real contacts or deep shadows, placed after the families are right. A point of focus is the most particular passage; quiet the rest without falsifying it. A drawing full of accents has no accent.

Key terms: accent, point of focus.

Watch me

A student adds a dark, sharp mark in every corner of every shadow, including the cloth at the edge of the paper. What has been lost, and what should they have done instead?

  1. There is no accent, because everything is accented, and the eye is pulled to the margin.

Modern language · Spanish: comparatives

30 min · Method: three patterns, then irregular comparatives · Instruction in English

Learn

Key terms

comparative
Definition
A pattern that compares two people or things. In Spanish: más + adjective (or adverb or noun) + que; menos + … + que; and tan + adjective or adverb + como for ‘as … as’.
Origin
From Middle English comparatif, from Middle French comparatif, from Latin comparātīvus, from comparāre (‘to compare’).
Example
El libro es más largo que la carta. Esta lección es menos difícil que la otra. Ana es tan alta como Luis.
Not this
Más que is not tan como, and más de is a different pattern used before a number (más de diez), not before the second person or thing being compared.
Connects to
Adjectives still agree: más larga, menos difíciles, tan altos como…
irregular comparative
Definition
A small set of comparatives that do not use más: mejor (better), peor (worse), mayor (older or greater), menor (younger or smaller). They still take que: mejor que, peor que.
Example
Este dibujo es mejor que el primero. Hoy me siento peor que ayer. Mi hermano es mayor que yo.
Not this
Do not write *más mejor or *más peor. Mayor/menor for age are the usual choice in this course, rather than *más viejo for a person when you mean ‘older’.
Connects to
Mejor and peor can also be adverbs: dibuja mejor, canta peor.

To compare, Spanish uses three regular patterns and a short irregular set.

More / less. más + adjective + que: El museo es más grande que la casa. menos + adjective + que: La carta es menos larga que el libro. The adjective agrees with the first noun.

As … as. tan + adjective + como: La lección es tan importante como el dibujo.

Nouns. más / menos + noun + que: Tengo más libros que cuadernos. tan is not used this way with a noun; for ‘as many as’ you will later meet tantos/tantas … como. Do not invent tan libros como today.

Before a number. más de / menos de + a number: más de diez páginas. That de is not the que of a two-thing comparison.

Irregular. mejor que, peor que, mayor que, menor que. Never más mejor.

Word How to say it Meaning
más … que mahs … keh more … than
menos … que MEH-nohs … keh less / fewer … than
tan … como tahn … KOH-moh as … as
mejor meh-HOR better
peor peh-OR worse
mayor mah-YOR older / greater
menor meh-NOR younger / smaller

A complete comparison names both sides, unless the second side is already obvious.

Remember

más + adj + que; menos + adj + que; tan + adj + como. Adjectives agree. Before a number: más de diez. Irregular: mejor, peor, mayor, menor — not más mejor.

Economics & personal finance · Supply and demand

30 min · Method: schedules, then shortage, surplus, and equilibrium · After Day 14 · Classroom numbers are invented examples

Learn

Key terms

demand and supply
Definition
Demand is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy, other things equal. Supply is the relationship between the price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer, other things equal. Each is a whole schedule or curve, not a single number.
Origin
Demand is from Middle English, from Old French demander, from Latin dēmandāre. Supply is from Middle English, from Old French, from Latin supplēre (‘to fill up, to complete’).
Example
If, in an invented classroom table, students will buy 40 sandwiches at $3 and 20 at $5, those two points belong to a demand schedule. If the canteen will offer 15 at $3 and 35 at $5, those points belong to a supply schedule.
Not this
Demand is not ‘what people wish they could have’, and supply is not ‘how many exist in a warehouse’. Willingness and ability, at a price, are part of the definition.
Connects to
Day 14’s price signal is what these two schedules together explain: why a price might rise or fall when the two sides do not match.
equilibrium, shortage, and surplus
Definition
The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At a price below that, there is a shortage (quantity demanded greater than quantity supplied). At a price above it, there is a surplus (quantity supplied greater than quantity demanded).
Origin
Equilibrium is from Latin aequilībrium, from aequus (‘equal’) + lībra (‘balance’).
Example
In the invented table: at $3, buyers want 40 and the canteen offers 15 — a shortage of 25. At $5, buyers want 20 and the canteen offers 35 — a surplus of 15. The equilibrium, if the table were completed, would be a price where the two quantities match.
Not this
Equilibrium is not ‘the fair price’ and not ‘a price that never changes’. It is the matching price in this model, other things equal. A change in tastes, costs, or income can shift a whole schedule and move the equilibrium.
Connects to
Shortage and surplus are about quantities at a price, not about whether anyone is morally at fault.

Day 14 treated a price as a signal. Today we draw (or tabulate) the two sides of that signal.

Demand. At each price, how many units buyers are willing and able to buy, other things equal. Usually, a higher price means a lower quantity demanded. That is a claim about the slope of a demand schedule, not a law about every luxury or every emergency.

Supply. At each price, how many units sellers are willing and able to offer, other things equal. Usually, a higher price means a higher quantity supplied.

The invented canteen table (not real data):

Price Quantity demanded Quantity supplied
$2 50 10
$3 40 15
$4 30 30
$5 20 35
$6 10 40

At $4, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (30). That is the equilibrium of this table. At $3, there is a shortage of 25. At $5, there is a surplus of 15.

A story that stays in the model. If the price were $3, some buyers would not find a sandwich; pressure on the price would be upward. If it were $5, some sandwiches would go unsold; pressure would be downward. The model does not say the canteen is kind or unkind; it says what the unmatched quantities do to the price, if the price is free to move.

Shifts (a first look). If more students suddenly want sandwiches at every price, the demand schedule shifts, and the equilibrium price in a new table would be higher. If costs of bread rise and the canteen will offer fewer sandwiches at each price, supply shifts, and the new equilibrium price would be higher. Do not confuse a movement along a schedule (a price change, other things equal) with a shift of the whole schedule.

Remember

Demand and supply are schedules, not single numbers. Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied. Below that price, a shortage; above, a surplus. The canteen table is invented. A shift of a whole schedule is not the same as a movement along it.
