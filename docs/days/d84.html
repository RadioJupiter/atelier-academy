Latin · Capitulum XV: sī, nisi, at, and antequam

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV continues. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

sī and nisi
Definition
Sī means if. Nisi means if not, unless, or except. Nisi is ne + sī in origin as a compound; in this course it is learned as a conjunction that sets the negative case of an if-clause.
Example
Sī tacētis, magister nōn īrātus est. — If you are silent, the schoolmaster is not angry. Nisi tacētis, magister īrātus est. — Unless you are silent, the schoolmaster is angry.
Not this
Not a first look at nōn, and not a relative clause (quī, quae, quod were taught in Capitulum VIII).
Connects to
These words hook a clause onto another clause, so the Latin sentence can now hold a condition.
at and antequam
Definition
At means but on the other hand; it stands first in its clause and sets two sides or two people against each other. Antequam means before, and introduces a clause.
Example
Titus recitat, at Mārcus tacet. — Titus recites, but Marcus (on the other side) is silent. Antequam magister venit, puerī clāmant. — Before the schoolmaster comes, the boys shout.
Not this
Not a first look at sed, which corrects or adds a limitation to what has just been said. At opposes two parties or two sides. Antequam is not the preposition ante used alone (already met as ‘before’ a noun).
Connects to
Ante and quam were both met earlier; together they introduce a clause of time, ‘before …’.

Four small words now hook one clause onto another.

Word How to say it Meaning
sī see if
nisi NIH-see if not, unless, except
at at but, on the other hand
antequam ahn-TEH-kwahm before (with a clause)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Sī tacēs, recitō. — If you are silent, I recite. Nisi tacēs, nōn recitō. — Unless you are silent, I do not recite.

At is not a first look at sed. Sed often corrects or limits the sentence just made. At stands at the front of its clause and sets two sides against each other: Ego recitō, at tū tacēs.

Antequam magister venit, discipulī adsunt. — Before the schoolmaster comes, the pupils are present. Antequam takes a verb; it is not only the preposition ante with a noun.

Remember

Sī, if; nisi, unless / if not; at, but on the other hand (two sides, not a first look at sed); antequam, before, with a clause.

Key terms: sī and nisi, at and antequam.

Watch me

  1. Sī vērum dīcis, magister nōn īrātus est. The sī-clause names the condition.
  2. Nisi vērum dīcis, magister īrātus est. Nisi names the opposite case.
  3. Antequam recitāmus, tacēmus. The antequam-clause names what comes first in time.

Hint 2: Two sides

If two people or two sides are being opposed, at is the better ‘but.’ If you are correcting the previous claim, sed is still right.

Mathematics · Geometry XLI: mixed circle measure, and a ring

45 min · Method: circumference, area, and sector together, then a ring as a difference of two areas

Learn

Key terms

annulus
Definition
An annulus, or ring, is the region between two circles that share a center. Its area is the difference of the two disk areas: A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²), where R is the outer radius and r is the inner radius.
Example
A classroom ring with outer radius 5 and inner radius 3 has area π(25 − 9) = 16π.
Not this
Not the area of the outer circle alone, and not π(R − r)², which would be a much smaller circle of radius R − r, not the ring.
Connects to
Yesterday’s area formula is used twice, then subtracted. The shared center matters: this lesson does not treat two off-center circles.

This lesson puts the week’s circle measures together, and adds one new region: a ring, or annulus, between two circles with the same center.

The ring is what is left when the inner disk is removed from the outer disk:

A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²).

It is not π(R − r)². That expression is the area of a single circle whose radius is the difference of the two radii, and that is a different figure.

For a classroom outer radius 5 and inner radius 3, A = π(25 − 9) = 16π.

Remember

A ring with a shared center has area π(R² − r²), not π(R − r)². Circumference, whole-circle area, and sector area stay available as already taught.

Key terms: annulus.

Watch me

Watch the ring with R = 5 and r = 3, then a mixed check on the same outer circle.

  1. Outer area: π × 5² = 25π. Inner area: π × 3² = 9π.
  2. The ring is the difference: 16π, or π(25 − 9) in one step.
  3. The outer circumference is 10π, a length, and is not added to the area.

Together

A classroom ring has outer radius 8 and inner radius 4. Find its area in terms of π. Then say why π(8 − 4)² is the wrong expression.

Hint 1: Difference of squares

A = π(8² − 4²) = π(64 − 16) = 48π.

Hint 2: The wrong square

π(8 − 4)² = 16π, which is a circle of radius 4, not the ring.

Hint 2: Near-miss

The "Not this" line on the card names a common mix-up. Explain why that mix-up would change an answer in this lesson.

Check

  1. A classroom ring has R = 6 and r = 2. What is its area, in terms of π?
  2. A circle has radius 5. Give its circumference and its area, and label each.
  3. Why is π(R − r)² not the area of the ring?

Writing & Rhetoric · The short complete essay, assembled

40 min · Method: assemble the analytical essay from the outline and this week’s paragraphs; not a first look at the essay itself

Learn

Key terms

assembled short essay
Definition
An assembled short essay is the opening, the body paragraphs in outline order, the other-side paragraph, and the close, copied and then repaired so that they read as one piece: pronouns and tense agree, quotations stay exact, and no heading is left out or repeated.
Example
The job today is to join what you have already written, and to mend joints, not to invent a second essay from memory.
Not this
Not a first look at the analytical essay (Day 9), the outline (Days 6 and 78), or this week’s paragraph types. Not a demand for a long paper.
Connects to
If a paragraph is still missing, write the missing one from its heading before you assemble. Do not hide a gap with a transition word.

Today the short analytical essay is assembled, not begun again. Lay out, in order: the opening, the body paragraphs (including the second body paragraph from Monday), the other-side paragraph, and the close.

Read the whole piece aloud, in English. Repair the joints:

- A heading left out of the outline must be written, not skipped.

- A repeated first paragraph must be cut back to one.

- A quotation that has drifted from the set wording must be corrected from the course text, or turned into a paraphrase and no longer called a quotation.

- The thesis in the opening and the thesis in the close must be the same claim, in wording that may differ.

The finished piece should be short and readable, not a 100-page packet. A few tight paragraphs is enough for this week’s essay.

Science · Water: the liquid is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C

40 min · Method: a sourced comparison of liquid water at 0 °C and near 4 °C, and why ice forms at a lake surface

Learn

Key terms

maximum density of liquid water near 4 °C
Definition
Liquid water is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C. The U.S. Geological Survey, using a Bureau of Reclamation table (1977), lists 0.99987 g/cm³ at 0 °C and 1.00000 g/cm³ at 4.0 °C for the figures used in that table. Those values are not mixed with yesterday’s NIST liquid-water figure.
Example
In a quiet lake, water near 4 °C can sit lower than colder water near 0 °C, because it is denser. Ice, still less dense than either, forms and floats at the surface.
Not this
Not a first look at density or at why ice floats, which were taught yesterday. Not a claim that every body of water on Earth is a still lake, and not a blended official number taken from two tables at once.
Connects to
USGS Water Science School, “Water Density,” https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-density , table credited to U.S. Department of the Interior, Bureau of Reclamation, 1977, Ground Water Manual, as cited there.

Yesterday: ice at 0 °C is less dense than liquid water at 0 °C, so ice floats. Today: the liquid itself is not densest at the ice point.

The USGS water-density page, citing a Bureau of Reclamation table from 1977, gives these liquid-water figures (grams per cubic centimeter):

0 °C (32 °F): 0.99987. 4.0 °C (39.2 °F): 1.00000.

In that table, the liquid at 4.0 °C is denser than the liquid at 0 °C. This course does not mix those figures with yesterday’s NIST value of 0.99984 g/cm³ for liquid water at 0 °C. The two tables were built in different places; each is used with its own label.

A school consequence, also drawn on that USGS page, is this. As a still lake cools, water near 4 °C is denser than colder water closer to 0 °C, so the colder water can remain nearer the surface. Ice, less dense than the liquid, then forms at the surface rather than from the bottom up. That is a density argument, not a certificate about every lake, current, or winter.

Remember

In the USGS / Bureau of Reclamation table used today, liquid water is denser at 4.0 °C (1.00000 g/cm³) than at 0 °C (0.99987 g/cm³). Ice still floats on the liquid. Do not mix these figures with yesterday’s NIST liquid-water number.

Key terms: maximum density of liquid water near 4 °C.

Watch me

  1. In today’s table, 1.00000 g/cm³ at 4.0 °C is greater than 0.99987 g/cm³ at 0 °C, so the 4 °C water is denser.
  2. Denser liquid tends to sit lower in a still body of water than less-dense liquid.
  3. Ice is still less dense than either liquid, as yesterday taught, so it floats at the surface. The USGS page uses this density pattern to explain why a lake does not typically freeze from the bottom up.

Hint 2: The surface

Literature · Inferno II: three Ladies, recovered courage, and the way (II.94–142)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Longfellow, Inferno II.94–142; Canto II is completed, Canto III reserved

Learn

Key terms

three Ladies benedight
Definition
In Longfellow, Inferno II.94–126, a “gentle Lady… in Heaven” asks Lucìa to help “thy faithful one”; Lucìa comes to Beatrice, “sitting with the ancient Rachel,” and Beatrice then comes to Virgil. Virgil asks why the speaker delays, “Seeing that three such Ladies benedight / Are caring for thee in the court of Heaven.”
Example
The three, in this telling, are the gentle Lady in Heaven, Lucìa, and Beatrice. This lesson does not add later names that Longfellow’s wording of these lines does not give.
Not this
Not a first look at Beatrice, named on Tuesday. Not a first look at the Greyhound. Not Canto III.
Connects to
Longfellow II.94–126, including “Lucìa,” as spelled in the 1867 text.

Dante, Inferno II, translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867, as posted at Wikisource: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_2 (page text from the linked scan pages). Spelling and punctuation are quoted as in that text, including Lucìa. This course does not add later identifications beyond what the set wording itself names.

Tuesday ended as Beatrice named herself and began to say why she was not afraid to enter. In II.94–114 she continues, and the chain of care is told:

“A gentle Lady is in Heaven, who grieves / At this impediment… / In her entreaty she besought Lucìa,” and Lucìa “came unto the place / Where I was sitting with the ancient Rachel.” Lucìa asks why Beatrice does not succor “him, who loved thee so.” Beatrice comes downward “Confiding in thy dignified discourse.”

Virgil has delivered the speaker “from that wild beast / Which barred the beautiful mountain’s short ascent,” and now asks: “What is it, then? Why, why dost thou delay? … / Seeing that three such Ladies benedight / Are caring for thee in the court of Heaven.”

The speaker’s courage returns, in a simile:

“Even as the flowerets, by nocturnal chill, / Bowed down and closed, when the sun whitens them, / Uplift themselves all open on their stems; / Such I became…”

He answers, “Thou hast my heart so with desire disposed / To the adventure… / That to my first intent I have returned.” Then the close of the canto:

“Now go, for one sole will is in us both, / Thou Leader, and thou Lord, and Master thou.” / “Thus said I to him; and when he had moved, / I entered on the deep and savage way.”

Canto III remains reserved.

Remember

A gentle Lady in Heaven asks Lucìa; Lucìa asks Beatrice; Beatrice has come to Virgil. Three Ladies benedight care for the speaker in Heaven’s court. His courage returns, like closed flowers opening in the sun, and he enters on the deep and savage way. Longfellow II.94–142; Canto III reserved.

Atelier · A pause on the grisaille: what the lay-in has earned

60 min · Method: a short written pause on the monochrome head, naming what the lay-in has earned and what a later sitting may do

Learn

Key terms

lay-in pause
Definition
A lay-in pause is a short written account, at the end of this week’s grisaille work, of what the monochrome lay-in has already earned (clear families, a terminator that turns, one modest shadow turn) and what is reserved for a later sitting (modeling in the light, a careful feature, any later color).
Example
The note is specific: a location and a skill, not a vague “the head is going well.” It is not a two-week plan, already taught, and it is not a grade.
Not this
Not a first look at critique or at the families. Not a finish today, and not a first look at full color.
Connects to
Monday through Wednesday already made and checked the lay-in. Today records that work so that a later sitting does not start as if nothing had been decided.

This week’s grisaille work was a lay-in, a check, and a little modeling in the shadow. It was not a finished head. Today you pause and write, so that the next sitting begins from a decision rather than from restlessness.

Look at the work in the same single light, at the same distance. Write three short notes:

1. What the lay-in has earned: name a place where the families still read, or where the terminator still turns.

3. What a later sitting may do first: one job only, still monochrome unless a later week says otherwise. Full color remains reserved for Term 3.

Do not “just finish a little” today unless the note has named a tiny repair, and then stop. The pause is the work.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of querer, and a four-stem contrast

40 min · Method: the irregular preterite of querer (quis-), then a contrast of pude, puse, supe, and quise

Learn

Key terms

quise
Definition
Quise is the first-person singular preterite of querer. The stem is quis-: quise, quisiste, quiso, quisimos, quisisteis, quisieron. In the preterite, the usual school sense is a completed act of wanting, often “I tried to” or, with a negation, “I refused to,” on that occasion — not a long-standing wish.
Example

simple and compound compared
Definition
A comparison of simple and compound interest, in this lesson, uses the already-taught formulas on the same invented principal, rate, and time, and names the difference honestly. Simple: A = P(1 + r t), or A = P + I with I = P r t. Compound, as taught on Day 9: A = P(1 + r)^n, when interest is added to the principal at the end of each of n equal periods.
Example
Invented: P = 1,000, r = 0.10 a year, three years. Simple: A = 1,000 × (1 + 0.10 × 3) = 1,300. Compound, yearly: A = 1,000 × (1.10)^3 = 1,331. The compound amount is 31 larger in this invented example, because interest is paid on interest already earned.

Last week found P from a simple-interest amount. Today keeps both formulas in view and compares them on one invented case.

Compound interest was taught on Day 9: A = P(1 + r)^n, interest on principal and on interest already earned. Simple interest, already used in this term, is I = P r t, or A = P(1 + r t). Last week you found P from a simple-interest amount. Today is a comparison, not a first look at either formula.

Invented figures, labeled invented: P = 1,000, r = 0.10 a year, for three years, with compounding once a year in the compound case.

Simple: A = 1,000 × (1 + 0.10 × 3) = 1,000 × 1.30 = 1,300.

Compound: A = 1,000 × (1.10)^3. 1.10² = 1.21; 1.21 × 1.10 = 1.331; so A = 1,331.

The compound amount is 31 larger, in this invented example, because the second and third years pay interest on interest already added. That difference is not a universal official “always 31,” and the rate is not a bank’s published rate.

The rule of 72, already taught, is not required today, and must not be mixed into this comparison as if it replaced either formula.
