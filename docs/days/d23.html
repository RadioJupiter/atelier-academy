Latin · Capitulum VII, part 3: reflexive sē, versus eum and eam

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VII · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

reflexive sē
Definition
The pronoun sē (accusative; also used as ablative) refers back to the subject of its own clause: the subject acts on himself, herself, or themselves.
Example
Mārcus sē in aquā videt. Marcus sees himself in the water. The one seen is the same as the one who sees.
Not this
sē is not eum or eam. Mārcus eum videt means Marcus sees him — some other male already in view, not Marcus himself.
Connects to
Spanish already used this idea with reflexives (me lavo). Latin marks it with sē in the third person.
eum / eam
Definition
The accusative of is / ea: ‘him’ or ‘her’, referring to someone other than the subject (in the ordinary case taught here).
Example
Iūlia Mārcum vocat; Mārcus eam audit. Julia calls Marcus; Marcus hears her (Julia).
Not this
Not a reflexive. If Marcus hears himself, the form is sē, not eum.
Connects to
You have already met eum / eam as object pronouns. Today the contrast with sē is the new skill.
Word How to say it Meaning
sē say himself / herself / themselves (reflexive)
eum eh-um him (not the subject)
eam eh-ahm her (not the subject)
videt WI-det sees
lavat LAH-waht washes
in aquā in AH-kwah in the water (ablative: place where)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The test. Ask: is the object the same person as the subject? If yes, sē. If no, eum or eam (or a name).

Original sentences: Mārcus sē in aquā videt. Iūlia sē lavat. Mārcus Iūliam videt; Iūlia eum nōn videt. Puerī sē lavant.

sē does not change for masculine, feminine, or plural in this form. The verb tells you the number: videt against vident, lavat against lavant.

Remember

sē = the subject acting on himself/herself/themselves. eum / eam = him / her, someone else. The form sē does not change for gender or number; the verb does.

Key terms: reflexive sē, eum / eam.

Watch me

  2. When the object is the same person as the subject, Latin uses sē.
  3. Mārcus eum videt would mean he sees some other male, not himself.

Mathematics · Geometry XII: cyclic quadrilaterals

60 min · Method: Euclid III.22; pictorial first (inscribed angles on complementary arcs), then the supplement rule

Learn

Key terms

cyclic quadrilateral
Definition
A quadrilateral whose four vertices all lie on a single circle. It is inscribed in that circle.
Origin
From Latin cyclicus, from Greek κυκλικός, ‘circular’ (Wiktionary).
Example
If A, B, C, and D lie on a circle, in that order around the circle, then ABCD is cyclic.
Not this
Not every quadrilateral is cyclic. A square is; a non-rectangular parallelogram, in general, is not (its opposite angles are equal, but they need not be supplementary).
Connects to
Euclid III.22: the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary — they add to 180°.
opposite angles supplementary
Definition
In a cyclic quadrilateral, each pair of opposite angles sums to 180°. That is the usable form of Euclid III.22 in this course.
Example
If ∠A = 70°, then ∠C = 110°. If ∠B = 95°, then ∠D = 85°.
Not this
Adjacent angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are not forced to be supplementary by this theorem (they may or may not be). Do not apply ‘interior angles on the same side’ from parallels unless you have parallels.
Connects to
A reason in outline: an opposite pair of inscribed angles stand on arcs that together make the whole circle, so their measures add to half of 360°, which is 180°.

The fact. If ABCD is cyclic, then ∠A + ∠C = 180°, and ∠B + ∠D = 180°.

A check that is also a test. If you are told a quadrilateral is cyclic, you may use the theorem. If you are asked whether a given quadrilateral can be cyclic, the opposite angles must be able to add to 180°. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle has opposite angles equal but generally not 90°, so a pair of equal obtuse angles cannot be supplementary, and that parallelogram is not cyclic.

Numbers. ∠A = 70° ⇒ ∠C = 110°. ∠B = 95° ⇒ ∠D = 85°. The four angles of any quadrilateral still sum to 360°, which is consistent: 70 + 95 + 110 + 85 = 360.

Remember

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on a circle. Opposite angles add to 180°. Not every quadrilateral is cyclic.

Key terms: cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles supplementary.

Watch me

ABCD is cyclic. ∠A = 70° and ∠B = 95°. Find ∠C and ∠D.

  1. Opposite angles are supplementary: ∠C = 180° − 70° = 110°.
  2. ∠D = 180° − 95° = 85°.
  3. Check: 70 + 95 + 110 + 85 = 360, as a quadrilateral requires.

Together

ABCD is cyclic and ∠C = 125°. What is ∠A?

Hint 1: Opposite

Hint 2: Supplement

Writing & Rhetoric · Participial phrases, and dangling modifiers

45 min · Method: explicit teaching of the attachment rule, with repairs; no new political content

Learn

Key terms

participial phrase
Definition
A phrase headed by a participle (often an -ing or a past form used as a modifier) that adds a circumstance or a description to a noun or to a clause, without itself being the main verb.
Example
“Standing on the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle.” Or, tighter: “The inscribed angle, standing on the same arc, is half the central angle.”
Not this
Not a finite clause (which has a subject doing a tensed verb: “The angle stands on the same arc”). And not a second independent sentence spliced with a comma.
Connects to
Day 13’s semicolon joins two independent clauses. A participle lets you downgrade one action into a modifier, so one clause stays in charge.
dangling modifier
Definition
A participial phrase that is not clearly attached to the noun it is meant to describe, so the sentence seems to say the wrong thing.
Example
Broken: “Standing on the same arc, we measure half the central angle.” (It is the inscribed angle that stands on the arc, not “we.”) Repaired: “Standing on the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle.” Or: “When the inscribed angle stands on the same arc, we measure half the central angle.”
Not this
Not every opening -ing phrase is a mistake — only those whose implied subject is not the subject (or the noun) that follows.
Connects to
The repair is to name the right subject next to the phrase, or to rewrite the phrase as a finite clause with because, when, or who.

Yesterday you held two infinitives in one slot. Today you attach a modifier without starting a new main clause.

A participle is a form of a verb used as a modifier. Standing, written, given. The phrase includes its objects and adverbs: standing on the same arc, written from the hints, given two sides of a right triangle.

The attachment rule. The thing the phrase describes must be the noun the phrase is actually next to — usually the subject of the main clause.

Broken: Written in 1776, we read the sentence as a model of parallelism. (We were not written in 1776.)

Repaired: Written in 1776, the sentence is a public-domain model of parallelism. Or: Because the sentence was written in 1776, we may use it as a public-domain model.

Use the tool when it earns its keep: to tuck a circumstance beside a claim, not to decorate every sentence.

Remember

A participial phrase modifies a noun (often the subject). If the implied subject of the phrase is not that noun, the modifier dangles. Repair by attaching it, or by rewriting as a clause.

Key terms: participial phrase, dangling modifier.

Physics · Kinetic energy, and the work–energy theorem

50 min · Method: KE = ½mv² from the constant-force case of W = Fs and v² = u² + 2as; then W_net = ΔKE

Learn

Key terms

kinetic energy
Definition
The energy an object has because it is moving. For a single object treated as a particle, KE = ½ m v², in joules when m is in kilograms and v is in meters per second.
Origin
From Greek κινητικός, from κινέω, ‘I move’ (Wiktionary).
Example
A 4 kg object moving at 3 m/s has KE = ½ × 4 × 3² = 2 × 9 = 18 J.
Not this
Not the same as momentum (p = m v). Momentum is a vector and is linear in v; kinetic energy is a scalar and depends on v². A sign on v is squared away: speed is what enters KE.
Connects to
Mass is the same quantity as in Newton’s second law. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, other things equal.
work–energy theorem
Definition
The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy: Wnet = ΔKE = KEfinal − KEinitial. This is the usable form in this course for a single particle, or an object that can be treated as one.
Example
If 18 J of net work is done on a 4 kg object that starts at rest, its KE becomes 18 J, so ½ × 4 × v² = 18, v² = 9, v = 3 m/s.
Not this
Not “all forces always increase speed.” Negative net work decreases KE. And the theorem uses *net* work: the sum of work by every force, or the work of the net force along the path.
Connects to
Today’s proofs are computational: you will go from work to ΔKE, or from a change in speed back to the net work required.

The formula. KE = ½ m v². Units: kg · (m/s)² = N·m = J, the same joule as yesterday.

Why v². If you push with a constant net force F along a distance s, W_net = F s. From the second law, F = m a, and from kinematics you already have v² = u² + 2 a s. Then W_net = m a s = ½ m (v² − u²) = KE_final − KE_initial. That is the work–energy theorem, in the constant-force case that this course starts with.

Numbers. 4 kg at 3 m/s: 18 J. 2 kg at 5 m/s: ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 J. From rest, 18 J of net work on 4 kg: v = 3 m/s.

A signed check. If a 2 kg object slows from 5 m/s to rest, ΔKE = 0 − 25 = −25 J, so the net work was −25 J (a braking force did that much negative work).

Remember

KE = ½mv², in joules. W_net = ΔKE. Doubling speed quadruples KE. Negative net work reduces KE. Momentum is a different quantity.

Key terms: kinetic energy, work–energy theorem.

  1. KE = ½ × 4 × 3² = 2 × 9 = 18 J.
  2. It starts at rest, so KE_initial = 0 and ΔKE = 18 J.
  3. The work–energy theorem: W_net = 18 J.

History · The Republic: consuls, Senate, and Polybius as a source

45 min · Method: a working institutional picture, with Polybius dated and read as a second-century analysis

Learn

Key terms

Roman Republic
Definition
The period of Roman government, after the traditional end of the kingship, in which annually elected magistrates (including two consuls) and assemblies, together with the Senate, shared the public business of the city and, later, of an empire. The traditional beginning is the late sixth century BCE; the Republic ends in the first century BCE with the rise of one-man rule. Those bookends are useful labels, not a claim that every year is equally well documented.
Origin
From French republique, from Latin rēs pūblica, literally ‘the public thing’ (Wiktionary).
Example
A consul is a chief annual magistrate of the Republic; there were two at a time, a check that the tradition itself emphasizes.
Not this
Not a modern electoral democracy, and not the same as the later rule of emperors. “Republic” here translates the Romans’ rēs pūblica — the public thing — not a later party name.
Connects to
Polybius, in the second century BCE, wrote a description of Roman government that later readers still use. He is closer to the mid-Republic than Livy is to the kings, but he too has aims, and he is not an eyewitness of the year 500 BCE.
consul
Definition
One of the two chief annual magistrates of the Roman Republic. The pair of consuls is a central fact in the tradition and in later Republican practice; the consulship is not a lifelong kingship.
Origin
From Latin cōnsul (Wiktionary).
Example
In a well-documented later year, you can often name the two consuls; that dating habit is itself part of how Romans recorded time.
Not this
Not a senator as such (a consul was typically a senator, but the offices are different), and not an emperor.
Connects to
The Senate, the next term, is a council; the consuls are executives of a year.
Senate
Definition
The council of leading Romans that advised magistrates and, in Republican practice, carried great weight in policy, finance, and foreign affairs. It was not a modern elected legislature.
Origin
From Latin senātus, from senex, ‘old’ (Wiktionary).
Example
Polybius treats the Senate as one part of a mixed constitution, beside magistrates and the people.
Not this
Not the same as an assembly of the people, and not a king’s private household. The name is connected with senex, ‘old’: a council of elders in origin, at least in the word.
Connects to
This lesson gives the working picture, not a full constitutional law course. The next lesson looks at a different class of source: early law, the Twelve Tables.

A working picture, with its source dated. After the kingship, Roman tradition describes a Republic: two consuls each year, other magistrates, assemblies of the people, and a Senate. That picture is clearest for the middle Republic, when writers such as Polybius (second century BCE) could watch Rome’s government at work. It is less well documented for the first decades after the traditional end of the kings.

Polybius, Book 6, as a class of source. Polybius was a Greek hostage and later a friend of leading Romans. He wrote to explain Rome’s rise to a Greek audience. His account of a “mixed” constitution — magistrates, Senate, and people each holding a share — is invaluable and is also an argument. Use it as a second-century analysis, not as a transcript of the year 509 BCE (a traditional date for the Republic’s beginning, to be treated as tradition).

What to hold. Two consuls, annual office, a Senate of weight, assemblies that could vote; no king. What not to hold yet: a detailed map of every assembly, or a claim that the early Republic already looked exactly like Polybius’s mid-Republican picture.

Remember

The Republic has two annual consuls, a Senate, and assemblies; it is not a kingship and not a modern democracy. Polybius (2nd c. BCE) is a major source for the mid-Republic and is also making an argument. 509 BCE, as a beginning, is traditional.

Key terms: Roman Republic, consul, Senate.

Atelier · Planes of the head, and one large terminator

90 min · Method: large planes on the same Bargue head or plaster; one main light

Learn

Key terms

planes of the head
Definition
A way of simplifying the head into a few large, relatively flat surfaces that catch or turn from the light — forehead, front plane of the face, side plane, jaw, and the underside of the chin — so the light can be read before small forms are carved in.
Example
A forehead that is one clear plane, turning at a constructed “corner” toward the temple, is easier to shade as a solid than a forehead drawn as a soft puzzle of little bumps from the start.
Not this
Not a claim that a living head is made of cardboard, and not a branded mannequin you must buy. The planes are a tool for seeing.
Connects to
The cube and the egg already taught this habit. Today it is applied to the head you have begun.
terminator of the head
Definition
The boundary where the large form of the head turns out of the light into shadow — a soft or firm line, depending on the light and the plane, but one large event first, not a separate terminator for every feature.
Example
On a head lit from the upper left, one shadow on the far side of the egg, with the features inside or across that large shadow, is the right order.
Not this
Not a tight outline around each feature’s own little shadow as the first act. Local shadows come after the large one is true.
Connects to
Week 4 used lost-and-found edges and accents; those tools apply on the head once the large terminator is in the right place.

Today’s work, on the same head. State three to six large planes: the front of the forehead, the side of the forehead, the front of the face (a slightly lower plane), the side plane of the cheek, the jaw, the underside of the chin, and the cylindrical neck. You need not name a commercial mannequin; you do need to decide where a plane turns.

Light. Keep a single main light. Find the terminator of the whole head. Features that sit in the light stay in the light; features that sit in the large shadow stay in the large shadow, even if you can see a reflected light. Reflected light is weaker than the main light — do not bleach the shadow out.

A caution. Planes are a starting analysis. If the plaster or plate is softer than your constructed box, soften after the turn is understood, not before.

Remember

Simplify the head into a few large planes. Place one terminator for the whole head before local feature shadows. Reflected light stays quieter than the main light. The subject can soften a plane; it should not erase the turn.

Key terms: planes of the head, terminator of the head.

Watch me

  3. The repair is to state the large shadow of the egg first, then put the features into that light scheme.

Modern language · Spanish: indirect-object pronouns

40 min · Method: explicit contrast with direct-object pronouns (week 3); placement before the verb

Learn

Key terms

indirect-object pronoun
Definition
A small word that stands in for the person to whom or for whom something is given, said, or shown: me, te, le, nos, os, les. In a simple sentence it goes immediately before the conjugated verb: Le doy el libro. With an infinitive or a present participle it may also attach: Voy a darle el libro / Le voy a dar el libro; Estoy dándole el libro / Le estoy dando el libro.
Example
Le doy el libro a Ana. (The a Ana can stay, to name or to emphasize the person; le still appears.)
Not this
Not a direct-object pronoun (lo, la, los, las), which stands in for the thing given. “I give it to her” will later need both; today, keep the thing as a noun and practice the person as a pronoun: Le doy el libro.
Connects to
Latin this week marked the same job with a dative ending. Spanish uses a pronoun before the verb, and often keeps a + name as well.
Word How to say it Meaning
me meh to/for me
te teh to/for you (tú)
le leh to/for him, her, or usted
nos nohs to/for us
os ohs to/for you (vosotros)
les lehs to/for them or ustedes
Le doy el libro leh DOY el LEE-broh I give the book to him/her
Me pides agua meh PEE-des AH-gwah You ask me for water

The job. Who receives the thing or the words? That person can be a pronoun: me, te, le, nos, os, les.

The place. Before a conjugated verb: Le doy el libro. Not Doy le el libro.

Naming the person. Spanish often adds a Ana, a ellos even when le or les is already there: Le doy el libro a Ana. The a here is the personal a you have already met, now with an indirect object. It does not replace le.

Original sentences. Te doy un lápiz. Le pido un libro a don Carlos. Nos sirven la comida. Os nombro las palabras. (or a simpler Les digo la frase.)

Not yet required: se lo doy (the spelling change when le meets lo). If you need “I give it to her,” keep the noun for a day or two, or wait until that combination is taught.

Remember

me, te, le, nos, os, les go before the conjugated verb (Le doy el libro). They mark the person to/for whom. a Ana can be added and does not replace le. Not lo/la (the thing). se lo is not required today.

Art history · High Classical Greece: the Parthenon and the Doryphoros

40 min · Method: two type examples, looking first; dates conventional; copies and later damage named

Learn

Key terms

Parthenon
Definition
The temple of Athena on the Acropolis in Athens, built in the mid-fifth century BCE (a conventional dating is about 447–432 BCE). In this course it is the type example of a High Classical Greek Doric temple, studied from photographs and plans, not as a building you are assumed to have stood in.
Example
A view of the west façade shows a Doric colonnade, a sculpted pediment (whose figures are now partly in museums), and a building that has been ruined, restored, and argued over.
Not this
Not a complete “as-new” object in the fifth century’s own photograph — we have later damage, removals, and restorations. And not a generic word for every Greek temple.
Connects to
The kouros and the Kritios Boy (week 4) were the standing figure. Today the architectural and sculptural program of a fifth-century temple is the object, with the same caution about what is original.
Doryphoros (Polykleitos)
Definition
The “Spear-Bearer,” a bronze athlete statue designed by Polykleitos in the fifth century BCE, known mainly from later marble copies. It is a standard example of High Classical contrapposto and of a written canon of proportions that does not survive as a complete treatise in Polykleitos’s own words.
Example
A Roman marble copy shows a standing male, weight on one leg, the other relaxed — a developed form of the contrapposto you already saw in the Kritios Boy.
Not this
Not an original bronze you can simply photograph in a fifth-century state, and not a proof that every Classical statue followed one surviving table of numbers. The canon is reported; the complete text is lost.
Connects to
This course will not invent Polykleitos’s measurements. The usable lesson is the stance, the copy problem, and the date in the High Classical fifth century.

High Classical Greece, in two objects. The Parthenon (a temple on the Athenian Acropolis, mid-fifth century BCE, often dated about 447–432 BCE) and the Doryphoros of Polykleitos (a fifth-century bronze type, known from later copies).

What to look at, in the Parthenon. A Doric order: columns with flutes, simple capitals, a frieze of triglyphs and metopes; a sculpted pediment; a building designed to be walked around and looked up at. Then the later history: explosion, ruin, the removal of sculptures (the “Elgin Marbles” / Parthenon sculptures, a modern controversy this course will name without settling in a slogan), and restoration. A photograph of the building today is not a photograph of 440 BCE.

What to look at, in the Doryphoros. Contrapposto more fully developed than in the Kritios Boy: a clear weight leg and a clear free leg, hips and shoulders in a counter-tilt, the head slightly turned. Then the copy problem: most of what we see are later marble versions of a lost bronze. Describe the copy as a copy.

Dates, said honestly. “About 447–432 BCE” for the Parthenon’s main construction is a conventional scholarly dating, not a claim that this course has re-dated the building. Polykleitos belongs to the fifth century BCE; exact years of the bronze are not required.

Remember

The Parthenon is a mid-fifth-century BCE Doric temple on the Acropolis (conventional dates about 447–432). The Doryphoros is a Polykleitos type known from later copies, a developed contrapposto. A modern photograph is not a fifth-century view. No invented canon numbers.

Key terms: Parthenon, Doryphoros (Polykleitos).
