Latin · Capitulum VI, part 4: on the road

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VI · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms

via
Definition
The Latin word for a road or way (also, by extension, a journey). It is a first-declension noun: via, viam, viā, viae, viīs.
Origin
From Latin via (‘road, way’).
Example
Via est inter Rōmam et oppidum. Mārcus per viam ad vīllam ambulat.
Not this
via is not only the English word via meaning ‘by way of’ (though English borrowed it). In this lesson it is a noun you decline, not a preposition.
Connects to
per viam uses yesterday's accusative preposition per. inter Rōmam et Capuam (a book name you may meet) uses inter + accusative.
īre
Definition
The Latin verb meaning ‘to go’. In this chapter you use the third-person forms it (he/she/it goes) and eunt (they go).
Example
Mārcus Rōmam it. Puerī ad vīllam eunt.
Not this
it is not English it, and eunt is not ‘aunt’. īre is a different verb from venīre (‘to come’): it is ‘go’, venit is ‘comes’.
Connects to
The place constructions of Days 16–17 are the usual complements of īre and venīre: Rōmam it, Rōmā venit, Rōmae habitat.

Mathematics · Geometry IX: the circle, chords

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry, the circle

Learn

Key terms

chord
Definition
A segment joining two points of a circle. A diameter is a chord through the center; it is the longest chord.
Origin
A spelling variant of cord, after Latin chorda, ultimately from Ancient Greek χορδή (‘gut string, the string of a lyre’).
Example
A chord of length 10 in a circle of radius 13 lies 12 units from the center, because a radius, a half-chord and the distance from the center form a right triangle 5-12-13.
Not this
A chord is not an arc (the curved part of the circumference between the same two points), and it is not a tangent (a tangent meets the circle at exactly one point).
Connects to
The right triangle in the figure is yesterday's Pythagorean habit, now built on a radius and a half-chord.
distance from the center
Definition
The perpendicular distance from the center of a circle to a chord. A radius to an endpoint, that perpendicular, and a half-chord form a right triangle.
Example
If the radius is 10 and the chord is 6 from the center, the half-chord is √(10² − 6²) = 8, so the chord is 16.
Not this
This distance is not the length of the chord, and it is not a radius unless the chord is a single point (a degenerate case you will not need).
Connects to
Theorem: the perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Conversely, the line from the center to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord.

A chord joins two points of a circle. A diameter is a chord through the center.

Theorem. The perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Conversely, the segment from the center to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord.

The right triangle. If O is the center, AB a chord, and M the foot of the perpendicular from O to AB, then M is the midpoint of AB, and △OMA is right-angled at M. So

OM² + AM² = OA².

Equal chords lie at equal distances from the center; a longer chord lies closer to the center. The longest chord is a diameter, at distance 0.

Remember

A perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Then OM² + (half-chord)² = radius². Equal chords are at equal distances from the center.

Key terms: chord, distance from the center.

Watch me

A circle has radius 13, and a chord of length 10. How far is the chord from the center?

  1. The perpendicular from the center bisects the chord, so each half is 5.
  2. The right triangle has hypotenuse 13 (a radius) and one leg 5, so the other leg is √(13² − 5²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12.
  3. The chord is 12 from the center. Check: 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13². ✓

Together

A circle has radius 10, and a chord is 6 from the center. Find the length of the chord.

Hint 1: Half

Half the chord is √(10² − 6²) = √(100 − 36) = √64 = 8.

Hint 2: Whole

The whole chord is 16.

Writing & Rhetoric · Quotations and attribution

45 min · Method: introduce, quote, and explain; no quotation left to ‘speak for itself’

Learn

Key terms

quotation
Definition
Someone else’s exact words, placed in quotation marks (or indented as a block, for a longer extract), introduced by a sentence of your own, and followed by an explanation in your own words.
Example
Lincoln writes that the nation was ‘dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.’ The quoted phrase is the standard he then says the war is testing.
Not this
A quotation is not a substitute for a topic sentence, and a sentence that is only a quotation is not a paragraph. Do not let a quoted clause do the arguing for you.
Connects to
Day 9’s analytical essay already required that every quotation be introduced and explained. Today that rule is practiced as a skill of its own.
attribution
Definition
The naming of who said or wrote the words, and in what work or on what occasion, so that a reader can find the source. In this course a short prose quotation is attributed in the sentence that introduces it.
Origin
From Middle French attribution, from Latin attribūtiō.
Example
‘In the first paragraph of the Gettysburg Address, Lincoln writes that…’ is an attribution. A bare pair of quotation marks with no name is not.
Not this
Attribution is not a bibliography entry alone, and it is not ‘someone once said’. If the writer or the work is missing, the quotation is unearned.
Connects to
Public-domain sources still need attribution. The fact that a text is free to quote does not make it anonymous.

A quotation has three parts: an introduction (who, where, and why the words are being used), the words themselves in quotation marks, and an explanation of what the words do in your argument.

How much to quote. Quote a phrase or a sentence you will actually use. Do not quote a whole paragraph and then paraphrase it. If you can say it just as well in your own words, summarize instead (Day 15).

Punctuation, for this course. The introducing clause, then a comma or a colon, then the quotation marks. The explanation is a new sentence, not a trailing ‘which means that’ tacked on without a verb of your own.

Today’s practice. Using Lincoln or Johnson (already in your notes), write three introduced-and-explained quotations that could sit in the two-paragraph essay outlined on Day 16.

Remember

Introduce (who, where, why), quote only what you will use, then explain in your own words. Attribute by name and work. A quotation is not a topic sentence and not a whole paragraph.

Key terms: quotation, attribution.

Watch me

  3. The word ‘inspiring’ is a response, not an explanation, and is dropped.

The introduction before the quotation, and the explanation after it.

Hint 2: Thesis

The paragraph also needs the writer’s own topic sentence; a quotation cannot be the topic sentence.

Physics · Momentum

45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student

Learn

Key terms

momentum p = mv
Definition
The product of mass and velocity, p = m v, a vector in the same direction as the velocity. The SI unit is kg·m/s.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin mōmentum; a doublet of moment and movement.
Example
A 3 kg body moving at 4 m/s has momentum 12 kg·m/s in the direction of the velocity.
Not this
Momentum is not the same as inertia (a property of mass alone), and it is not the same as kinetic energy (which uses v² and is not a vector). A heavy body at rest has zero momentum.
Connects to
Newton’s second law can be read as: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum. Day 10’s third law will give conservation in the next paragraph.
conservation of momentum
Definition
If two (or more) bodies interact and the net external force on the system is zero (or is negligible for a short collision), the total momentum of the system does not change. Internal action–reaction pairs (Day 10) cancel in the total.
Example
A 3 kg cart at 4 m/s catches up to a 1 kg cart at rest, and they stick: 12 = (3+1)v, so v = 3 m/s in the original direction.
Not this
Conservation of momentum is not ‘every body keeps its own momentum’, and it is not a claim that energy is always conserved in a collision (a sticking collision loses kinetic energy).
Connects to
Day 10: forces between two bodies are equal and opposite. Over the same time, the momentum changes are equal and opposite, so the total is constant when no outside force matters.

Momentum is p = m v, a vector. A net force changes momentum: Fnet = Δp / Δt (this is Newton’s second law, written for a change in mv).

A closed system. For two carts on a level track, the internal pushes during a collision are an action–reaction pair (Day 10). If friction and other outside horizontal forces are negligible for that short time, the total momentum p1 + p2 is the same before and after.

A sticking collision (inelastic). 3 kg at 4 m/s, 1 kg at rest, they stick: total p before is 12 kg·m/s, total mass after is 4 kg, so v = 3 m/s. Kinetic energy is not conserved in this collision (check: before, (1/2)(3)(16) = 24 J; after, (1/2)(4)(9) = 18 J). Momentum still is.

Always pick a direction as positive, and let a velocity the other way be negative.

Remember

p = m v, a vector. If the net external force is negligible, the system’s total momentum is conserved, even when kinetic energy is not (as when bodies stick). Choose a positive direction and keep signs.

Key terms: momentum, conservation of momentum.

Watch me

  2. KE before = (1/2)(3)(4)² = 24 J. KE after = (1/2)(4)(3)² = 18 J.
  3. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not. The missing 6 J has gone into deformation and heat, which this course need not model further today.

Hint 2: After

History · The Hellenistic world and Ptolemaic Egypt

45 min · Method: from Alexander’s death to successor kingdoms, with Egypt as the case

Learn

Key terms

Hellenistic
Definition
The period, after Alexander’s death in 323 BCE, in which his empire was divided among successor kingdoms (the Diadochi and their heirs) whose courts used Greek as a language of rule, while older cultures continued. It lasts, in a common textbook frame, down to the Roman take-over of those kingdoms (in Egypt, 30 BCE).
Origin
From German hellenistisch, from Ancient Greek Ἑλληνιστής (‘one who uses the Greek language’), from Ἑλλάς (‘Greece’). In English, Hellenist + -ic.
Example
Ptolemaic Egypt, the Seleucid empire, and the Antigonid kingdom in Macedon are Hellenistic kingdoms. Alexandria, with its museum and library, is a Hellenistic city in Egypt.
Not this
Hellenistic is not ‘Greek instead of Egyptian’. The word names a mixed and often bilingual world. It is also not ‘Hellenic’ as a synonym of classical Athens (Week 2).
Connects to
You have now a sequence: Old Kingdom Egypt; New Kingdom Egypt (Ramesses II); Achaemenid rule; Alexander; the Ptolemies.
Ptolemaic Egypt
Definition
The Hellenistic kingdom in Egypt ruled by the dynasty founded by Ptolemy, a companion of Alexander (the same Ptolemy Arrian later preferred as a source), from the late 4th century BCE until 30 BCE. The kings ruled from Alexandria and used both Greek and Egyptian forms of kingship.
Example
The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE, later than this week’s core texts) is a Ptolemaic inscription in three scripts, and a reminder that the court had to speak to more than one audience. This lesson does not make you read the Stone; it names why such an object exists.
Not this
Ptolemaic Egypt is not the Old Kingdom, and the Ptolemies are not native 4th-Dynasty kings. Calling them ‘pharaohs’ needs a note: they took on Egyptian royal titles as well as Greek ones.
Connects to
Arrian’s Ptolemy is both a source for Alexander and, later, a founder of this dynasty — two jobs that must not be confused in a sentence.

Alexander died in 323 BCE without a secure adult heir. His generals (the Diadochi, ‘successors’) fought, and the empire broke into kingdoms. Egypt came to Ptolemy I and his line: Ptolemaic Egypt, ruled from Alexandria, until 30 BCE.

What ‘Hellenistic’ means here. Greek is a language of court, army, and many cities; Egyptian religion, temples, and rural life continue; the kings use both Greek and Egyptian forms. The word is not a claim that Egypt became ‘just Greece’.

A document as a symptom. The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE) is a later Ptolemaic priestly decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek. You need not read it this week; you do need to know why a decree might be issued in more than one script: more than one audience.

A limit. This lesson is a map, not a full history of the wars of the successors. The aim is to place Ptolemaic Egypt in the sequence you already have, and to keep Arrian’s Ptolemy (the narrator’s source) distinct from Ptolemy the dynast (the same man, two later jobs).

Remember

After 323 BCE the empire splits. Ptolemaic Egypt: Ptolemy’s line in Egypt, from Alexandria, to 30 BCE, using Greek and Egyptian forms of rule. Hellenistic does not mean ‘Egypt became Greece’. The Rosetta Stone (196 BCE) shows a trilingual audience; it is later than Alexander, and you need not read it this week.

Key terms: Hellenistic, Ptolemaic Egypt.

  3. ‘Hellenistic’ names that mixture, not a simple replacement.

Atelier · Lost-and-found edges

45 min · Method: continue the plaster feature or the still life; study the quality of edges · After terminators (Day 8) and today’s preceding solids

Learn

Key terms

lost-and-found edge
Definition
A contour or an inner edge that is sharp in one passage (found) and soft or gone in another (lost), according to the light and the turn of the form. The drawing’s outline is not a wire of even thickness all the way around.
Example
On a sphere, the contour is often a little sharper on the light side and softer, or even lost into the background, on the shadow side. On a plaster nose, a ridge may be found, and the turn into a shadow plane may lose the line.
Not this
A lost edge is not a smudge of dirt, and a found edge is not a cartoon outline. Both must be seen: you lose an edge where the values of two neighboring passages nearly meet, or where the form turns slowly from you.
Connects to
Week 2 already softened a terminator on a curved form. Today that idea is extended to the outer contour and to the joins between objects.
edge quality
Definition
How sudden or gradual a change of value is at a boundary: razor-sharp, firm, soft, or lost. Edge quality is decided after the large values are in, or along with them, not as a decorative outline drawn first and never revised.
Example
A cast-shadow edge on the table may be sharper near the object and softer as it moves away, if that is what the light is doing. A plane break on a cube stays relatively firm.
Not this
Edge quality is not ‘blending everything’, and it is not ‘outlining everything’. It is a choice that follows the look of the set-up.
Connects to
If you find every edge, the form can look cut out. If you lose every edge, the form can fall apart. The work is to vary them on purpose.

A drawing is not a coloring-book outline filled in. Edges change.

Found. A firm, clear boundary: a cube’s plane break, a cast-shadow edge close to the object in a hard light, a ridge of plaster turned against a darker ground.

Lost. A boundary that almost disappears: two values that nearly match (a shadow side against a dark cloth), or a slow turn (the shadow contour of a sphere). You still know the form is there, because the construction and the neighboring values hold it.

Order. Keep yesterday’s (or the still life’s) construction. Then, on a pass of edge quality, walk around every contour and every inner edge and ask: is this found, firm, soft, or lost, in the set-up I am looking at? Change the drawing to match, rather than leaving a wire.

Background. A lost edge often needs the background to be brought up in value so that the contour can disappear into it. Do not leave a white halo around the object ‘to keep it safe’.

A common error. A single, even outline; or a smear that loses a sharp ridge you can clearly see.

Continue one study (plaster feature or the group of solids) and make the edges deliberate.

Remember

Edges are found, firm, soft, or lost, according to the look of the set-up. A lost edge is where values nearly meet or the form turns slowly; a found edge is a clear, seen boundary. Vary them on purpose. Avoid a wire outline and a white halo.

Key terms: lost-and-found edge, edge quality.

Watch me

A student has drawn a perfect, even outline around a sphere, equally sharp in the light and in the shadow. What should they check in the set-up, and what might they do on the shadow side?

  1. They should look at whether the shadow contour is really as sharp as the light contour, and at the value of the background there.
  2. If the shadow side nearly matches the ground, or turns slowly, they should soften or lose the edge, and bring the background value up so a white halo does not remain.
  3. The construction of the sphere stays; only the quality of the edge changes.

Together

When should you refuse to lose an edge, even if you like the idea of lost-and-found?

Hint 1: Seen sharpness

Modern language · Spanish: irregular yo forms

30 min · Method: grouped irregular yo, then contrast saber/conocer · Instruction in English

Learn

Key terms

irregular yo form
Definition
A present-tense first-person singular that does not follow the ordinary stem + -o pattern, while the other persons are often regular (or follow a pattern already taught). Today’s set: hago, pongo, salgo, traigo, vengo, digo, oigo, conozco, sé, veo, doy. You already know soy, estoy, voy, and tengo.
Example
Hacer: hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen. The yo form is the odd one; haces is regular from the stem hac-.
Not this
An irregular yo is not a new tense, and it does not make every form irregular. Do not write *haco or *sabo.
Connects to
Once you know hago, the rest of hacer in the present is the regular -er pattern from hac-.
saber and conocer
Definition
Two verbs that both translate as ‘to know’. Saber is to know a fact, or to know how to do something (saber + infinitive). Conocer is to be acquainted with a person, a place, or a work. Their yo forms are sé and conozco.
Example
Sé la respuesta. Sé dibujar. Conozco la ciudad. Conozco a Ana (personal a, because the object is a person).
Not this
Do not swap them: *Conozco la respuesta is not the usual way to say you know a fact, and *Sé a Ana is wrong.
Connects to
Conocer + a person needs the personal a from Day 17. Saber does not take that a before a fact.

Some verbs have an irregular yo in the present. Learn them in groups. The other persons often stay ordinary.

-go verbs. hacer → hago (haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen). poner → pongo. salir → salgo. traer → traigo.

venir (e → ie, plus irregular yo): vengo, vienes, viene, venimos, venís, vienen.

decir is taught as a listed set (the yo form is digo; the other stressed forms have e → i, which is not to be copied onto pensar): digo, dices, dice, decimos, decís, dicen.

-zco. conocer → conozco, conoces, conoce, conocemos, conocéis, conocen.

Others to memorize. ver → veo, ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven. saber → sé, sabes, sabe, sabemos, sabéis, saben. dar → doy, das, da, damos, dais, dan. You already have soy, estoy, voy, tengo.

saber vs conocer. Sé la lección. Sé nadar. Conozco a la profesora. Conozco este libro. (A book can be ‘known’ as a work; a fact still takes saber.)

Art history · The kouros and contrapposto

30 min · Method: close looking, then a comparison across two periods · After Day 13 (Aegean) and Day 8 (Egypt)

Learn

Key terms

kouros
Definition
An Archaic Greek standing youth, typically in marble, usually nude, with the left foot forward, the weight still even on both legs, the arms at the sides, and often an ‘Archaic smile’. The plural is kouroi. The word is Ionic Greek for ‘boy’ or ‘youth’.
Origin
From Ionic Greek κοῦρος, a variant of Attic κόρος (‘boy’).
Example
A kouros may have been a grave marker or a dedication; a particular statue’s function has to be argued from its find-spot and inscriptions, when those exist, not assumed for every example.
Not this
A kouros is not a Classical athlete in contrapposto, and it is not a Cycladic folded-arm figure. The even stance and the smile are part of the Archaic convention, not a candid photograph of a mood.
Connects to
You have seen an Egyptian standing figure with one foot forward (Day 8). The comparison is useful, and so are the differences: the typical kouros is nude, and the carving is not in the same composite-view convention.
contrapposto
Definition
A standing pose in which the weight rests mainly on one leg, the other is relaxed, and the hips and shoulders tilt in opposite directions, so the spine forms a slight S-curve. The Early Classical marble youth known as the Kritios Boy (Acropolis, Athens, early fifth century BCE) is a standard example.
Origin
Borrowed from Italian contrapposto (‘contrasting, opposing’), the past participle of contrapporre (‘to set against’), from Latin contrāpōnere, from contrā (‘against’) + pōnō (‘I place’).
Example
In the Kritios Boy the weight shifts to one leg, the pelvis tilts, the head turns slightly, and the Archaic smile is gone. The figure looks able to move.
Not this
Contrapposto is not ‘standing on one foot as a trick’, and it is not the same thing as a walking figure with both legs stiff. It is a counterpoise of the whole body.
Connects to
Day 15’s anatomy (the weight-bearing hip, the relaxed knee) is the living version of what this pose requires. The art-historical name is for the designed pose in sculpture.

Two moments in the Greek standing figure, not a story of ‘progress’ as a moral, but a change you can see.

The kouros (Archaic). A typical kouros stands frontal, left foot forward, weight even, fists close to the thighs, hair often patterned in a bead-like or wig-like convention, the face with the Archaic smile. Dates for the type run through the seventh and sixth centuries BCE; do not treat a single year as exact unless an inscription or a secure context forces it. Function: some are funerary, some votive; argue from evidence.

Comparison with Egypt. The shared ‘one foot forward, frontal, compact arms’ is real and often taught as a connection. The differences are also real: nudity as a convention for the male youth, a different treatment of the body in the round, and no Egyptian composite view (profile head with a frontal eye, and so on).

Contrapposto and the Kritios Boy. In the early fifth century BCE, after the Archaic period, the Kritios Boy (found on the Acropolis of Athens; the name comes from a later attribution, not from a signature on the statue that we must take as certain) shows weight on one leg, a relaxed other leg, a tilt of the pelvis, a slight turn of the head, and no Archaic smile. That counter-tilt is contrapposto.

Look at the two stances as problems of balance. What has to happen at the hip, the knee, and the shoulder for the weight to shift and the figure still to stand?

Remember

A kouros is an Archaic standing youth: nude, left foot forward, even weight, often an Archaic smile. Contrapposto shifts the weight to one leg and counter-tilts hips and shoulders; the Kritios Boy (Acropolis, early fifth century BCE) is the type example in this lesson. Compare with Egypt without erasing differences.

Key terms: kouros, contrapposto.

  2. The Kritios Boy rests the weight on one leg, lets the other relax, tilts the pelvis, and slightly turns the head; the smile is gone.
  3. The difference is in the balance of the whole figure, not only in ‘one foot in front’.
