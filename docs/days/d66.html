Latin · Capitulum XII, part 1: third-declension adjectives (fortis, -e)

40 minutes · Method: a new adjective pattern, in original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

third-declension adjective
Definition
An adjective that uses third-declension endings and agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender. The first look in this chapter is the pair of forms such as fortis (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and forte (neuter nominative or accusative singular).
Example
Mīles fortis est. The adjective fortis matches mīles (masculine nominative singular).
Not this
Not a first-and-second-declension adjective such as magnus, magna, magnum, which was already taught. Fortis does not become *fortus.
Connects to
Capitulum XII begins these adjectives, with original sentences, not a copied textbook story. Agreement is the same job as with magnus: the endings simply belong to a different pattern.
fortis, -e
Definition
A third-declension adjective meaning strong or brave. The dictionary pair is fortis, forte: fortis for the masculine or feminine nominative singular, forte for the neuter nominative or accusative singular.
Example
Mīles fortis est. Scūtum forte est.
Not this
Not *fortus, and not a change of meaning between the two example sentences: the endings change to match the noun, the sense stays “strong” or “brave.”
Connects to
Other adjectives of this pattern in the chapter’s first look include tristis, -e (sad). The sentences used here are original.
Word How to say it Meaning
mīles MEE-les a soldier (nominative singular, masculine)
fortis FOR-tis strong, brave (masculine or feminine nominative singular)
forte FOR-teh strong (neuter nominative or accusative singular)
Mīles fortis est MEE-les FOR-tis est The soldier is brave

Capitulum XII is titled Mīles Rōmānus in the course’s chapter list. This sitting begins the chapter’s first new job: third-declension adjectives. You already know adjectives such as magnus, magna, magnum, which take first- and second-declension endings. A third-declension adjective uses a different set of endings, but it still agrees with its noun in case, number, and gender.

The first pair to learn is fortis, -e: fortis in the masculine or feminine nominative singular, forte in the neuter nominative or accusative singular. So Mīles fortis est (the soldier is brave), and Scūtum forte est (the shield is strong). The sense is the same kind of describing; the ending changes to match the noun.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Mīles fortis est. Scūtum forte est. Mīles nōn tristis est. Scūtum nōn forte est.

The wider army vocabulary of the chapter, the dative of possession, comparatives, and the fourth declension are not today’s jobs.

Remember

A third-declension adjective agrees with its noun, but its endings are not those of magnus, -a, -um. fortis is the masculine or feminine nominative singular; forte is the neuter nominative or accusative singular. Today’s sentences are original.

Key terms: third-declension adjective, fortis, -e.

Mathematics · Geometry XXIX: the Pythagorean theorem

40 minutes · Method: a right triangle, squares on the sides, then a² + b² = c²

Learn

Key terms

Pythagorean theorem
Definition
In a right triangle, the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. If the legs are a and b and the hypotenuse is c, then a² + b² = c².
Example
A triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5 is right-angled at the angle between 3 and 4, because 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5².
Not this
Not a claim about every triangle, only a right triangle. A triangle with sides 3, 5, and 6 (already used as a similarity example) is not made right by this theorem: 3² + 5² = 9 + 25 = 34, which is not 6².
Connects to
This is the content of Euclid I.47. Today’s first picture is the squares on the sides of a 3-4-5 triangle. A second proof, and the converse, come later this week.
hypotenuse
Definition
The side opposite the right angle in a right triangle. It is the longest side of that triangle.
Origin
From Latin hypotenusa, from Ancient Greek ὑποτείνουσα πλευρά, “side subtending [the right angle],” from ὑποτείνω, “to subtend, to stretch under.”
Example
In a 3-4-5 right triangle, the hypotenuse is 5, the side that lies opposite the right angle.
Not this
Not either leg (the two sides that form the right angle), and not a name for a side of an acute or obtuse triangle, which has no right angle and so has no hypotenuse.
Connects to
The word comes from Latin hypotenusa, from Greek ὑποτείνουσα πλευρά, “the side subtending (the right angle),” from ὑποτείνω, “to stretch under, to subtend.”

In a right triangle, one angle is a right angle. The two sides that form that angle are the legs. The side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse.

The Pythagorean theorem says: the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the two legs. If the legs are a and b and the hypotenuse is c, then

a² + b² = c².

The figure is a 3-4-5 triangle, with a square drawn on each side. The square on 3 has area 9, the square on 4 has area 16, and the square on 5 has area 25. Then 9 + 16 = 25, so 3² + 4² = 5². The right-angle mark is at the angle between the sides 3 and 4; that is the only right angle in the figure.

This is the content of Euclid I.47. Today’s job is the statement, the names (leg, hypotenuse), and a first numerical check. A second proof, using similar triangles from week 13, comes tomorrow. The converse (if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right) is not yet today’s claim.

Remember

In a right triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle). A 3-4-5 triangle satisfies 9 + 16 = 25. The theorem is about right triangles, not about every triangle.

Key terms: Pythagorean theorem, hypotenuse.

Writing & Rhetoric · The précis

40 minutes · Method: a short, faithful restatement in your own words, on a set extract

Learn

Key terms

précis
Definition
A short restatement of a passage in your own words, that keeps the author’s points and their order and proportion, without adding a new opinion, and without simply quoting.
Origin
Borrowed from French précis, “summary,” from a substantive use of the adjective meaning “precise.”
Example
A précis of the set Procopius sentence-group would keep Justinian, the disordered state he took over, and that he made it both larger and more illustrious, including by expelling barbarians, and would not add a modern judgment of his reign.
Not this
Not a quotation (which keeps the author’s wording), not a loose summary that drops the order or the main points, and not a reaction or a review.
Connects to
The word is borrowed from French précis, “summary,” from the adjective meaning “precise.” A précis is exact in that sense: it is short, but it is faithful.

A précis is a short restatement of a passage in your own words. It keeps the author’s points, in their order and in fair proportion. It does not add a new opinion. It is not a quotation, which keeps the author’s wording. It is also tighter than a loose summary that drops a main point or rearranges the passage until the author’s shape is gone.

The set passage for this week’s writing is one sentence-group from Procopius, The Buildings, I.1.6, in H. B. Dewing’s 1940 translation (public domain):

In our own age there has been born the Emperor Justinian, who, taking over the State when it was harassed by disorder, has not only made it greater in extent, but also much more illustrious, by expelling from it those barbarians who had from of old pressed hard upon it, as I have made clear in detail in the Books on the Wars.

A sound précis of this passage will still have Justinian, the state he took over (harassed by disorder), and the two claims that he made it greater in extent and more illustrious, including by expelling barbarians who had long pressed upon it. It will not quietly drop “more illustrious,” or add a modern verdict on Justinian, or paste Dewing’s wording and call that a précis.

Physics · The center of mass

40 minutes · Method: a balance point, then mass × distance, with labeled invented numbers

Learn

Key terms

center of mass
Definition
The balance point of a system of masses, treated as the single point at which the system’s mass may be taken to act for the purpose of translation, or of balance under gravity (when that point is also called the center of gravity, for a uniform gravitational field).
Example
For two labeled invented masses, 2 and 4, on a light bar, the center of mass lies closer to the 4: twice as far from the 2 as from the 4, so the products of mass and distance match, as in last week’s lever.
Not this
Not necessarily the geometric midpoint, unless the masses (or a uniform object) are symmetric. A uniform bar does have its center of mass at its midpoint; a bar with unequal masses does not.
Connects to
Last week’s lever, in this course’s first model, already used mass × distance on each side. The center of mass is the point at which those products balance. Numbers in the figure are invented and labeled invented.

The center of mass of a system is its balance point: the point at which the system’s mass may be taken to act, for balance under gravity (in a uniform field, also called the center of gravity).

A uniform bar, with mass spread evenly, has its center of mass at the midpoint. That is a special case, not the general one.

For two masses on a light bar, the center of mass is the point at which the bar would balance, the same habit as last week’s lever: mass × distance on the left equals mass × distance on the right, in this course’s first model. In the figure, a labeled invented mass 2 sits 4 units from the fulcrum, and a labeled invented mass 4 sits 2 units from the fulcrum, because 2 × 4 = 4 × 2. The balance point is closer to the larger mass.

These masses and distances are invented classroom numbers, labeled invented. This lesson does not invent an official NASA or laboratory center-of-mass figure for a named spacecraft or a named building.

Remember

The center of mass is the balance point. A uniform bar balances at its midpoint. For two masses on a light bar, mass × distance matches on each side, as in last week’s lever, so the point lies closer to the larger mass. Invented numbers stay labeled invented.

Justinian
Definition

Atelier · The eye sockets, on the blocked-in head

40 minutes · Method: sockets as planes, fitted to last week’s block-in

Learn

Key terms

the eye socket, as a plane
Definition
The bony recess of the eye, first drawn as a shallow plane and a socket, not as a finished eyelash or a bright highlight. On the blocked-in head, each socket is placed in relation to the mid-line and to the brow already set last week.
Example
A first pass marks two sockets, level with each other, under the brow plane, without drawing individual lashes.
Not this
Not a decorative eye copied from a photograph, and not a first look at the whole head (the head was blocked in last week). A socket is a form; lashes are later, if they are wanted at all.
Connects to
Last week’s proportions and the large terminator stay in place. Features are fitted into that head, not drawn as a separate mask.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -er verbs

15–20 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · regular -er preterite only

Learn

Key terms

the preterite of regular -er verbs
Definition
The past-tense forms of a regular -er verb, such as comer: comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron. These endings are not the -ar endings already taught (hablé, hablaste, habló…).
Example
Ayer comí pan. Anoche comió fruta.
Not this
Not the present (como, comes, come), and not the -ar preterite (hablé, habló). Comí is not *comé.
Connects to

Last week taught the preterite of regular -ar verbs (hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablasteis, hablaron). Today is the preterite of regular -er verbs, with comer (to eat) as the model:

comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron.

These endings are not the -ar set. Comí is not comé. The time words already taught — ayer, anoche, el lunes pasado — still mark the past.

Original sentences: Ayer comí pan. Anoche comió fruta. El lunes pasado comimos pan. Ayer no comí fruta.

Instruction stays in English. This is not a history retelling, and irregular pasts are not begun. Regular -ir verbs, which share these same preterite endings, are tomorrow’s job.

Logic · Darii, a first-figure AII syllogism

30 minutes · Method: a second valid first-figure form, beside last week’s Celarent

Learn

Key terms

Darii
Definition
A valid first-figure syllogism of the form AII: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The major premise is universal affirmative, the minor and the conclusion particular affirmative.
Example
All soldiers are brave; some of these men are soldiers; therefore some of these men are brave.
Not this
Not Celarent (EAE), already taught, and not a particular major with a universal minor in this figure, which would not be Darii. “Some M are P; all S are M; therefore…” is a different arrangement, and is not today’s form.
Connects to
As with Celarent, the major term is P (the predicate of the conclusion), the minor term is S (the subject of the conclusion), and the middle term M appears in both premises and not in the conclusion.

Last week taught Celarent (EAE): no M are P; all S are M; therefore no S are P. Today is another valid first-figure form, Darii (AII):

  • All M are P.
  • Some S are M.
  • Therefore some S are P.

The major premise is universal affirmative (all M are P). The minor premise and the conclusion are particular affirmative (some S are M; some S are P). The middle term M still joins the premises and does not appear in the conclusion. The major term P is the predicate of the conclusion; the minor term S is the subject of the conclusion — the same jobs as last week.

A model, in invented classroom wording, not a historical claim: “All soldiers are brave; some of these men are soldiers; therefore some of these men are brave.” The example is for the form, not a claim about every soldier in Procopius.

Darii is not Celarent. Do not swap “all” and “no,” or turn the conclusion into a universal (“all of these men are brave”), which the particular minor does not give.

Remember

Darii is AII in the first figure: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The middle term still joins the premises. It is not Celarent (EAE), and the conclusion stays particular.

Key terms: Darii.

Watch me

Write a Darii syllogism in the pattern all / some / therefore some, using the terms “masts,” “wooden things,” and “these objects.”

  1. All masts are wooden things; some of these objects are masts; therefore some of these objects are wooden things.
  2. The conclusion is particular, because the minor is particular.
