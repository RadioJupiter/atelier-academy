Latin · Capitulum XV: prior, posterior, and īnferior

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV continues. Original sentences only; the book’s story is read in the book, not copied here.

Learn

Key terms

prior, posterior, and īnferior
Definition
These three comparatives order or locate, rather than only comparing a quality such as longior. Prior, prius means the first of two, the former. Posterior, posterius means the one behind, or the latter of two. Īnferior, īnferius means the one below, the lower.
Example
Liber prior — the former of the two books. Sella īnferior — the lower seat. Sellae posteriōrēs — the seats at the back.
Not this
Not a first look at ordinary comparatives of quality, already taught in Capitulum XII (longior, brevior). Prior is a true comparative with no ordinary positive taught in this course; do not invent a positive form for it here.
Connects to
Last week closed the present table and taught sī, nisi, at, antequam, and licet. Today stays in Capitulum XV and adds this ordering set.

Capitulum XV is still open. The book’s story stays in the book. Here the new grammar is taught in original sentences.

Remember

Prior is the first of two; posterior is the one behind, or the latter of two; īnferior is the lower. These order or locate. They are not a first look at longior and the quality comparatives of Capitulum XII.

Mathematics · Geometry XLII: the volume of a right rectangular prism

45 min · Method: packing unit cubes, then V = length × width × height

Learn

Key terms

volume of a solid
Definition
The volume of a solid is the amount of space it occupies, measured in cubic units. A classroom cube of edge 1 has volume 1 cubic unit. Volume is not the same as area, which measures a face, and not the same as last week’s density, which is mass per unit volume.
Example
A classroom box that holds 24 unit cubes has volume 24 cubic units, however those cubes are arranged, so long as they fill the box without gaps or overlaps.
Not this
Not the area of a face, and not last week’s circle area πr², which is a measure of a flat region. Not a mass, and not a density.
Connects to
Last week measured length around a circle and area inside it. Today begins the space inside a solid.
volume of a right rectangular prism
Definition
A right rectangular prism, or classroom box, has three edges that meet at right angles: a length ℓ, a width w, and a height h. Its volume is V = ℓ × w × h, in cubic units. That product is the number of unit cubes that fill the box.
Example
A classroom prism of length 6, width 3, and height 4 has volume V = 6 × 3 × 4 = 72 cubic units.
Not this
Not the surface area (the total area of the faces), which is reserved. Not a slanted solid, and not a cylinder, which comes later this week.
Connects to
If you think of a layer of ℓ × w unit cubes, there are h such layers, so the volume is (ℓ × w) × h.

Last week’s work was length and area in a plane, including the area of a circle. Today is the space inside a solid, its volume, measured in cubic units.

A right rectangular prism is a classroom box: three edges meet at right angles. Pack it with unit cubes. One layer is a rectangle of ℓ by w cubes, so that layer has ℓ × w cubes. There are h layers. So

V = ℓ × w × h.

For the classroom prism in the diagram, V = 6 × 3 × 4 = 72 cubic units. The figures are invented and labeled invented. Leave units in the answer when they are given; if a problem uses unnamed classroom units, “cubic units” is enough.

Remember

Volume is the space a solid occupies, in cubic units. For a right rectangular prism, V = ℓ × w × h, the number of unit cubes that fill the box. This is not face area, and not density.

Key terms: volume of a solid, volume of a right rectangular prism.

Watch me

  2. There are 4 layers, so V = 18 × 4 = 72 cubic units.
  3. The same product in one step is 6 × 3 × 4 = 72. The order of multiplication does not change the volume.

Definition
Hints from a set verse inscription, in Franklin’s method already taught, are brief cues for each line or tightly bound group of lines, written in order, strong enough to recall the thought and not the original wording. The inscription here is Inferno III.1–9 in Longfellow.

Physics · Elastic potential energy, PE_el = ½kx²

40 min · Method: the energy stored in a Hooke’s-law spring is the area under the force–displacement graph, PE_el = ½kx², from OpenStax 16.1, with sourced Example 16.2

Learn

Key terms

elastic potential energy
Definition
Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in a system that obeys Hooke’s law when it is stretched or compressed from equilibrium. OpenStax 16.1 gives PE_el = ½kx², which is the area of the triangle under a straight-line graph of the applied force kx against the displacement x.
Example
OpenStax Example 16.2 uses a toy dart gun with k = 50.0 N/m and x = 0.150 m. PE_el = ½ × 50.0 × (0.150)² = 0.563 J. Those figures are OpenStax’s, not invented.
Not this
Not a first look at Hooke’s law, F = −kx, or at the force constant k, taught last week. Not a first look at work, kinetic energy, or potential energy as kinds, already taught in Days 21–28. Not gravitational potential energy, and not a claim that every spring stores this energy once it has left Hooke’s-law behavior.
Connects to
The minus sign in F = −kx is still the restoring force. The work you do to stretch the spring slowly is the stored energy, ½kx², and is not itself negative in this formula.

Last week’s Hooke’s law was the force: F = −kx. Today is the energy stored when that law holds.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.1 (CC BY), notes that the work done to stretch or compress such a spring equals the area under the graph of force against displacement. When the size of the force is kx and the graph is a straight line, that area is a triangle of base x and height kx:

PEel = ½kx².

OpenStax Example 16.2 (the toy dart gun, in section 16.1) uses k = 50.0 N/m and a compression x = 0.150 m:

PEel = ½ × 50.0 × (0.150)² = ½ × 50.0 × 0.0225 = 0.563 J.

Those figures are OpenStax’s. A classroom Together problem later is invented and labeled invented. Units: k in N/m, x in m, energy in joules. Kinetic energy, and the conversion of this stored energy into motion, wait until Wednesday.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.1, “Hooke’s Law: Stress and Strain Revisited” (CC BY): https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-1-hookes-law-stress-and-strain-revisited. The formula PE_el = ½kx² and Example 16.2 (toy gun: k = 50.0 N/m, x = 0.150 m, PE_el = 0.563 J; a 2.00 g projectile, friction and the mass of the spring neglected, v = 23.7 m/s) are in that section. Section 16.5, “Energy and the Simple Harmonic Oscillator,” is a related later discussion of energy in SHM, not the source of those example figures: https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-5-energy-and-the-simple-harmonic-oscillator. Sourced numerical examples are copied from OpenStax and labeled as such. Other classroom stretches and masses are invented and labeled invented.

Remember

When Hooke’s law holds, the stored elastic potential energy is PE_el = ½kx², the area of the triangle under the kx-against-x graph. OpenStax Example 16.2: k = 50.0 N/m, x = 0.150 m, PE_el = 0.563 J. This is not a first look at F = −kx, work, or energy as kinds.

History · The one device: four-cornered courses and lead in the joints (Dewing I.1.50–53)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.50–53; no invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

lead in the joints (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.53, the pier stones “were held together neither by lime (titanos), which they call “asbestus”, nor by asphalt, the material which was the pride of Semiramis in Babylon, nor by any other such thing, but by lead (molibdos) poured into the interstices, which flowed about everywhere in the spaces between the stones and hardened in the joints, binding them to each other.”
Example
The lead is the one binding material Dewing names for this device. Lime and asphalt are the rejected comparisons, not the method used.
Not this
Not a first look at Justinian, Anthemius, or Isidorus, already named in earlier weeks, and not a first look at last week’s four piers as supports. Not an invented weight of lead, and not a claim that Dewing has described every device that secured the church.

four-cornered courses (Dewing)
Definition

Corner stones are cut to the angles; stones between the corners are cut as rectangles. This is the shape of the courses, not a modern plan measurement.
Not this

Atelier · Modeling in the light family, still monochrome

60 min · Method: modest modeling inside the already-stated light family, without breaking the family or jumping to full color

Learn

Key terms

modeling in the light family
Definition
Modeling in the light family, at this stage, means small, monochrome changes of value inside the light, to show a turn of the form, while the whole family remains lighter than the shadow family. The shadow family’s already-modeled turns are left almost alone.
Example
A careful halftone, just inside the light and still lighter than the shadow, may state a turn toward the terminator. It must still belong to the light family when seen at a distance.
Not this
Not a first look at the families, or at last week’s modeling in the shadow. Not a finish of the features, and not full color, which remains reserved for Term 3. Not a flattening wash that steals the light’s breadth.
Connects to
Last week reserved the light family on purpose. Today is that reserved step, still in grisaille or in a named drawing rehearsal if paint is not ready.

Last week’s work stated the families, checked them, and modeled a little inside the shadow. The light family was left almost untouched on purpose. Today is that reserved step: modest modeling in the light, still monochrome.

Key terms

dije
Definition
Dije is the first-person singular preterite of decir, to say or to tell. The stem is dij-: dije, dijiste, dijo, dijimos, dijisteis, dijeron. There is no i in dijeron (not dijieron).
Example
Ayer dije la verdad. — Yesterday I told the truth. (Invented.) Ella no dijo nada. — She said nothing. (Invented.)

Elective · Logic: Festino, a second-figure EIO syllogism

40 min · Method: Festino as second-figure EIO, after last week’s Camestres, with the middle term as predicate of both premises

Learn

Key terms

Festino
Definition
Festino is a second-figure syllogism of the form EIO: a universal negative premise, a particular affirmative premise, and a particular negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P.
Example
No birds are cats. Some pets are cats. Therefore some pets are not birds. The middle term, “cats,” is the predicate of both premises.
Not this
Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2) or Camestres (AEE-2), already taught. Festino’s conclusion is particular (Some S are not P), not universal. Not Baroco, which is reserved.
Connects to
Last week: Camestres, AEE, second figure. Today: Festino, EIO, still second figure. The vowels in the name mark the three propositions: E, I, O.

Last week’s Camestres was second-figure AEE: All P are M; No S are M; therefore No S are P. Today is the next second-figure mood taught in this course, Festino, whose vowels are E, I, O:

No P are M.

Some S are M.

Therefore Some S are not P.

The middle term M is still the predicate of both premises, which is what makes the figure the second figure, already taught. What is new is the particular premise and the particular conclusion.

A clean classroom example: No birds are cats. Some pets are cats. Therefore some pets are not birds.

A near-miss is to write a universal conclusion, “No pets are birds.” The premises do not give that: they give only that some pets, namely those that are cats, are not birds. Another near-miss is to begin with All P are M and still call the mood Festino. If the first premise is a universal affirmative about P, you are no longer in Festino (and you may have slipped toward a reserved or different mood).

Remember

Festino is EIO in the second figure: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P. Camestres, already taught, is AEE in the same figure. The conclusion of Festino is particular, not universal. Baroco is reserved.

Key terms: Festino.

Watch me

Watch the pets-and-cats example labeled as Festino, then a universal-conclusion miss.

  1. No birds (P) are cats (M). That is E: No P are M.
  2. Some pets (S) are cats (M). That is I: Some S are M.
  3. Therefore some pets are not birds: Some S are not P. “No pets are birds” would overstep the premises.

Together

Hint 1: The vowels

Hint 2: The figure

Circles is the predicate of both premises, so the figure is the second.
