Latin · A purpose clause with nē, a first look

40 min · Method: nē + the already-taught present subjunctive of sequor; contrasted with ut and with sequētur; original sentences; result clauses reserved

Learn

Key terms

a purpose clause with nē, a first look
Definition
A purpose clause with nē, in this first look, marks a negative purpose: in order that something not happen. The verb in the nē-clause is the already-taught present subjunctive. Nē eum sequātur may be glossed in order that she not follow him, or in order that he not follow him, according to the subject. It is not last week’s ut eum sequātur, which marks a positive purpose, and not the future sequētur. It is not a result clause, which remains reserved.
Example
In an original sentence, Puella manet nē eum sequātur: she stays in order that she not follow him. The verb in the nē-clause is sequātur, with ā, not the future sequētur. The sentence is original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg.
Not this
Not ut eum sequātur, last week’s positive purpose with ut. Not sequētur, the future. Not a result clause, which remains reserved. Not a first look at nē as if it were already last week’s ut.
Connects to
Last week’s purpose clause with ut and the present subjunctive of sequor and of ēgredior. Ēgredior in a nē-clause waits until tomorrow.

Last week’s purpose clause used ut and the present subjunctive to mark a positive purpose: Puella venit ut eum sequātur, she comes in order that she follow him. Today the new first look is a negative purpose with nē. Puella manet nē eum sequātur: she stays in order that she not follow him. The verb in the nē-clause is still the present subjunctive already taught, sequātur with ā, not the future sequētur with ē. The first-person sequar is still spelled the same as the future, so a purpose clause is clearer in the other persons. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the English gloss, not with an unquoted English “boy” or “girl.” A nē-clause with ēgredior waits until tomorrow. A result clause remains reserved.

40 min · Method: if lim f = L and lim g = M, then lim (f + g) = L + M; invented functions; continuity and a formal epsilon argument reserved

Learn

Key terms

the limit of a sum, a first look
Definition
If two quantities have limits, the limit of their sum is the sum of those limits. In the notation already taught, if lim x→a f(x) = L and lim x→a g(x) = M, then lim x→a (f(x) + g(x)) = L + M. The figures used today are invented and labeled invented. This is not yet continuity, and not a formal epsilon argument, both of which remain reserved.
Example
On invented functions f(x) = 2x and g(x) = x + 1, as x approaches 3, lim f = 6 and lim g = 4, already available from last week’s first look at a limit. Then lim (f + g) = 6 + 4 = 10. The same approaching can be seen from the invented values of 2x + (x + 1) = 3x + 1, which approach 10.

Key terms

invective (progymnasmata)
Definition
An invective, in this course, is a blame of a general type, or of an already-taught fault of a type, not a blame of a living person, and not a first look at a new untried claim. It names what is blamed and enlarges already-taught reasons why that type deserves blame. It is not a commonplace, which amplifies a general already-acknowledged fault, and not an encomium, which praises a type or an already-taught work.

History · Six islands on the aforesaid Sunday, and the pilots’ leagues, from the second-voyage letter

40 min · Method: Major 1870 wording, confirmed from the page; six islands; pilots’ 800 or 780, plus 300, 1100 in all; Major’s footnotes distinct from the body; no official conversion of leagues

Learn

Key terms

six islands on the aforesaid Sunday, and the pilots’ leagues
Definition
In Major’s 1870 wording of the second-voyage letter, confirmed from the page, after last week’s landfall “on the first Sunday after All Saints, namely, the third of November,” the letter continues: “On the morning of the aforesaid Sunday, we saw lying before us an island, and soon on the right hand another appeared: the first was high and mountainous, on the side nearest to us; the other flat, and very thickly wooded.” “As soon as it became lighter, other islands began to appear on both sides; so that on that day, there were six islands to be seen lying in different directions, and most of them of considerable size.” The pilots “reckoned on that day, that between leaving Ferro and first reaching land, we had made eight hundred leagues; others said seven hundred and eighty (so that the difference was not great), and three hundred more between Ferro and Cadiz, making in all eleven hundred leagues.” Those figures are the letter’s, not a modern official conversion. Footnotes on that page name “The island of Dominica, so called from having been discovered on a Sunday,” and “The island Marigalante, so called from the name of the ship in which Columbus sailed.” Those names are Major’s notes, not wording that stands in the body of the letter, and they are not taught as if they stood in the body.

Spanish · The irregular participle visto, a first look

40 min · Method: he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han + visto, from ver; contrasted with hay, haya, and last week’s dicho, hecho, and escrito; other irregulars and the present-perfect subjunctive reserved

Learn

Key terms

the irregular participle visto, a first look
Definition
The present perfect, already taught with regular participles and with dicho, hecho, and escrito, now takes one more irregular participle as a first look: visto, from ver, to see. He visto is I have seen. Has visto is you (s.) have seen. Ha visto is he has seen, or she has seen. The helping forms remain he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han. This is still not hay, there is, or there are, and not haya, “that there be.” Other irregular participles remain reserved. A present-perfect subjunctive remains reserved.
Example
A fair first sentence: Ella ha visto la carta, she has seen the letter. The helping form is ha, and the participle is irregular, visto, not vido. The sentence is original classroom Spanish.
Not this
Not dicho, hecho, or escrito, already taught, used as if they were new. Not hay, and not haya. Not vido, which is not the form taught here. Not a present-perfect subjunctive, which remains reserved.
