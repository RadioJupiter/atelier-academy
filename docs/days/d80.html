Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes the area of a parallelogram (A = b × h), a triangle as half that parallelogram (½bh), the area of a trapezoid (A = ½(a + b)h), and a regular polygon’s area as ½ × perimeter × apothem, including a regular hexagon as six equilateral triangles. The circle’s area is still reserved. No new formula is begun.

Remember

A = bh for a parallelogram; ½bh for the triangle on the same base and height; ½(a + b)h for a trapezoid; ½ × perimeter × apothem for a regular polygon. Height is perpendicular. A slanted side is not the height.

This Friday mixes circular reasoning, post hoc, the multi-paragraph outline, and a paragraph written from that outline. No new fallacy is begun. The analytical essay remains the Day 9 form, now planned across more than one paragraph.

Remember

A circle restates the claim as support. Post hoc treats sequence as cause. A multi-paragraph outline plans a thesis, paragraph claims and grounds, and an other-side note, before the draft. One paragraph opens with its claim and explains a ground.

Physics · Friday mix: oscillation and the simple pendulum

45 min · Friday mix of this week’s oscillations, not a new oscillator

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • oscillation — Day 76
  • period (oscillation) — Day 76
  • simple pendulum — Day 78

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes oscillation, period, frequency, and the simple pendulum for a small swing, with T = 2π√(L/g) from OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.4. No new kind of oscillator is begun. g stays the already-taught Earth figure, about 9.8 m/s². Classroom timings stay labeled invented unless they are calculations from the formula.

Remember

Oscillation: a repeating back-and-forth. T is the time for one cycle; f = 1/T. For a simple pendulum and a small swing (less than about 15°), T = 2π√(L/g); T depends on L and g, not on mass.

Watch me

  1. f = 1/2 cycle per second.
  2. About 2.0 s, from T = 2π√(1/9.8), a calculation, not an official stopwatch certificate.

Together

What happens to T if L is made four times as long, in this model?

Hint 1

T goes with √L.

Hint 2

T becomes twice as long.

Check

These mix this week’s oscillation work.

  1. What limit does OpenStax 16.4 place on the simple formula, in degrees of amplitude?
  2. Does a heavier bob change T, in this model?

Exercises

  1. If f = 2 cycles per second, what is T?
  2. Why is a 2 s classroom timing labeled invented, while ‘about 2.0 s’ for L = 1.00 m is a calculation?

Elective · Anatomy: the foot — tarsus, metatarsals, and phalanges

30 min · Anatomy · the foot, parallel to last week’s hand; individual tarsal names reserved

Learn

Key terms

tarsus
Definition
The tarsus is the group of bones in the posterior foot, the counterpart of the carpus in the hand. In this lesson it is taught as a group. The seven individual tarsal names are reserved, as the eight carpal names were reserved last week.
Example
The tarsus sits at the back of the foot, toward the ankle; the metatarsals run forward from it toward the toes.
Not this
Not a first look at the carpus, metacarpals, or hand phalanges, which were taught last week. Not a claim that the tarsus is a single bone. The individual names (for example the calcaneus as a later name) are reserved, not invented unofficially today.
Connects to
Last week’s hand: carpus as a group, metacarpals, phalanges (thumb two, fingers three), palm-forward. Today the foot, in the same grouping, from the same kind of school anatomy: tarsus as a group, metatarsals, phalanges (great toe two, other toes three).
metatarsal
Definition
A metatarsal is one of the long bones of the midfoot, between the tarsus and the phalanges of the toes, the counterpart of a metacarpal in the hand.
Example
Five metatarsals run toward the five toes, as five metacarpals run toward the five digits of the hand. This lesson does not invent an official length for any one of them.
Not this
Not a phalanx, and not a tarsal bone. Not a metacarpal renamed without the foot’s own name.
Connects to
Last week’s metacarpals sit in the palm. Today’s metatarsals sit in the midfoot. The grouping is parallel; the names are not interchangeable.

Last week the hand was taught as carpus (a group; the eight individual names reserved), metacarpals, and phalanges (two in the thumb, three in each of the other fingers), palm-forward. Those are not first looks today.

Today the foot, in the same grouping:

Word How to say it Meaning
tarsus TAR-sus the group of bones in the posterior foot, as a group
metatarsal met-ah-TAR-sul one of the long bones of the midfoot, between tarsus and phalanges
hallux HAL-uks the great toe
  • The tarsus is the posterior group, toward the ankle, the counterpart of the carpus. The seven individual names are reserved, as the eight carpal names were reserved last week.
  • Five metatarsals run forward from the tarsus toward the toes, the counterpart of the metacarpals.
  • The phalanges of the toes follow the same count already used in the hand: two in the hallux (the great toe), three in each of the other toes.

A sole-down, toes-forward view is enough; this lesson does not invent official lengths or a measured arch. No living, named person’s foot is required or described. The site holds no identifying personal information.

The reserved seven names will be taught later, from a named anatomy source, not guessed today. The grouping (tarsus as a group of seven, five metatarsals, fourteen phalanges in all) follows standard human anatomy as used last week for the hand; it is not a new official measurement of a particular foot.

Remember

The foot is taught as tarsus (a group; seven individual names reserved), five metatarsals, and phalanges (two in the hallux, three in each of the other toes). It parallels last week’s hand. No official lengths are invented, and no identifying person is described.

Remember

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the area of a parallelogram, and why is “base times a slanted side” a miss?
  2. The area is A = b × h, where h is the perpendicular height to the chosen base. A parallelogram can be rearranged into a rectangle with that same base and height.
  3. A slanted side is not h, because it does not meet the base at a right angle. A parallelogram of base 8 and height 5 has area 40, not 8 times a longer slanted side.
  4. A near-miss is to treat this as a first look at the shape itself (the parallelogram was named on Day 14). The new work this week is the area.

Together

Hint 1: The support

Circular reasoning supports a claim with a restatement of the claim, or with a premise that already assumes the conclusion.

Hint 2: True or false

A claim can be true and still be supported in a circle. A fallacy is a flaw in the support, as last week already taught.
