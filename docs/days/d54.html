Mathematics · Geometry XXIV: a short mix — a tangent-secant, and an inequality

40 min · Method: keep the pictures separate; III.36 for a tangent and a secant; the graph for a quadratic inequality

Learn

Key terms

a short mix of this week’s algebra and geometry
Definition
A sitting that uses Monday’s and Wednesday’s quadratic inequalities and Tuesday’s tangent-secant theorem, each in its own picture, without mixing the theorems into one invented hybrid.
Example
A tangent-secant product, then a quadratic inequality read from a graph, labeled as two separate tasks.
Not this
Not a new theorem, and do not use PT² = PA × PB on a quadratic’s roots.
Connects to
Days 51–53 are the week’s new mathematics. Tomorrow’s Friday mix stays in the same range.

This sitting does not add a new theorem. It uses Tuesday’s tangent-secant theorem and this week’s quadratic inequalities, as two separate jobs.

Job A, a tangent and a secant. From P, PT = 5 and PA = 4. Then PT² = PA × PB, so 25 = 4 × PB, and PB = 25/4 = 6.25. A is still nearer than B, because 4 < 6.25.

Job B, an inequality. (x − 4)(x − 10) < 0, opening upward, so 4 < x < 10. If the same product is written with a minus, −(x − 4)(x − 10) < 0 is the downward-opening companion, true outside the roots: x < 4 or x > 10.

The habit. Name the picture before you compute. A product of lengths on a tangent and a secant is III.36. An interval on a number line from a parabola is an inequality. We will not write PT² = (x − 4)(x − 10) as if the two jobs were one theorem.

Remember

Keep the pictures separate. III.36: PT² = PA × PB. A quadratic inequality is read from the graph. Do not merge the two into a hybrid theorem.

Key terms: a short mix of this week’s algebra and geometry.

Learn

Key terms

a few accents, and a stop
Definition
A late sitting on the same two-form group: a few darker or lighter accents where the study really needs them, then a stop, so tomorrow’s critique has a finished-enough page rather than an overworked one.
Example
One darker contact where a form meets its shadow, or one lighter accent on a light plane, then a stop. Not a new form, and not a head.
Not this
Not a second lamp, not a full “finish” that polishes away the planes, and not a portrait.
Connects to
Week 10 used a few accents and a stop on the cube. Today the same habit covers both forms.

The group is already set, checked, and shadowed. Today you may add a few accents, then stop.

An accent is a small, deliberate darker or lighter note: a slightly darker contact where a form meets its cast shadow, or a slightly cleaner light on a light plane. Two or three such notes are enough.

A stop is a decision. Tomorrow’s critique needs a page that still shows planes, a turn, and agreeing shadows. Polishing until the cube is a soft ball, or inventing a second lamp, is not a finish this course is asking for.

Still not a head. No features on the sphere.

A few accents, then a stop. Keep the planes, the turn, and the one-lamp shadows. Still not a head.

Modern language · Spanish: porque and por eso (present)

40 min · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Key terms

porque and por eso
Definition
Two present-tense reason words: porque (because) introduces a reason; por eso (that is why) introduces a consequence. Instruction stays in English. Do not retell the history letter in Spanish.
Example
Lee porque quiere leer. Está aquí; por eso lee.
Not this
Not por and para (already contrasted in week 6), and not a past-tense reason lesson.
Connects to
cūr / quia were reviewed in Latin this week; today’s Spanish pair is taught in English, as Spanish, not as a translation drill of the Latin.
Word How to say it Meaning
porque pohr-KEH because
por eso pohr EH-soh that is why / therefore (a present consequence)
Lee porque escribe leh-EH pohr-KEH es-KREE-beh He/she reads because he/she writes (a model of porque, not a claim about a person)
Escribe; por eso lee es-KREE-beh pohr EH-soh leh-EH He/she writes; that is why he/she reads

porque introduces a reason (because). por eso introduces a consequence (that is why). Instruction stays in English. The history letter and the mathematics stay in English.

Original sentences: Lee porque quiere leer. Está aquí; por eso lee. No está aquí; por eso no lee. Escribe porque lee.

For the guide: keep the reasons in the present, in ordinary classroom pictures (reading, being here). Do not retell 79 CE in Spanish.

Remember

porque: because. por eso: that is why. Present only. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No history letter in the Spanish.

Economics · Unit price, in an invented shop

30 min · Method: unit price = total ÷ number of units, in a labeled invented shop; not a profit and not a budget

Learn

Key terms

unit price
Definition
The price of one unit, in an invented shop example: total price divided by the number of units (when the units are the same kind). It is not a profit, and not a budget. Invented numbers in this lesson are labeled as invented.
Example
If a labeled invented pack of 4 identical notebooks costs $6, the unit price is $6 ÷ 4 = $1.50 per notebook.
Not this
Not a profit (already taught as a leftover after costs in an invented shop), and not a claim about a real shop’s ticket. Don't invent a NASA figure or a historical price.
Connects to
Week 7 taught a budget; week 10 taught profit as a leftover in an invented shop. Today is a different job: the price of one unit, still in a labeled invented shop.

A budget (week 7) and a profit as a leftover after costs (week 10) have already been taught, in invented shop or household examples. Today is a different job: a unit price.

The method. When the units are of the same kind, the unit price is the total price divided by the number of units. All shop numbers in this lesson are labeled invented classroom numbers, not a real ticket and not a historical price.

A worked invented example. A pack of 4 identical notebooks is marked $6 (invented). The unit price is $6 ÷ 4 = $1.50 per notebook. A pack of 3 identical notebooks marked $6 (invented) would be $2 per notebook. The totals are the same; the unit prices are not.

A comparison, still invented. If one pack is 4 for $6 and another is 5 for $8, the unit prices are $1.50 and $1.60. The second pack has a higher total, and also a higher unit price, in this invented pair. We will not invent a “better buy” law that ignores whether the units are really the same.

Unit price is not profit. Profit, already taught, is what is left after costs. A unit price can be known even when a profit is not being asked for.

Remember

Unit price = total price ÷ number of same-kind units, in a labeled invented shop. Not a profit (already taught) and not a budget. Invented numbers stay labeled. No real ticket and no historical price.

Key terms: unit price.
