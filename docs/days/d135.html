Remember

Friday mix, not a new exercise. Recall a chreia as a short, useful anecdote that attributes a saying, a deed, or both to a named person; last week’s narrative, with who, what, when, and where, in order, and no stated moral; and the fable, with a stated moral. The set Diogenes example, from Clough’s Plutarch, remains the model chreia. A maxim, and a full later elaboration, remain reserved.

Remember

A chreia attributes a saying or deed to a named person on a brief occasion. A narrative tells an event in order, without a required saying. A fable has a stated moral.

Watch me

  1. A fable, a narrative, and a chreia.
  2. “I would have you stand from between me and the sun.”
  3. Do not invent official facts beyond the set passage, and do not name a living person in an original chreia.

Together

Hint 1: A narrative

If the point is the event in order — who, what, when, and where — and there is no attributed saying, it is a narrative.

Hint 2: A chreia

If the point is a named person’s saying or deed on a brief occasion, it is a chreia.

Check

Remember

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: Gray 1918, the axis, a first look

40 min · Method: Gray’s axis as the pivot for the atlas, with the odontoid process; C7 reserved; official lengths reserved

Learn

Key terms

the axis (second cervical vertebra)
Definition
In Gray, 1918, as posted at Theodora, the second cervical vertebra “is named the epistropheus or axis because it forms the pivot upon which the first vertebra, carrying the head, rotates.” “The most distinctive characteristic of this bone is the strong odontoid process which rises perpendicularly from the upper surface of the body.” Official lengths remain reserved. The seventh cervical vertebra remains reserved.
Example
Gray also notes a facet on the front of the dens “for articulation with that on the anterior arch of the atlas,” already taught, and a groove for the transverse atlantal ligament. The lesson uses those marks, not an official measured length of the dens.
Not this
Not last week’s atlas, which has no body and no spinous process. Not the seventh cervical vertebra, the vertebra prominens, which remains reserved. Not an official measured length invented for the dens.
Connects to
Last week’s atlas, and the earlier foramen transversarium. The axis is the second of the three Gray set apart.

Last week’s anatomy lesson, from Gray, 1918, “The Cervical Vertebrae,” as posted at Theodora, treated the atlas, the first cervical vertebra, ring-like, with no body and no spinous process, and reserved the axis and the seventh. Today the second: the axis. Gray: “The second cervical vertebra is named the epistropheus or axis because it forms the pivot upon which the first vertebra, carrying the head, rotates.” “The most distinctive characteristic of this bone is the strong odontoid process which rises perpendicularly from the upper surface of the body.” The dens, or odontoid process, “exhibits a slight constriction or neck, where it joins the body.” “On its anterior surface is an oval or nearly circular facet for articulation with that on the anterior arch of the atlas.” “On the back of the neck, and frequently extending on to its lateral surfaces, is a shallow groove for the transverse atlantal ligament which retains the process in position.” Invent no official length of the dens, and no official diameter of a foramen. The seventh cervical vertebra remains reserved. This is artistic anatomy from a public-domain text, not a medical diagnosis, and no body is being described from life.

Remember

Gray names the second cervical vertebra the axis because it forms the pivot upon which the atlas, carrying the head, rotates. Its distinctive mark is the odontoid process, or dens, rising from the body. Official lengths remain reserved.
