Key terms (from earlier lessons)

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Mathematics · Geometry XLVII: the volume of a right cone

45 min · Method: a right cone is to a right cylinder as a pyramid is to a prism: V = (1/3)πr²h

Learn

Key terms

volume of a right cone
Definition
A right cone has a circular base and its apex directly above the center of that base. Its volume is V = (1/3) × B × h, with B the already-taught area of a circle, πr². So V = (1/3)πr²h, in cubic units. Exact answers stay in terms of π.
Example
A classroom cone of radius 3 and height 9 has V = (1/3)π × 9 × 9 = 27π cubic units. A cylinder of the same radius and height would have volume 81π, three times as much.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s cylinder V = πr²h, and not a first look at yesterday’s 1/3. Not a sphere, and not a slanted cone. Not last week’s circle area taken as a volume.
Connects to
The one-third is the same relation already seen for a pyramid and a prism. The base is now a circle.

Yesterday a pyramid was one-third of a prism with the same base and height. A right cone stands in the same relation to a right cylinder:

V = (1/3)πr²h.

For the classroom cone in the diagram, r = 3 and h = 9, so V = (1/3) × π × 9 × 9 = 27π cubic units. A cylinder with the same r and h, from last week, would be 81π. Leave exact answers in terms of π. The height is perpendicular to the base; a slanted length along the side is not h. Classroom lengths are invented.

Remember

A right cone has V = (1/3)πr²h, one-third of last week’s cylinder with the same r and h. Exact answers stay in terms of π. This is not a first look at πr² or at V = πr²h.

Key terms: volume of a right cone.

Science · The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, from the Harvey/NIST table

40 min · Method: ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g, from the same Harvey/NIST table already used for fusion and density, with invented masses labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

enthalpy of sublimation of ice
Definition
The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, written ΔsublH in the Harvey/NIST table, is the energy needed to turn ice directly into vapor at the transition, per gram, at the stated temperature. At 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the table gives ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g.
Example
An invented 10 g of ice, already at 0 °C, would need 10 × 2834 = 2.834 × 10⁴ J, or 28.34 kJ, to sublime at 0 °C. The mass is invented; the 2834 J/g is Harvey’s.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g, and not a first look at melting or at density. Not an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2834 − 333.4 is a 100 °C boiling figure.
Connects to
Harvey’s heading is “Phase Transition Properties,” and the two figures ΔfusH and ΔsublH stand side by side at 0 °C. Sublimation here is ice to vapor, not ice to liquid.

Last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g is the energy to melt one gram of ice at 0 °C. The same Harvey/NIST table, under the same phase-transition heading, also gives

ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g

at 101.325 kPa. That is the energy needed to turn one gram of ice directly into vapor at 0 °C. It is not the melting figure, and it is not a boiling figure at 100 °C.

For an invented 10 g of ice already at 0 °C, the energy to sublime it at 0 °C is 10 × 2834 = 28,340 J, or 28.34 kJ. The mass is invented; the 2834 J/g is Harvey’s.

2834 / 333.4 ≈ 8.50, so, at 0 °C in this table, subliming 1 g takes about eight and a half times the energy of melting 1 g. That comparison uses two 0 °C figures from the same table. It is not a license to treat 2834 − 333.4 = 2500.6 J/g as an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C. That 100 °C figure was not opened as a definite fetched table row for this week, and it is not invented.

Numerical figures are from A. H. Harvey, “Properties of Ice and Supercooled Water,” a NIST update to a CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics table, at standard atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water (PDF: https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=926353). ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g. Last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g and ice cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 0 °C remain available as contrasts, not as new first looks. The difference 2834 − 333.4 is not to be treated as an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, which was not taken from a fetched table row for this week and is not invented.

Remember

Definition

Elective · Music: a sequence, a first look

40 min · Method: a musical sequence as a pattern repeated at a different pitch, using already-taught phrases and cadences only as contrasts, not as first looks

Learn

Key terms

musical sequence
Definition
A musical sequence, in this lesson’s school sense, is a short figure or phrase that is then repeated at a different pitch, usually starting a step or more higher or lower, so that the shape is heard again, moved. Classroom examples in this lesson are invented school figures, labeled invented, not quotations from a copyrighted score.
Example
An invented rising three-note figure that is then sung again starting one step higher is a rising sequence. The same figure starting one step lower is a falling sequence.
Not this
Not a first look at a period, or at last week’s contrasting and parallel openings, though a consequent may happen to sequence a figure from the antecedent. Not a first look at a cadence. Not every later textbook’s extra names for kinds of sequence, which are reserved.
Connects to
Last week named whether a period’s openings match. Today names a different job: a figure heard again, moved in pitch.

Last week’s period was antecedent and consequent, named parallel or contrasting by their openings. Today is a different tool, a sequence: a short figure repeated at a different pitch.

The shape stays recognizable; the starting pitch changes, usually a step higher (a rising sequence) or a step lower (a falling sequence). Classroom sung or tapped examples in this lesson are invented school figures, labeled invented, not quotations from a copyrighted score.

A sequence is not itself a cadence, and it is not the same job as naming a period parallel or contrasting, though a phrase inside a period might contain a sequence. Further textbook types remain reserved.

Remember

A sequence repeats a short figure at a different pitch, rising or falling. Classroom figures are invented and labeled invented. This is not a first look at a period or at a cadence. Further types are reserved.

Key terms: musical sequence.

  2. Invented: the same figure starting one step lower. That is a falling sequence.
  3. A parallel period repeats an opening at the same stage of a phrase, as last week taught; a sequence moves the figure to a new pitch. They are not the same name.
