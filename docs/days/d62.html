30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. XI · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Key terms

third-declension neuter (more nouns)
Definition
The same neuter rule applied to more body nouns of the third family: pectus, pectoris (chest); ōs, ōris (mouth or face, with a long ō); crūs, crūris (leg). Nominative equals accusative in the singular; those cases end in -a in the plural. Do not confuse ōs, ōris with os, ossis, “bone,” which is a different word.
Example
Puer pectus habet. Puella ōs videt. Crūs longum est.
Not this
Not os, ossis “bone,” and not a masculine accusative in -em. ōs (mouth) has a long ō; keep the two words apart.
Connects to
Yesterday’s rule for corpus and caput is used again, with three more stems: pector-, ōr-, crūr-.
Word How to say it Meaning
pectus, pectoris n. PEK-toos, PEK-to-ris chest; of the chest
ōs, ōris n. OHS, OH-ris mouth or face; of the mouth (long ō)
crūs, crūris n. KROOS, KROO-ris leg; of the leg
cor, cordis n. kor, KOR-dis heart; of the heart

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The same neuter rule covers more of the body’s nouns. Pectus, pectoris; ōs, ōris (mouth or face, with a long ō); crūs, crūris; and cor, cordis all belong to the third family, and all are neuter. The stem is still in the genitive. The nominative and the accusative stay the same in the singular, and they end in -a in the plural: pectora, ōra, crūra, corda.

A near-miss that is worth naming once: os, ossis means “bone.” That is a different word from ōs, ōris “mouth” or “face.” Today’s sentences use only the long-ō word.

Original sentences: Pectus hominis magnum est. Puella ōs videt. Crūs longum est. Puer cor habet.

Remember

Pectus, ōs (long ō, mouth or face), crūs, and cor are third-declension neuters. Nominative equals accusative in the singular; those cases end in -a in the plural. ōs, ōris is not os, ossis (“bone”).

Mathematics · Geometry XXVI: AA similarity

40 min · Method: the angle sum, then two equal angles as a test for similarity

Learn

Key terms

AA similarity
Definition
If two angles of one triangle are equal to two angles of another triangle, the third angles are equal as well (because the angle sum is 180°), and the triangles are similar. The name AA records that two pairs of equal angles are enough.
Example
If ∠P = ∠S = 50° and ∠Q = ∠T = 70°, then ∠R = ∠U = 60°, and △PQR ∼ △STU.
Not this
Not a claim that two equal sides, without equal angles, already give similarity. Not SAS or SSS congruence from Term 1, which decide same size as well as same shape. AA is about similarity, not congruence, unless you have more information.
Connects to
Yesterday’s definition of similar triangles, and Term 1’s angle sum of a triangle, together give this test. You do not need to measure the sides first.

Once two pairs of angles match, the third pair is forced. Each triangle’s angles add to 180°, so the remaining angle is 180° minus the two you already know. If those two are the same in both triangles, the third is the same as well. All three corresponding angles are equal, and the triangles are similar.

That test is called AA similarity (angle-angle). Two equal angles are enough; you do not first have to show the third pair, and you do not first have to measure the sides. The sides will then be proportional, by the definition of similarity.

AA does not, by itself, make the triangles congruent. They may still differ in size.

Remember

AA similarity: if two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, the third angles are equal and the triangles are similar. The angle sum (180°) is what forces the third pair. AA does not, by itself, give congruence.

Key terms: AA similarity.

  3. The sides need not be equal; AA gives similarity, not congruence.

Yesterday’s three parts are now used on a short, named public-domain text, not on an invented window or lamp. The extract is the opening of Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address, in the Bliss copy. Later of the address remains reserved; today uses only these two sentences, quoted exactly.

Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth, on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. Now we are engaged in a great civil war, testing whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure.

Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address, Bliss copy, as posted at Wikisource. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gettysburg_Address_(Bliss_copy) Later of the address remains reserved.

A careful Toulmin reading of the second sentence might look like this. Claim: the war is a test of whether that nation — or any nation so conceived and so dedicated — can long endure. Grounds: “we are engaged in a great civil war.” Warrant: a civil war of this kind tests whether a nation conceived in Liberty and dedicated to that proposition can endure.

The first sentence supplies the description “so conceived and so dedicated”: a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. That wording belongs to Lincoln; do not tidy it into a paraphrase and then treat the paraphrase as a quotation.

This is argument analysis, not a history lesson on the war, and not a request to agree or disagree with the address as a whole.

Science · Water: melting and boiling at one atmosphere

50 min · Method: the familiar 0 °C and 100 °C, set beside sourced modern figures

Learn

Key terms

melting point (at 1 atm)
Definition
The temperature at which a solid and its liquid are in equilibrium at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. For water, the Celsius scale was originally defined so that this point, at one atmosphere, was 0 °C. That historical definition is no longer how the scale is fixed, but “about 0 °C” remains a good ordinary description of water’s ice point at 1 atm.
Example
Ice and liquid water together, at one atmosphere, sit at about 0 °C in ordinary laboratory language. This course uses that familiar figure as an approximation, beside the sourced modern scale described in the lesson.
Not this
Not the triple point, which is a different condition (solid, liquid, and vapor together at a unique pressure and temperature). Not a claim that every substance melts at 0 °C. Evaporation, already taught, is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and is not the same as melting.
Connects to
Evaporation was taught in the oil-paint module. Today begins the water module with the familiar ice point and boiling point, and with the sourced modern figures for those points.
boiling point (at 1 atm)
Definition
The temperature at which a liquid boils at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. The Celsius scale was originally defined so that this point for pure water, at one atmosphere, was 100 °C. On the International Temperature Scale of 1990, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 99.974 °C (373.124 K). “About 100 °C” remains a good ordinary approximation.
Example
In ordinary laboratory language, water at one atmosphere boils at about 100 °C. The sourced ITS-90 figure, about 99.974 °C, is the more precise modern value taught beside that approximation.
Not this
Not melting, and not the triple point. Not a claim that the modern measured boiling point is defined to be exactly 100 °C. The historical 100 and the ITS-90 figure must not be blended into a fake official exact 100.
Connects to
The IAPWS FAQ is the source for both the historical definition and the ITS-90 atmospheric boiling temperature. Familiar “about 100 °C” and the sourced 99.974 °C are kept side by side.

Water is found as a solid (ice), a liquid, and a gas (water vapor). Melting is the change from solid to liquid; boiling is the change from liquid to gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point. Evaporation, already taught, is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and it can happen below the boiling point. That earlier idea is not retaught as new; it is only kept distinct from boiling.

Ordinary laboratory language still says that, at one atmosphere, ice melts at about 0 °C and water boils at about 100 °C. Those figures have a history. According to the IAPWS FAQ, the Celsius scale was originally defined so that the freezing point and the boiling point of pure water, both at one atmosphere, were 0 and 100 degrees. That definition ceased to be valid when the International Temperature Scale of 1990 (ITS-90) was adopted.

On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 99.974 °C (373.124 K). The familiar “about 100 °C” is still a good ordinary approximation. This course does not blend those two figures into a fake official exact 100. The International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS), Frequently Asked Questions, FAQ 1 (https://iapws.org/faqs/faq1.html). The Celsius scale was originally defined so that the freezing point and boiling point of pure water, both at one atmosphere, were 0 and 100 degrees. That definition ceased to be valid with the International Temperature Scale of 1990 (ITS-90). On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 373.124 K (99.974 °C). The triple point of water — the unique pressure and temperature where solid, liquid, and vapor coexist — is exactly 273.16 K on that scale, which is 0.01 °C.

Remember

At one atmosphere, water’s ice point is about 0 °C and its boiling point is about 100 °C in ordinary language. Historically, Celsius used those exact numbers as the definition. On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature is approximately 99.974 °C. Evaporation, already taught, is not the same as boiling. The source is the IAPWS FAQ.

Key terms

Inferno
Definition
The first of the three canticles of Dante’s Divine Comedy, the journey through Hell. In this course the opening is read in Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s 1867 public-domain translation. Later cantos remain reserved.

From Italian inferno, “hell,” from Latin infernus, “of the lower regions,” and inferna, “the lower regions” (English Wiktionary, entry “inferno”).

Not this

Connects to

canto
Definition

Connects to

Term 2 of literature opens Dante’s Inferno, the first canticle of the Divine Comedy, in a public-domain English translation: Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867. Midway upon the journey of our life
I found myself within a forest dark,

But of the good to treat, which there I found,
Speak will I of the other things I saw there.

Hablamos still looks the same in both tenses. In a sentence without a time word, the other verbs, or a clear context, have to do the work. Tomorrow adds time words such as ayer.

Original practice, not a history retelling in Spanish: Yo canté. Tú compraste pan. Ella habló. Nosotros cantamos. Ustedes compraron.

Remember

hablo / hablas / habla is present; hablé / hablaste / habló is preterite. Regular -ar verbs share those past endings. hablamos can be either tense. cantó and habló keep their written accents. Instruction remains in English.

Music theory · Triad inversion: the third or the fifth in the bass

30 min · Method: the already-taught triad, now named by which tone is in the bass

Learn

Key terms

triad inversion
Definition
A change of which triad tone is in the bass: root position has the root in the bass; first inversion has the third in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The three tones of the triad are the same; only the lowest note changes.
Example
A C-major triad is C–E–G in root position, E–G–C in first inversion, and G–C–E in second inversion. The bass is C, then E, then G.
Not this
Not interval inversion, already taught on Day 37, which turns a single interval by moving one of its two notes by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth, and so on). Triad inversion is about which chord tone is in the bass, not about inverting one interval.
Connects to
Days 17 and 42 taught the triad and the major and minor triad. Day 37 taught interval inversion, with the octave as the frame. Today’s inversion is of a whole triad, and those two uses of the word must be kept apart.

You already know a triad: three tones, a root, a third, and a fifth (a major or a minor triad, as Day 42 taught). You also already know interval inversion: one interval, turned so that the old top note is below, or the old bottom note is above, inside an octave. Today’s word is different.

Triad inversion asks which of the three triad tones is in the bass — the lowest note. In root position, the root is in the bass (C–E–G for a C-major triad). In first inversion, the third is in the bass (E–G–C). In second inversion, the fifth is in the bass (G–C–E). The three tones remain the same; they are only stacked in a new order above a new bass.

Do not name this as interval inversion. A fifth inverting to a fourth is a two-note job. A triad inversion is a three-note job, named from the bass.

Remember

A triad is in root position when the root is in the bass, in first inversion when the third is in the bass, and in second inversion when the fifth is in the bass. The three tones stay the same. This is not interval inversion (Day 37).

Key terms: triad inversion.

Watch me

Together

A G-major triad is G–B–D. What is its first inversion, and how is that different from inverting the interval G–D?

Hint 1: The third in the bass

The third of G major is B, so first inversion has B in the bass: B–D–G.

Hint 2: Interval inversion

G–D is a fifth; its interval inversion is a fourth, D–G. That is a two-note inversion, not a triad inversion.
