Mathematics · Geometry LXII: sine and cosine, mixed

45 min · Method: a mixed practice of sine, cosine, and the complementary relation, in invented right triangles. Degrees only

Learn

Key terms

sine and cosine, mixed
Definition
A short mixed practice of this week’s methods: sin A is opposite over hypotenuse; cos A is adjacent over hypotenuse; and sin(90° − A) = cos A, because the two acute angles of a right triangle add to 90°. Classroom lengths remain invented. Degrees only. The radian and the unit circle remain reserved. No new ratio is begun.
Example
In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, A opposite 3: sin A = 3/5, cos A = 4/5, sin(90° − A) = 4/5. In a classroom 5-12-13 triangle, A opposite 5: sin A = 5/13, cos A = 12/13.
Not this

Modern language · Spanish: iba, era, and veía, a three-verb contrast

40 min · Method: iba, era, and veía, side by side, with the already-taught preterites fui and vi as contrasts

Learn

Key terms

iba, era, and veía, contrasted
Definition
Three irregular imperfects, already taught this week, are now set side by side so they are not merged: iba (ir), era (ser), and veía (ver). Ir and ser share the already-taught preterite fui, but not the imperfect. Ver has the already-taught preterite vi. In this first look, the imperfect is still for ongoing or habitual past action, or for a past state with era; the preterite is for a completed event. Instruction remains in English. Classroom sentences are invented and do not identify a living person.
Example
Cuando yo iba, yo veía. Ella era estudiante, an invented sentence. Ayer yo fui, y yo vi.
Not this
Not a first look at any of the three imperfects, or at fui and vi, already taught. Last week’s hablaba and comía may appear as already-taught regular contrasts, not as first looks.
Connects to

Remember

Iba (ir), era (ser), veía (ver). Ir and ser share fui, not the imperfect. In this first look, the imperfect is ongoing, habitual, or, with era, a past state; the preterite is a completed event. Instruction remains in English.

Seminar · Economics: successive discounts, a first look

40 min · Method: two percent-off discounts in a row, on invented figures. Last week’s single discount is a contrast, not a first look. Ten percent then ten percent is not twenty percent off the original

Learn

Key terms

successive discounts
Definition
A successive discount, in this first look, takes one percent off an invented price, then takes a second percent off the amount that remains, not off the original price again. If 10 percent is taken off an invented $100, the first amount paid is $90. A second 10 percent off that $90 is $9, and the final amount paid is $81, not $80. Two discounts of 10 percent are not one discount of 20 percent off the original. Last week’s single discount, 25 percent off an invented $80, remains a different job, already taught. The figures are invented, not a real store sale. Tax and compound interest remain reserved.
Example
A second invented case: 25 percent off $80 is $60, already taught as a single discount; then a further 10 percent off that $60 is $6, and the final amount paid is $54, not 35 percent of 80, which would be a different single discount.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s single percent-off discount, or at percent itself, already taught. Not a real store’s price, and not a first look at tax or at compound interest.
