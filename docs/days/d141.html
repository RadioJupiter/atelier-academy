Latin · The perfect of sequor, a first look

40 min · Method: secūtus sum, the perfect participle plus the present of sum; original sentences, not Ørberg

Learn

Key terms

perfect of sequor
Definition
The perfect of sequor uses the already-taught deponent perfect pattern: the perfect participle plus the present of sum. The participle is secūtus. The six forms are secūtus sum, secūtus es, secūtus est, secūtī sumus, secūtī estis, secūtī sunt: I followed, or I have followed, and so on. It is not the present sequor, not the future sequar, and not the pluperfect secūtus eram.
Example
Puer mē secūtus est: he followed me, or he has followed me. The following is completed in the past, but is not marked as already completed before another past moment.
Not this
Not sequor, I follow. Not sequar, I shall follow. Not secūtus eram, I had followed. Not locūtus sum, I spoke, the perfect of loquor.
Connects to
Last week’s future sequar, the already-taught present and pluperfect, and the already-taught perfect of loquor, locūtus sum.

The present of sequor, the future sequar, and the pluperfect secūtus eram have already been taught. Today the perfect of the same verb, using a pattern already known from locūtus sum: the perfect participle plus the present of sum. Mathematics · Geometry LXXXIII: the radian, a first look

40 min · Method: a radian is the central angle whose arc equals the radius; 2π radians = 360°; exact values in π

Learn

Key terms

the radian
Definition
A radian is a measure of a central angle: the angle for which the arc it cuts off has the same length as the radius. A full turn is 2π radians, which is the same turn as 360°, so π radians = 180°, π/2 radians = 90°, π/3 = 60°, π/4 = 45°, and π/6 = 30°. It is not a degree, and it is not an official measured length of a real wheel.
Example
The central angle that cuts off an arc equal to the radius is 1 radian. Because the whole circumference is 2π r, the whole central angle is 2π radians. These are exact relations, not invented official measurements.
Not this
Not a degree. Not an approximate stand-in that replaces π with 3.14 in exact work. Not a claim that a classroom wheel has been officially measured in radians. Not the ambiguous case, which remains reserved.
Connects to
The already-taught unit circle in degrees, related angles, and arc length in degrees. The conversion between the two measures is part of today’s job.

Until now angles have been measured in degrees. A second measure, used especially when an arc is compared with its radius, is the radian. A radian is the central angle that cuts off an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle.

The whole circumference is 2π r, so the whole central angle is 2π r / r = 2π radians, the same full turn as 360°. Therefore π radians = 180°, π/2 radians = 90°, π/3 = 60°, π/4 = 45°, and π/6 = 30°. To convert, θ in degrees equals θ in radians times 180/π, and θ in radians equals θ in degrees times π/180. These are exact relations. Do not replace π with 3.14 in exact work, and do not invent an official measured radian angle on a real wheel. The ambiguous case remains reserved.

Remember

A radian is the central angle whose arc equals the radius. 2π radians = 360°, so π radians = 180°. Convert by multiplying by 180/π or by π/180. Keep exact values in π.

Definition
A refutation in this course examines a claim and gives reasons why the claim should not be accepted. It is the next progymnasmata exercise after last week’s maxim. It is not a maxim, not a chreia, and not a narrative.
Example

Physics · The Doppler effect, a first look, from OpenStax 17.4

40 min · Method: OpenStax 17.4; f_obs for a moving source and for a moving observer; official Example 17.4 waits

Learn

Key terms

the Doppler effect
Definition
The Doppler effect, in OpenStax 17.4, is an alteration in the observed frequency of a sound due to motion of either the source or the observer. The actual change in frequency due to that relative motion is called a Doppler shift. Relative motion of source and observer toward one another increases the received frequency; relative motion apart decreases it.
Example
For a stationary observer and a moving source, f_obs = f_s × v_w / (v_w ± v_s), with the minus sign for motion toward the observer and the plus sign for motion away. For a stationary source and a moving observer, f_obs = f_s × (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w, with the plus sign for motion toward the source and the minus sign for motion away. Official Example 17.4 waits until Wednesday.
Not this
Not a change in the speed of sound in the medium, which remains v_w whether the source is moving or not. Not last week’s decibel, which is a level of intensity, not a shift of frequency. Not a sonic boom, which waits. Not an official measured frequency of a real train not in the book’s example.
Connects to
Last week’s sound, speed of sound, and frequency. The effect is named for Christian Johann Doppler (1803–1853), as OpenStax 17.4 states.

OpenStax 17.4, “Doppler Effect and Sonic Booms,” at https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms, defines the Doppler effect as an alteration in the observed frequency of a sound due to motion of either the source or the observer. The actual change in frequency due to that relative motion is called a Doppler shift. The sound still moves in the medium at the already-taught speed v_w, whether the source is moving or not; v_w = fλ remains. If the source moves, compressions are closer together ahead of the source and farther apart behind it, so a stationary observer ahead hears a higher frequency, and a stationary observer behind hears a lower one. If the observer moves toward a stationary source, the observer passes through more crests in the same time and hears a higher frequency; motion away lowers the frequency. In general, relative motion toward one another increases the received frequency, and relative motion apart decreases it.

For a stationary observer and a moving source, f_obs = f_s × v_w / (v_w ± v_s), where f_s is the source frequency, v_s is the speed of the source along the line joining source and observer, and v_w is the speed of sound. The minus sign is used for motion toward the observer, and the plus sign for motion away. For a stationary source and a moving observer, f_obs = f_s × (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w, where v_obs is the speed of the observer along that line; the plus sign is for motion toward the source, and the minus sign for motion away. Official Example 17.4, a 150 Hz train horn, waits until Wednesday. Sonic booms wait until Friday. Do not invent an official frequency of a real vehicle not in the book.

Remember

The Doppler effect is a change in observed frequency due to motion of the source or the observer. Motion toward raises the frequency; motion apart lowers it. The two official formulas distinguish a moving source from a moving observer.

  2. The plus sign is used when the source moves away, and f_obs is lower than f_s.
  3. v_w itself is not changed by the motion of the source.

Learn

Key terms

present subjunctive of ser
Definition
The present subjunctive of ser is irregular: sea, seas, sea, seamos, seáis, sean. It is not the present indicative soy or es, not the future será, not the conditional sería, and not the regular -ar subjunctive hable.
Example
In an invented sentence, “Quiere que ella sea puntual.” Sea is the present subjunctive of ser, “that she be,” not the indicative es. The sentence is invented classroom Spanish, not an official quotation.
Not this
Not es, the present indicative. Not será, the future. Not sería, the conditional. Not sea confused with vaya, the present subjunctive of ir, which waits until tomorrow. Not hable, a regular -ar subjunctive.
Connects to

Es is present indicative, he or she is. Será is future, he or she will be. Sería is conditional, he or she would be.

Hint 2: A regular verb

Hable is the regular present subjunctive of hablar, not a form of ser.

Seminar · Logic: the addition rule for overlapping events, a first look

40 min · Method: invented fair cube; overlap subtracted; distinguished from mutual exclusivity and from independence

Learn

Key terms

the addition rule for overlapping events
Definition
When two events can both happen, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The last term is subtracted so the overlap is not counted twice. This is not last week’s product rule for independent events, and it is not the earlier addition rule that was used only when events were mutually exclusive.
Example
On an invented fair cube, P(even or greater than 3) = P(even) + P(greater than 3) − P(even and greater than 3) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 2/3. The even faces are 2, 4, and 6; the faces greater than 3 are 4, 5, and 6; the overlap is 4 and 6. The cube is an invented classroom figure, not an official experiment.
Not this
Not P(A) + P(B) with the overlap left in, which would give 1 on this invented cube, a miss already noted in an earlier week. Not P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) used in place of today’s subtraction, unless independence has been separately established and the question is “and.” Not an official probability of a real event.
Connects to
Last week’s independent events, and the earlier mutually exclusive addition rule. Official probabilities of real events remain reserved.

When two events were mutually exclusive, they could not both happen, and P(A or B) was P(A) + P(B). Last week, independent events could both happen, and P(A and B) was P(A) × P(B). Today the “or” rule is completed for events that can overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The last term is subtracted so the outcomes that belong to both are not counted twice.

On an invented fair cube, let A be “even” and B be “greater than 3.” P(A) = 3/6, because 2, 4, and 6 are even. P(B) = 3/6, because 4, 5, and 6 are greater than 3. P(A and B) = 2/6, because 4 and 6 are both even and greater than 3. So P(A or B) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 = 2/3. The faces that satisfy the “or” are 2, 4, 5, and 6, four faces out of six, which agrees. Adding without the subtraction would give 1, which is a miss: 2 would be counted once, but 4 and 6 would be counted twice, and 1 would be left out of the “or” while the total was wrongly filled. The cube and the events are invented classroom figures, not official probabilities of a real event.

If two events are mutually exclusive, P(A and B) is 0, and today’s rule reduces to the earlier addition rule. Today’s subtraction is not last week’s product rule, and the product must not be used in place of P(A and B) unless independence has been separately established.

Remember

For overlapping events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). On an invented fair cube, P(even or greater than 3) = 2/3. Mutually exclusive events are the special case in which the overlap is 0.

Watch me

On an invented fair cube, what is P(even or greater than 3), and why is 3/6 + 3/6 = 1 a miss?

  1. P(even) = 3/6, P(greater than 3) = 3/6, and P(both) = 2/6, so P(even or greater than 3) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 2/3.
  2. Adding without the subtraction counts 4 and 6 twice and gives 1, which is not the four faces 2, 4, 5, and 6.
  3. The cube is an invented classroom figure.
