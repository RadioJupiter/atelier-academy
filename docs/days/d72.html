Latin · Capitulum XIII: Kalendae, Nonae, and Īdūs

25 minutes · Method: the three named days of the Roman month, from Allen and Greenough §631

Learn

Key terms

Kalendae
Definition
The first day of a Roman month (Kalendae, Kalendārum, feminine plural).
Origin
Allen and Greenough §631.a note: Kalendae is derived from calāre, “to call,” the day on which the pontiffs announced the new moon.
Example
Kalendae Mārtiæ are the first of March. Every month has Kalendae on the first day.
Not this
Not the Nonae or the Īdūs, and not a name for the whole month. Kalendae is always a plural in form, even though it names one day.
Connects to
Allen and Greenough §631.a: the first day of the month was called Kalendae. The same grammar notes that Kalendae is derived from calāre, “to call.”
Nonae
Definition
A named day in each Roman month: the seventh of March, May, July, and October, and the fifth of the other months (Nonae, Nonārum, feminine plural).
Example
Nonae Mārtiæ are 7 March; Nonae Aprīlēs are 5 April.
Not this
Not the first of the month (that is Kalendae), and not the Īdūs. Nonae are not on the same numbered day in every month.
Connects to
Allen and Greenough §631.c. The name is connected with “ninths,” because the Nonae fall nine days before the Īdūs, counting inclusively; that inclusive count is taught later this week.
Īdūs
Definition
A named day in each Roman month: the fifteenth of March, May, July, and October, and the thirteenth of the other months (Īdūs, Īduum, feminine plural, fourth declension).
Example
Īdūs Mārtiæ are 15 March; Īdūs Aprīlēs are 13 April.
Not this
Not Kalendae or Nonae, and not a second-declension noun. Īdūs, Īduum continues the fourth declension begun last week with exercitus and metus, but Īdūs is used in the plural for this date.
Connects to
Allen and Greenough §631.b. Tomorrow uses the ablative Īdibus and the accusative Īdūs in dating phrases.

The Romans did not number every day of the month as “the sixth of March.” Three days in each month had names, and other days were later counted from those names.

Kalendae (Kalendārum, feminine plural) are the first day of every month.

Īdūs (Īduum, feminine plural, fourth declension) are the fifteenth of March, May, July, and October, and the thirteenth of the other months.

Nonae (Nonārum, feminine plural) are the seventh of March, May, July, and October, and the fifth of the other months. A short memory rhyme in English is only a help, not a source: those four months have the later Nonae and Īdūs.

Kalendae is always plural in form, even though it names one day. Īdūs is a fourth-declension plural, a continuation of last week’s first look at that declension, not a reteaching of exercitus as a new word.

Word How to say it Meaning
Kalendae, -ārum, f. pl. ka-LEN-dye the Kalends, the 1st of the month
Nonae, -ārum, f. pl. NO-nye the Nones, the 5th or the 7th
Īdūs, Īduum, f. pl. EE-doos the Ides, the 13th or the 15th

Kalendae: the 1st of every month. Nonae: the 7th in March, May, July, and October; the 5th in the other months. Īdūs: the 15th in those four months; the 13th in the other months. Īdūs, Īduum is fourth declension, feminine plural.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXI: a height from Pythagoras, then the area

40 minutes · Method: find h from a² + b² = c², then A = ½bh

Learn

Key terms

a height found by the Pythagorean theorem
Definition
When a perpendicular height of a triangle is not given, but it forms a right triangle with known sides, that height can be found from a² + b² = c², and then the area is ½bh.
Example
In a right triangle with legs 6 and 8, if the base is 8, the perpendicular height to that base is the other leg, 6, and the area is ½ × 8 × 6 = 24. The hypotenuse 10 is not the height to that base.
Not this
Not using the hypotenuse as if it were a height to a leg, and not adding the legs to make an area.
Connects to
The Pythagorean theorem itself was taught last week; today it is used as a tool to find h before ½bh is applied.

Sometimes the perpendicular height is not written on the figure, but it can be found. If the height is a leg of a right triangle whose other sides are known, last week’s theorem gives it: a² + b² = c², with c the hypotenuse.

This is a 6-8-10 triangle, which is right-angled at C, because 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10² (the converse, already taught). If CB is chosen as the base, of length 8, then the perpendicular height to that base is the other leg, CA = 6. The area is

A = ½ × 8 × 6 = 24.

The hypotenuse 10 is not the height to that base. The lengths are given measures in the figure.

A triangle that is not right-angled can still have a height found this way when an altitude splits off a right triangle with enough known sides. That case is used on Thursday with an isosceles triangle. Today’s job is the right-triangle case, cleanly.

Remember

If a perpendicular height is a leg of a right triangle with known sides, find h from a² + b² = c², then use A = ½bh. The hypotenuse is not a height to a leg.

Key terms: a height found by the Pythagorean theorem.

Writing & Rhetoric · The straw man

40 minutes · Method: do not answer a weakened or distorted version of the claim

Learn

Key terms

straw man
Definition
A fallacy in which a person answers a weaker, distorted, or invented version of someone else’s claim, as if that version were the claim that had been made.
Example
An invented example: A says, “A triangle’s height in A = ½bh must be perpendicular to the base.” B replies, “A thinks slanted sides do not have lengths,” and argues against that. A did not deny that slanted sides have lengths.
Not this
Not an accurate restatement of a claim followed by a real objection, and not a précis of what was actually said. A genuine disagreement with the claim as made is not a straw man.
Connects to
A précis, from last week, is a faithful short restatement. A straw man is an unfaithful restatement used as if it were the other person’s view.

A straw man is a fallacy in which someone answers a weaker, distorted, or invented version of another person’s claim, as if that were the claim that had been made. It is easier to knock down a claim no one offered. The name is a picture: a straw figure is set up and then knocked down.

An invented example, labeled invented:

A says: “A triangle’s height in A = ½bh must be perpendicular to the base.” B replies: “A thinks slanted sides do not have lengths, which is absurd.”

A did not say that slanted sides have no lengths. B has replaced A’s claim with a weaker or different one, and has answered that instead. A sound reply would meet the actual claim: that the height in the area formula has to be perpendicular.

A précis of A’s sentence would keep A’s point. A straw man does the opposite: it changes the point and then treats the change as if it were A’s.

The classroom arguments in this week’s writing are invented examples, labeled invented. They are for practicing the method. No contemporary political case is used as the set example. A précis, already taught last week, is not retaught as a first look.

Remember

A straw man answers a weaker, distorted, or invented version of someone else’s claim. A faithful restatement, or a genuine objection to the claim as made, is not a straw man. A précis is a near-miss only in the sense that it is the faithful job this fallacy refuses.

Key terms: straw man.

Science · Water: solutions, solute, and solvent

40 minutes · Method: IUPAC names for a solution and for dissolving

Learn

Key terms

solution
Definition
A liquid or solid phase containing more than one substance, when for convenience one (or more) substance, called the solvent, is treated differently from the other substances, called solutes.
Example
Salt water, in a school picture, is a liquid solution: water is treated as the solvent and the salt as the solute. The picture is for the names, not an official concentration.
Not this
Not a mere pile of two dry solids that have not formed one phase, and not last week’s surface tension under a new name. A solution is one phase with more than one substance.
Connects to
This is the IUPAC Gold Book definition (entry S05746). Water remains the week’s solvent of interest; other solvents are not begun as a list.
solute
Definition
The minor component of a solution, regarded as having been dissolved by the solvent.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin solutus, past participle of solvō.
Example
In that same school picture of salt water, the salt is the solute.
Not this
Not the solvent, and not a second name for the whole solution.
Connects to
IUPAC Gold Book S05744. “Minor component” is IUPAC’s wording; a later sitting will treat dilute and concentrated without inventing official percentages.
solvent
Definition
The substance in a solution that is treated differently from the solutes; often, and in this week’s water work, the major component regarded as having dissolved the solute.
Origin
From French solvent, from Latin solventem, accusative of solvens, the present participle of solvō.
Example
In the same school picture, water is the solvent.
Not this
Not the solute, and not a claim that water is the only solvent in chemistry. Today’s module is still Water.
Connects to
The Gold Book’s solution entry defines the solvent as the substance treated differently from the solutes. This lesson also uses the common wording “major component,” which matches the Gold Book’s solute entry (the solute as minor).
dissolution
Definition
The mixing of two phases with the formation of one new homogeneous phase, the solution. In school language, this is the dissolving itself.
Origin
From Middle English dissolucioun, from Old French dissolucion, from Latin dissolūtiō, “a dissolving, destroying, breaking up, dissolution.”
Example
Solid salt and liquid water can mix to form one liquid solution; that mixing is dissolution.
Not this
Not a change of name for melting (a solid becoming a liquid of the same substance), which belongs to earlier Water work on states, and not a surface-tension effect under a new name.
Connects to
IUPAC Gold Book D01806. The everyday verb is “dissolves”; the Gold Book’s noun is dissolution.

The Water module continues, but today’s job is not surface tension. When a substance dissolves in water, two phases mix and form one new homogeneous phase: a solution. The IUPAC Gold Book calls that mixing dissolution.

In the solution, one substance is treated as the solvent and the other (or others) as the solute. The Gold Book defines a solution as a liquid or solid phase containing more than one substance, when for convenience the solvent is treated differently from the solutes. It defines the solute as the minor component, regarded as having been dissolved by the solvent.

A school picture, not an official measurement: salt water, with water as the solvent and salt as the solute. The lesson does not invent a number of grams, a percentage, or a solubility.

Melting is not dissolution. Melting is a change of state of one substance (ice to water, already taught). Dissolution makes one phase from more than one substance.

Definitions in this week’s science follow the IUPAC Gold Book, opened at https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05746.html (solution), https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05744.html (solute), and https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/D01806.html (dissolution). No official percentage, solubility, or molarity figure is invented. Surface tension from last week is not retaught as new. Saturated solutions remain reserved.

Remember

A solution is one phase with more than one substance. The solvent is the substance treated differently (often the major component); the solute is the minor component. Dissolution is the mixing that forms that one phase. Melting is not the same job.

Key terms: solution, solute, solvent, dissolution.

Literature · Inferno I, lines 61–90: Virgilius

40 minutes · Method: the set Longfellow lines in which Virgilius appears and is recognized

Learn

Key terms

Virgilius (Longfellow, Inferno I)
Definition
The figure who presents himself in Longfellow’s Inferno I, lines 61–90: “Not man; man once I was,” born “Sub Julio,” who lived at Rome “under the good Augustus,” a poet who sang “that just / Son of Anchises,” and whom the speaker recognizes as “that Virgilius and that fountain.”
Example
The set lines include: “Now, art thou that Virgilius and that fountain / Which spreads abroad so wide a river of speech?”
Not this
Not a modern spelling substituted for Longfellow’s “Virgilius” when the task is to quote this translation, and not a figure imported from later cantos that have not been set. Last week’s panther, lion, and she-wolf remain in lines already taught; this sitting is the meeting.
Connects to
The speaker has been thrust back “thither where the sun is silent” at the end of last week’s lines. These lines begin as he is “rushing downward to the lowland.”

Last week’s set lines ended with the she-wolf thrusting the speaker back “thither where the sun is silent.” Today continues in Longfellow’s 1867 translation, Canto I, lines 61–90. Lines 91–111, including a prophecy of a Greyhound, remain reserved.

While I was rushing downward to the lowland,
Before mine eyes did one present himself,
Who seemed from long-continued silence hoarse.
When I beheld him in the desert vast,
“Have pity on me,” unto him I cried,
“Whiche’er thou art, or shade or real man!”
He answered me: “Not man; man once I was,
And both my parents were of Lombardy,
And Mantuans by country both of them.
Sub Julio was I born, though it was late,
And lived at Rome under the good Augustus,
During the time of false and lying gods.
A Poet was I, and I sang that just
Son of Anchises, who came forth from Troy,
After that Ilion the superb was burned.
But thou, why goest thou back to such annoyance?
Why climb’st thou not the Mount Delectable,
Which is the source and cause of every joy?”
“Now, art thou that Virgilius and that fountain
Which spreads abroad so wide a river of speech?”
I made response to him with bashful forehead.
“O, of the other poets honor and light,
Avail me the long study and great love
That have impelled me to explore thy volume!
Thou art my master, and my author thou,
Thou art alone the one from whom I took
The beautiful style that has done honor to me.
Behold the beast, for which I have turned back;
Do thou protect me from her, famous Sage,
For she doth make my veins and pulses tremble.”

The speaker, “rushing downward to the lowland,” sees one who seems “from long-continued silence hoarse.” He cries, “Have pity on me,” and “Whiche’er thou art, or shade or real man!” The answer is: “Not man; man once I was.” The speaker’s parents were “of Lombardy,” and “Mantuans by country.” He was born Sub Julio, “though it was late,” and lived at Rome “under the good Augustus.” He is a poet who sang “that just / Son of Anchises, who came forth from Troy, / After that Ilion the superb was burned.”

The speaker recognizes him: “Now, art thou that Virgilius and that fountain / Which spreads abroad so wide a river of speech?” He calls him “master” and “author,” the one from whom he took “the beautiful style that has done honor to me.” He then asks protection from “the beast, for which I have turned back.”

Quote Longfellow’s wording, including “Virgilius,” “honor,” “Whiche’er,” “climb’st,” and “Sub Julio.” A remembered other translation, or an Italian name used as if it were this English line, is not a quotation of Longfellow. The Greyhound of the reserved lines is not to be imported from memory.

Atelier · Value in the sockets

90 minutes · Method: the already-placed socket as a recess in value

Learn

Key terms

value in the eye socket
Definition
The well of the already-placed socket is treated as a recess: usually darker than the forehead and the brow, with a terminator where the plane turns, not a outlined almond drawn on the surface.
Example
If the light is from above and to one side, the inner well of the socket goes down in value, while the plane of the brow above it stays lighter.
Not this
Not a new placement of the socket, not a flat shaded patch with no plane, and not a drawing of eyelashes as a substitute for the recess.
Connects to
Last week’s card was the socket as a plane. Today is the value of that plane.

Yesterday named the value pass. Today’s job is the sockets. The socket was already placed last week as a plane. Today it is treated as a recess in value.

The well of the socket usually goes darker than the forehead and the brow, because it turns away from a light from above. The terminator sits where that plane turns, not as a hard outline drawn around an almond eye on the surface. Lashes and a fully drawn iris are not today’s job; the recess is.

Work both sockets from the same light. If the light is from one side, the two wells will not be identical in value, but both remain recesses, not flat patches.

This week is a value pass across the features already placed last week on the blocked-in head. It is not a new medium, not a first head, and not a first feature-pass. Definition

Elective · Music: authentic and plagal cadences

25 minutes · Method: V–I as an authentic cadence; IV–I as a plagal cadence

Learn

Key terms

authentic cadence
Definition
A cadence that arrives from V to I: the dominant chord moves to the tonic chord. In last week’s I–IV–V–I progression, the last two chords are an authentic cadence.
Origin
Borrowed from Middle French cadence, from Old Italian cadenza, “conclusion of a phrase of music,” from Latin *cadentia, literally “a falling,” from cadō, “to fall, to cease.”
Example
In C major, the chords G major to C major (V–I) form an authentic cadence.
Not this
Not a plagal cadence, which arrives from IV to I, and not a first look at I–IV–V–I as a progression (that was last week). Today names the arrival V–I.
Connects to
The word cadence is borrowed from Middle French cadence, from Old Italian cadenza, “conclusion of a phrase of music,” from a Latin word meaning a falling, from cadō, “to fall, to cease,” as the English Wiktionary entry states.
plagal cadence
Definition
A cadence that arrives from IV to I: the subdominant chord moves to the tonic chord.
Example
In C major, the chords F major to C major (IV–I) form a plagal cadence.
Not this
Not an authentic cadence (V–I), and not a claim that last week’s I–IV–V–I ended with IV–I. That progression ended V–I.
Connects to
Both cadences arrive at I. They differ in the chord that leads there. Inversion, already taught, can apply to these chords, but today’s first look is the two roots: V–I and IV–I.

Last week built a short I–IV–V–I progression, with inversion already taught. Today names two cadences, arrivals at I, that sit inside that work.

An authentic cadence arrives from V to I. In the progression I–IV–V–I, the last two chords are an authentic cadence. In C major, that is G major to C major.

A plagal cadence arrives from IV to I. In C major, that is F major to C major. Last week’s full progression did not end with a plagal cadence; it ended V–I. A plagal arrival can still be heard on its own, or as IV–I extracted from a longer phrase.

Both cadences arrive at the tonic. They differ in the chord that leads there. This lesson does not begin a new Roman-numeral function beyond V, IV, and I, already used last week, and it does not invent official tempo or metronome marks.

Remember

Authentic cadence: V–I. Plagal cadence: IV–I. Both arrive at I. Last week’s I–IV–V–I ends with an authentic cadence, not a plagal one.

Key terms: authentic cadence, plagal cadence.
