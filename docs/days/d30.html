Definition

Example

Mathematics · Two-week problem set: circles and progressions

60 min · Two-week check of Euclid III.20–22, 31, 35 and of AP/GP sums; reasons required

Learn

Key terms

two-week mathematics check
Definition
A mixed problem set on this fortnight’s skills: central and inscribed angles, the angle in a semicircle, cyclic quadrilaterals, intersecting chords, and the sums of arithmetic and geometric progressions, with the reason named each time.
Example
An 80° central angle, a diameter of 13, a cyclic quad with a 70° angle, segments 3 and 8 against 4 and x, and S of an AP or a GP — each is a separate, labeled item, or two facts in one figure with both hypotheses checked.
Not this
Not a new theorem day, and not a request to invent a formula you were not taught (no infinite GP sums).
Connects to
Write the reason beside the number. A correct number with the wrong theorem is not yet the skill.

Closed notes, then the key. Name the hypothesis and the theorem or formula before you compute.

A short reminder card (then turn it over):

  • Same arc: inscribed = half central (III.20); same segment: inscribed angles equal (III.21).
  • Diameter: angle in the semicircle is 90° (III.31).
  • Cyclic quad: opposite angles sum to 180° (III.22).
  • Interior crossing: AE · EC = BE · ED (III.35).
  • AP sum: n/2 · (first + last).
  • GP sum: a(r^n − 1)/(r − 1) if r ≠ 1; else n a.

This is a two-week set, in the same spirit as Day 20, not a new topic.

Remember

Name the fact, then compute. Inscribed/central, semicircle, cyclic quad, intersecting chords, AP sum, GP sum. No new formulas.

Writing & Rhetoric · Two-week check: a three-paragraph essay

45 min · Two-week check: one short essay, jobs on the list, then a revision pass of the failing sentences

Learn

Key terms

three-paragraph essay
Definition
A complete short argument or exposition with one thesis, three paragraphs (opening, support, close), at least one attributed piece of evidence or a précis of a source, a real concession or limit, and a concluding return to the thesis in new words.
Example
A short essay on why a late source must be labeled late, or on why a net equation must be labeled a summary, doing all of the jobs on the list, is a three-paragraph essay in this course’s sense.
Not this
Not three unconnected paragraphs, not a summary of a lesson, and not a page of quotations. Length is not the test; the jobs on the list are the test.
Connects to
This is the two-week writing check. Use a school subject from the course. A revision pass (Day 20) still means checking the list and changing the sentences that fail.

The list, then you write.

  1. A thesis in the opening, in your own words.
  2. A support paragraph with a topic sentence and attributed evidence (theorem, short quotation, or précis).
  3. A close with a real concession or limit and a return in new words.
  4. Joints that are grammatical: parallelism where you use a series; no dangling modifier; attribution in place.

Conditions. Closed notes for the first draft if you can work from your hint-list only; the source page may be opened for an exact short quotation or a date you must not invent. Time the sitting. Then mark from the key’s list, not from “how it felt.”

Remember

One thesis, three paragraphs, attributed evidence, a real concession, a return in new words. Jobs, not length, are the test. Revise the sentences that fail the list.

Key terms: three-paragraph essay.

Watch me

  2. The thesis is one sentence, on a school subject, not a title only.
  3. The essay itself is the Check that follows; this Watch is the start of the sitting.

Physics · Two-week check: work, energy, and a first look at conservation

50 min · Two-week check of W, KE, PE, and KE + PE, including one friction item; g = 9.8; a first look beyond Term 1

Learn

Key terms

two-week physics check
Definition
A mixed check of work, kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy, and mechanical energy, including one problem in which friction does work so that KE + PE is not constant.
Example
W = F s cosθ, KE = ½ m v², PE = m g h, W_net = ΔKE, and, with no friction, KE + PE constant. With friction, W_friction = Δ(KE + PE) in the sense that the mechanical sum changes by that work.
Not this
Not a new law, and not a request to use g = 10 unless you label an approximation. g = 9.8 in this course’s checks.
Connects to
This fortnight was a first look at energy, beyond the Term 1 list. The check should be as honest as the teaching.

A reminder, then a mixed sitting.

  • W = F s cosθ, in joules. Holding still is zero work. A perpendicular force does zero work.
  • KE = ½ m v²; W_net = ΔKE.
  • PE = m g h near the Earth, one chosen zero, g = 9.8 N/kg.
  • No friction: KE + PE constant. From rest, v = √(2 g h) at a drop h.
  • With friction opposite a displacement s: W_friction = −F_friction s, and the mechanical sum changes by that work.

A friction example you should be able to do. A 2 kg object starts from rest at 2.5 m (49 J of PE). Friction does −9 J of work on the way down. Then KE + PE at the floor is 40 J, so KE = 40 J, v² = 40, v = √40 = 2√10 m/s (about 6.3 m/s, approximate). The 9 J has left the mechanical account; the work–energy theorem still holds if that work is included in W_net.

Remember

W = F s cosθ; KE = ½mv²; PE = mgh; W_net = ΔKE; KE + PE constant only when friction (and other such forces) do no work. g = 9.8. Name the model you are using.

Key terms: two-week physics check.

Watch me

  1. No friction: KE + PE is 49 J; at the floor KE = 49 J, v = 7 m/s.
  2. With W_friction = −9 J, the mechanical sum at the floor is 40 J, so KE = 40 J.
  3. v² = 40, v = √40 = 2√10 m/s (about 6.3 m/s, approximate).

Hint 2: Arithmetic

History · Two-week synthesis: how we know the Roman Republic

45 min · Two-week check: early tradition, mid-Republican analysis, late participant sources; one habit throughout

Learn

Key terms

how we know the Republic
Definition
A synthesis question: early Rome and early law are known through later tradition, archaeology, and fragments; the mid-Republic can be analyzed with writers such as Polybius; the late Republic speaks in participant sources that are also advocacy. The same habit of dating and classifying sources runs through all three.
Example
A careful paragraph might move from the Twelve Tables as later fragments, through Polybius on government and on the Punic Wars, to Caesar’s commentaries as primary and interested.
Not this
Not a claim that we know nothing, and not a claim that we know the early city as clearly as the 50s BCE. The quality of the evidence changes, and the wording should change with it.
Connects to
This is the two-week history check: a short synthesis, not a new period.

Three bands of evidence, one habit.

  1. Early: Livy and other late narratives; traditional dates (753, 509, mid-fifth-century Tables) labeled as tradition; archaeology as independent, limited evidence; the Twelve Tables as later fragments.
  2. Mid-Republic and the Punic Wars: a standard chronology (264–146 BCE for those wars); Polybius as a second-century historian and analyst; scarce Carthaginian literary voices in what we have.
  3. Late Republic: participant sources (Caesar, Cicero) that are also from a position; later biographies still later.

The check. Write in complete sentences, with dates and tags, without inventing a source you were not given.

Remember

Date and classify the source. Early tradition and fragments; Polybius in the second century; late-Republican participants who are also advocates. The evidence gets richer; the habit does not retire.

Key terms: how we know the Republic.

Watch me

  1. A model: Early Rome is known through later narrative (Livy, centuries later) and through archaeology, with traditional dates labeled as such; the Twelve Tables survive as later fragments.
  2. The Punic Wars have a standard chronology (264–146 BCE); Polybius is a central second-century source, and Carthaginian voices are scarce in the literary record we have.
  3. The late Republic offers participant sources such as Caesar’s commentaries and Cicero’s letters, which are primary and also written from a position.

Atelier · Critique and a two-week plan

90 min · Method: a short, specific critique of the fortnight’s head, then a repair plan; no character judgments

Learn

Key terms

critique (two-week head)
Definition
A structured look at the fortnight’s head or plaster: largest strengths, largest remaining errors of proportion or value, and a short written plan for what a later sitting (or a later week) would repair first. It is specific and brief, not a judgment of the student’s character.
Example
“The tilt and the large terminator are true; the mouth is still high; next time I measure mouth-to-nose first” is a critique.
Not this
Not “it’s bad,” not “it’s fine,” and not a list of twenty equally weighted nits. Name two or three facts about the drawing.
Connects to
Week 4 already used a critique-and-plan Friday. Today’s object is this head.

The order of a critique. (1) What is true, in a sentence (tilt, envelope, a value family, a feature in context). (2) The largest remaining error (a measure or a false value, not a missing eyelash). (3) A next step, small enough to start with.

A two-week look. Features were taught in context; lost edges and accents came late. The critique should use those words, not a vague “more realistic.”

Plan, not a new head from zero, unless the envelope is genuinely lost. A later week can start a second plate; tomorrow’s plan can still be a repair of this one.

Remember

Name a strength, the largest remaining error of measure or value, and a next step. Be specific. Do not start a new head to avoid looking at this one, unless the envelope is truly lost.

Key terms: critique (two-week head).

Modern language · Spanish: two-week review and a short composition

40 min · Two-week check: a present-tense composition using the fortnight’s skills; no new tense

Learn

Key terms

two-week Spanish composition
Definition
A short present-tense composition that must use this fortnight’s skills: e → i stems, the present progressive, indirect-object pronouns, the gustar pattern, tener que / hay / hay que, demonstratives, telling time, and a careful por / para contrast. No new tense is introduced.
Example
A paragraph about a class at a stated hour, what you have to do, what you like, what you are doing now, and a book for a person, can use most of the list without a hunt for rare words.
Not this
Not a preterite story, and not a vocabulary display. If you do not know a word, use one you were taught.
Connects to
Week 5 reviewed the first four skills; week 6 added the rest. Today is a check of both weeks.

The list, then you write, in the present.

  • e → i: pedir, servir, repetir, vestir (pido, not *piedo).
  • Progressive: estoy leyendo, está pidiendo (not *estoy leer).
  • IOP: Le doy el libro a Ana.
  • gustar: Me gusta el libro; Me gustan las clases; Nos gusta leer.
  • tener que, hay, hay que.
  • este / ese / aquel (and agreement).
  • ¿Qué hora es? Es la una / Son las dos, a las diez.
  • por vs para, by job.

A short paragraph or two (not a packet) is enough if the list is honestly used. Then mark what you used, and repair one missed job rather than recopying the whole.

Remember

Write in the present, using the fortnight’s skills (stems, progressive, IOP, gustar, hay/tener que, demonstratives, time, por/para). Repair a missed job; do not start a past-tense story.

Elective · Poetry 3: imagery and metaphor, with a public-domain poem

40 min · Text: William Blake, “The Tyger,” 1794, public domain; imagery and metaphor named and used on short quotations

Learn

Key terms

imagery
Definition
Language that appeals to the senses — seeing, hearing, touch, and the rest — so a reader can picture or otherwise sense a scene or an object. Imagery can be literal description, and it can also support a metaphor; the two are not the same word.
Origin
From French imagerie, from image (Wiktionary).
Example
In Blake’s “The Tyger” (1794, public domain): “burning bright / In the forests of the night” gives visual fire and a dark wood, before the poem’s questions about a maker.
Not this
Not only visual pictures (sound and texture count), and not a list of every adjective in a poem as if each were a finished image.
Connects to
Poetry 1 and 2 already used meter, caesura, enjambment, and the volta. Today the skill is noticing sensory language and saying what it is doing, not hunting for a “hidden meaning” with no text under it.
metaphor
Definition
A comparison made by speaking of one thing as if it were another, without a comparing word such as *like* or *as* (those usually mark a simile). A metaphor carries a description or a name across from one domain to another.
Origin
From Latin metaphora, from Greek μεταφορά, ‘a carrying across’ (Wiktionary).
Example
If a poem calls a tiger a fire, or treats a maker’s work as a forging, it is asking you to see one thing in terms of another, not only to measure a real furnace.
Not this
Not a simile (which still names the comparison with like or as), and not every strong adjective. “The forest is dark” can be imagery without being a metaphor.
Connects to
The origin is Greek μεταφορά, ‘a carrying across,’ through Latin metaphora (Wiktionary). That origin matches the job: a carrying of name or description from one thing to another.

A public-domain model. William Blake, “The Tyger,” from Songs of Experience (1794), is in the public domain. A first stanza, for study (short, attributed):

“Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night;
What immortal hand or eye,
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?”

Imagery. “Burning bright” and “forests of the night” are sensory (fire, darkness, a wood). You can say what you see and hear in the words without yet settling every debate about the poem.

Metaphor and question. The tiger is spoken of as burning — a carrying-across, not a zoological claim. The stanza then asks about a maker (“hand or eye,” “frame”). A later stanza’s forge and anvil (if you read on, still public domain) extend a making metaphor. You need not interpret the whole theology of the poem today.

The skill. Quote a short phrase, name it as imagery or as metaphor (or as both, if both are honest), and say what it does in the stanza. Do not invent a biography of Blake as a substitute for the lines.

Remember

Imagery appeals to the senses. A metaphor speaks of one thing as if it were another, without like or as (those usually mark a simile). Quote shortly, attribute, and say what the language is doing. Blake (1794) is the model; a full theology of the poem is not required.

Key terms: imagery, metaphor.

Watch me

In “burning bright / In the forests of the night,” what is working as imagery, and what, if anything, is already metaphorical?

  1. Imagery: brightness and burning as something seen; a forest and night as a place and a darkness.
  2. Metaphor (or a metaphor already under way): the tiger is spoken of as burning, a fire-term carried across to an animal.
  3. You can name both without deciding every later question about the poem’s maker.

Hint 1: like or as

Hint 2: Carrying across

A metaphor drops the comparing word: “the proof, a ladder, had a broken rung.”

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is a finite geometric-progression sum different from an arithmetic-progression sum, and what extra fact do you need?
  2. An AP sum uses the average of the first and last terms: S = n/2 · (a + ℓ). A finite GP sum uses S = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1, or S = n a when r = 1.
  3. You need the common ratio r, not only a common difference d, and you must not use the AP pairing formula on a GP.
  4. A near-miss is to add n times the first term, or to apply n/2 · (a + ℓ), to a geometric sequence.

Together

In your own words, what is mechanical energy in the no-friction model taught this week, and when is it not conserved in the way that model says?

Hint 1: The model

Mechanical energy is KE + PE, with PE = m g h near the Earth in this course’s first model. With no work by friction or other non-conservative forces, the sum stays constant.

Hint 2: Friction

When friction does negative work, mechanical energy decreases by that amount; the energy is not “destroyed,” but it leaves the KE + PE account as this course is keeping it.
