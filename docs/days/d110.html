Remember

The deponent perfect is locūtus sum and cōnātus sum, active in meaning, with the participle agreeing in number. The present, loquor, is a different tense. The ablative absolute has no sum: a present participle is contemporaneous; a perfect participle is already complete.

  2. Puerī locūtī sunt. Nōs cōnātī sumus.
  3. Urbe captā, agricolae fugiunt. Magistrō recitante, puerī silent.

Together

Hint 1: The six

Locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt.

Hint 2: The contrast

The tense uses est. The absolute has no sum.

Mathematics · Friday mix: sine, cosine, and complementary angles

30 min · Friday mix of this week’s right-triangle ratios. Degrees only. The radian, the unit circle, and tangent remain reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • sine of an acute angle — Day 106
  • cosine of an acute angle — Day 107
  • sine and cosine of complementary angles — Day 108

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today is a mix of this week’s methods. No new ratio is begun. Degrees only. Close the cards first.

Remember

Sin A is opposite over hypotenuse. Cos A is adjacent over hypotenuse. Sin(90° − A) = cos A, because the two acute angles add to 90°. In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5 and cos A = 4/5. Sine and cosine are ratios, not lengths. Degrees only.

Watch me

Watch a short mix, then write your own.

  1. 3-4-5, A opposite 3: sin A = 3/5, cos A = 4/5.
  2. Sin(90° − A) = 4/5.
  3. A similar 6-8-10 triangle keeps the same ratios.

Together

In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, A is opposite 3. Give sin A, cos A, and sin(90° − A). Then say why a 6-8-10 triangle with the same angle A has the same sine.

Hint 1: The three

3/5, 4/5, and 4/5.

Hint 2: Similarity

The triangles are similar, so the ratios do not change.

Check

Mix this week’s mathematics. Degrees only.

  1. Write the definitions of sin A and of cos A, and the complementary relation.
  2. In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, A is opposite 3. Give sin A, cos A, sin(90° − A), and cos(90° − A).
  3. In a classroom 5-12-13 triangle, A is opposite 5. Give sin A and cos A.
  4. Why are sine and cosine ratios, not lengths, and what remains reserved?

Exercises

  1. A classroom right triangle similar to 3-4-5 has hypotenuse 20, and A is the angle whose opposite side is 12. Give sin A and cos A.

Physics · Friday mix: forced oscillations, resonance, and the shape of the peak

30 min · Friday mix of this week’s OpenStax 16.8 work. Invent no official measured pitch, peak height, or wind speed

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • forced oscillation and resonance — Day 106
  • damping and the resonance peak — Day 108

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today is a mix of this week’s physics. No new section is begun. Close the cards first.

Remember

A forced oscillation is driven by a periodic driving force. The natural frequency is the frequency with no driving and no damping. Resonance is driving at that natural frequency, when energy is transferred most efficiently and the amplitude is greatest. Less damping gives a higher and narrower peak; heavy damping lowers the amplitude and spreads the response. OpenStax 16.8. Invent no official measured pitch, peak height, or wind speed.

Hint 1: Resonance

Hint 2: The peak

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: the curves of the vertebral column, from Gray 1918

40 min · Method: a first look at Gray’s curves of the vertebral column, from Theodora’s public-domain text, “The Vertebral Column as a Whole.” Official regional lengths remain reserved. Last week’s count is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

curves of the vertebral column, from Gray
Definition
Gray 1918, in the public-domain text used here from Theodora, “The Vertebral Column as a Whole,” describes four curves viewed laterally, called cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and pelvic. The cervical curve is convex forward, the least marked of all the curves. The thoracic curve is concave forward; its most prominent point behind corresponds to the spinous process of the seventh thoracic vertebra. The lumbar curve is convex anteriorly. The pelvic curve begins at the sacrovertebral articulation and ends at the point of the coccyx; its concavity is directed downward and forward. The thoracic and pelvic curves are termed primary, because they alone are present during fetal life. The cervical and lumbar curves are compensatory or secondary, and are developed after birth. Official average lengths, including Gray’s 71 cm and 61 cm figures, remain reserved on this first day. A slight lateral curvature, also named by Gray, remains reserved. Keep Gray’s spelling vertebræ when you use that word. Last week’s count of 33 vertebræ is already taught, not a first look today.
Example
Today’s job is the four named curves, convex or concave forward, and the primary-or-secondary division. It is not a recitation of every process, and not a required memorizing of Gray’s developmental ages, though Gray does give them. Bartleby no longer serves the page; the set text is Theodora, the vertebral column as a whole.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s count of 33 vertebræ, or at the true-or-false division, already taught. Not a requirement to name official regional lengths. Gray’s 71 cm and 61 cm figures, and the slight lateral curvature, remain reserved. “Child,” where it appears in Gray’s developmental note, is Gray’s wording in a public-domain anatomy text, not an identification of a living person in this course, and is not a required check item today.
