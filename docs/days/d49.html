Mathematics · Geometry XXII: the incenter, and A = r s

60 min · Method: angle bisectors already constructed, now used to name the incenter; area as three small triangles. Invented figures stay labeled.

Learn

Key terms

incenter
Definition
The center of a triangle’s incircle: the point where the angle bisectors meet, equally far from all three sides. That common perpendicular distance is the inradius r.
Origin
From in- + center (English Wiktionary).
Example
In the invented 6-8-10 triangle already used on Day 47 (area 24, semiperimeter 12), r = A / s = 24 / 12 = 2.
Not this
Not the circumcenter (the center of the circumcircle), which is equally far from the three vertices, not from the three sides.
Connects to
Day 12’s angle-bisector construction, Day 47’s incircle, and the area of a triangle. The three small triangles from the incenter to the sides give A = r s, with s the semiperimeter.
inradius
Definition
The radius of the incircle: the perpendicular distance from the incenter to any side. In a triangle, A = r s, where A is the area and s is the semiperimeter (half the perimeter).
Example
For the invented 6-8-10 right triangle, A = (6 · 8)/2 = 24, s = 12, and r = 24 / 12 = 2.
Not this
Not the circumradius (the radius of the circumcircle). A = r s is for the incircle; it is not a new area formula for a rectangle.
Connects to
Day 47’s incircle, and the already-taught area of a triangle.

Yesterday the incircle was found by equal tangents. Today is its center and its radius.

The incenter I is the point inside the triangle that is equally far from all three sides. Each angle bisector is the set of points equally far from two sides (a use of the already-taught angle bisector). So the angle bisectors meet at I, and a perpendicular from I to a side is the inradius r. You already have a construction for an angle bisector (Day 12); today names what their meeting point is for.

Why A = r s. The three small triangles formed by I and the sides have the same height r. Their areas add to the area A of the triangle: A = (1/2) a r + (1/2) b r + (1/2) c r = r · (a + b + c)/2 = r s, where s is the semiperimeter.

A worked triangle. The invented 6-8-10 triangle from Day 47 is a right triangle, so A = (6 · 8)/2 = 24. The perimeter is 24, so s = 12. Then r = A / s = 24 / 12 = 2.

The tangent lengths from Day 47 (4, 2, and 6) are not needed for this computation of r, but they describe the same incircle.

Remember

The incenter is where the angle bisectors meet, equally far from the sides. The inradius r satisfies A = r s. It is not the circumcenter. Invented figures stay labeled.

Key terms: incenter, inradius.

Writing & Rhetoric · A precis of the set opening, in your own words

40 min · Method: a precis of the same four attributed lines only.

Learn

Key terms

a precis of the set opening
Definition
A very short restatement, in your own words, of only the set extract, preserving the point and the attribution, without later scenes and without copied phrasing presented as your own.
Example
A precis of the four lines: Those listening hear a man, from his couch, begin to tell — at a queen's command — a fate whose memory still hurts. (Dryden, *Aeneid* Book II, 1697.)
Not this
Not a quotation pretending to be a precis, and not a summary of the rest of Book II.
Connects to
The precis work already taught, now used on the same four attributed lines.

A precis is shorter than the passage and is in your own words. It still has to be true to the set extract, and it still needs the attribution. It is not a quotation, and it is not a later chapter in miniature.

Set extract, still the same four lines:

All were attentive to the God-like Man; When from his lofty couch he thus began. Great Queen, what you command me to relate, Renews the sad Remembrance of our Fate.

John Dryden, trans., Aeneid Book II, opening, in The Works of Virgil containing his Pastorals, Georgics and Aeneis, first published 1697 (Wikisource, The Works of Virgil (Dryden)/Aeneid/Book II). Public domain. Literature may set a second short passage today; this writing precis still uses only the opening already used in writing this week.

A model precis. At a queen's command, a man begins, from his couch, to tell a fate that hurts to remember, and those around him listen. (Dryden, Aeneid Book II, 1697.)

The model does not name a later reserved scene. Yours should not either, unless those words are in the four lines — and they are not.

Remember

A short restatement in your own words of the four set lines, with Dryden, 1697, still attributed. Not a quotation, and not the rest of Book II.

Science · Refractive errors continued: astigmatism and presbyopia

50 min · Method: the same NEI page, the remaining two common types; no invented diopters or diagnoses

Learn

Key terms

astigmatism
Definition
A refractive error that, in the NEI's wording, can make far-away and nearby objects look blurry or distorted.
Origin
From a- (negative prefix) + the stem of Ancient Greek stigma (“mark, point”) + -ism; coined by William Whewell in 1846 (English Wiktionary).
Example
The same NEI page that defined myopia and hyperopia lists astigmatism as a third common type, with blur or distortion at more than one distance.
Not this
Not “just myopia plus hyperopia” as a slogan, and not a classroom diagnosis. The NEI wording is about blur or distortion of far-away and nearby objects.
Connects to
Tuesday's refractive error, now the third type on the same NEI page.
presbyopia
Definition
A refractive error that, in the NEI's wording, makes it hard for middle-aged and older adults to see things up close. The same page says it is common in adults ages 45 and older, and lists aging of the lens among the causes of refractive errors.
Origin
From New Latin presbyopia, from Ancient Greek presbus (“old man”) and New Latin -opia, from Ancient Greek ops (“eye”) (English Wiktionary).
Example
A labeled illustration: a middle-aged adult who now holds a page farther away in order to read it is the kind of near-work difficulty the NEI name describes. This is not a diagnosis, and 45 is the NEI's wording, not a classroom cutoff we invented.
Not this
Not myopia, and not a claim that every older adult has the same sight. The lesson does not invent a mechanism beyond the NEI's “aging of the lens.”
Connects to
Tuesday's refractive error, and the already-taught accommodation of the lens, now beside the NEI's note on aging of the lens.

Tuesday took two of the NEI's four common refractive errors. Today takes the other two from the same page, still without invented diopters and still without a classroom diagnosis.

Astigmatism, in the NEI's wording, "can make far-away and nearby objects look blurry or distorted."

Presbyopia "makes it hard for middle-aged and older adults to see things up close." The page also says that presbyopia is common in adults ages 45 and older, and that refractive errors can be caused by eyeball length, problems with the shape of the cornea, or aging of the lens. That last cause sits beside week 8's lesson on accommodation (the lens changing shape), without inventing a new mechanism or a classroom age test.

Treatment remains as Tuesday: the NEI says a doctor can prescribe eyeglasses or contact lenses. Surgery remains listed by the NEI and untaught here.

Remember

Astigmatism, in the NEI's wording, can blur or distort far and near. Presbyopia makes close work hard for middle-aged and older adults; the NEI also names aging of the lens. Still no invented numbers, and no classroom diagnosis.

Key terms: astigmatism, presbyopia.

Not this

Connects to

creo que KREH-oh keh I believe that / I think that

crees que KREH-es keh you believe that

Instruction stays in English. Today is two ways to introduce an opinion in the present: pienso que and creo que, then a present-tense clause you already know.

profit, in an invented shop example
Definition
In this first look, profit is a leftover: the amount that remains when cost is subtracted from the money taken in (the price received, or the takings) in a labeled, invented example. It is not a law about every real shop, and the figures are invented and labeled as invented.
Origin
From Middle English profit, from Old French profit, from Latin profectus, “advance, progress, growth, increase, profit,” from proficere (English Wiktionary).
Example
An invented classroom shop sells one stool for 30, and the labeled cost of that stool is 18, so the leftover is 30 − 18 = 12. Those numbers are invented.
Not this
Not price alone, not cost alone, and not a claim about a real business or a real wage. If the takings are less than the cost, the leftover is negative; this lesson will name that as a loss in the same invented sense, not as a drama.
Connects to

Today is a first look at profit as a leftover in a labeled, invented shop example. The figures are invented, and they stay labeled as invented. This is not a report on a real shop.

If the money taken in for a thing is the price received, and the cost is what was paid or used up to have that thing ready to sell, then in this first model

profit = money taken in − cost.

A worked invented example. One stool is sold for 30. The labeled cost is 18. The leftover is 30 − 18 = 12. If instead the cost were 40, the leftover would be 30 − 40 = −12, which this lesson will call a loss in the same invented sense.

Price and cost are not the same number. An incentive (last week) might change what a shop decides to do; today only computes a leftover from labeled figures. No real firm's books are being claimed.

Remember

In this first look, profit = money taken in − cost, in a labeled invented example. Price and cost are not the same. A negative leftover is a loss in the same invented sense. Not a real shop.
