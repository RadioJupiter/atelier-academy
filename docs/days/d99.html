Mathematics · Geometry LIV: the surface area of a composite solid

45 min · Method: the surface area of a composite is the sum of the exposed faces only; a hidden joined face is not counted

Learn

Key terms

surface area of a composite solid
Definition
The surface area of a composite solid is the sum of the areas that can still be seen or touched. A face where two pieces join is hidden and is not counted. That is why you cannot simply add the two solids’ complete surface areas: each complete surface still includes the hidden face.
Example
The classroom cube of edge 6 with a square pyramid of height 4 and slant height 5 on the top, invented lengths already used this week: the cube would have surface 6 × 36 = 216, but the top face 36 is hidden, so the cube contributes 180. The pyramid’s four faces contribute 60, and its base is hidden, so it contributes 60, not 96. Together, 240 square units.
Not this
Not yesterday’s volume, which does add the pieces in full. Not a first look at a cube’s surface, or at Monday’s pyramid surface, except as already-taught pieces. Classroom lengths are invented.
Connects to
Monday’s pyramid surface, Tuesday’s cone surface, and yesterday’s composite volume, as a contrast.

Yesterday the joined face did not change the volume. Today it does change how you count the outside.

Cube of edge 6: six faces of 36 would be 216, but the top is hidden under the pyramid, so the cube contributes 5 × 36 = 180. The pyramid’s four faces, with l = 5, contribute 60; its base is hidden, so it is not added. The composite’s surface is 240 square units.

A second classroom case: the cone of radius 3, height 4, l = 5, with a hemisphere of radius 3 on the base. The cone’s lateral area is 15π; its base is hidden. The hemisphere’s curved surface is half of last week’s sphere surface 4πr² = 36π, so 18π; its flat disk is hidden. Together, 33π square units, in terms of π.

Remember

Count only exposed faces. Do not add two complete surfaces. Classroom lengths are invented. Exact π answers stay in terms of π.

Writing & Rhetoric · A précis of Dewing I.1.67–73

40 min · Method: a précis, already taught, of Dewing, Buildings I.1.67–73, the eastern arch

Learn

Key terms

précis of Dewing I.1.67–73
Definition
A précis of Dewing I.1.67–73 keeps his points, order, and proportion: the church was built not with money alone but also with labor of the mind and the other powers of the soul, as he will “shew”; the eastern arch was unfinished in the middle; the piers began to crack; Anthemius and Isidorus, having no hope in their skill, went to the Emperor; he commanded them to complete the curve, saying, “For when it rests upon itself, it will no longer need the props beneath it”; and, because there are many witnesses, Dewing will tell it; the artisans did as instructed, and the arch hung secure.
Example
A sound précis will still have the cracked piers, the Emperor’s command and the quoted reason, and the arch hanging secure, and will not invent a modern measurement of the crack, or drop the claim that the story has witnesses.
Not this
Not a first look at yesterday’s treasures and the forty thousand pounds’ weight of silver, except as already-taught context. British spellings in quotations follow Dewing, including “shew” and “labour.” Anthemius and Isidorus are already-taught names, not first looks.
Connects to
Yesterday’s précis of I.1.64–66, and Wednesday’s history reading of I.1.67–73.

Dewing continues. The Emperor built the church “not with money alone,” but also “with labour of the mind and with the other powers of the soul, as I shall straightway shew.” One of the arches toward the east had been built up from either side, but was not wholly completed in the middle. The piers, unable to carry the mass, suddenly began to crack, and seemed on the point of collapsing. Anthemius and Isidorus, “terrified at what had happened,” having “come to have no hope in their technical skill,” carried the matter to the Emperor. He, “impelled by I know not what, but I suppose by God (for he is not himself a master-builder),” commanded them to carry the curve to its final completion. “For when it rests upon itself,” he said, “it will no longer need the props beneath it.” If the story were without witness, Dewing says, it would have seemed flattery and incredible; but there are many witnesses. The artisans carried out the instructions, “and the whole arch then hung secure, sealing by experiment the truth of his idea.”

Keep “labour” and “shew” inside quotations. Do not invent a width for the crack, or a modern engineering proof Dewing does not give.

Science · Fusion, sublimation, and vaporization, with the sources kept distinct

40 min · Method: a mixed comparison of Harvey’s 0 °C ice figures and OpenStax’s Lv at 100 °C, without blending the tables

Learn

Key terms

three sourced phase-change figures for water
Definition
Three already-taught figures, kept with their sources: Harvey/NIST, ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g and ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g, at 101.325 kPa; OpenStax Table 14.2, Lv = 2256 kJ/kg at 100.0 °C. A comparison may be made, but a difference or a blend is not a new official figure. Ice Ih still has cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 0 °C in Harvey, used only as a 1 °C contrast, not as a new first look.
Example
Vaporizing 1 g at 100 °C, on OpenStax’s figure, takes 2256 J. Melting 1 g at 0 °C, on Harvey’s figure, takes 333.4 J. Subliming 1 g at 0 °C, on Harvey’s figure, takes 2834 J. Those are three sourced jobs, not one subtracted table.
Not this
Not a first look at any of the three figures, except that yesterday was the first look at OpenStax’s Lv. Not a new official 100 °C row fetched from Harvey, which still has not been used for Lv. Not a blend of OpenStax’s Lf 334 kJ/kg with Harvey’s 333.4 J/g.
Connects to

Three sourced figures now sit on the table. They are not one table, and they are not to be merged.

Harvey/NIST, ice at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa: to melt 1 g takes 333.4 J; to sublime 1 g takes 2834 J; to warm 1 g of ice 1 °C takes 2.10 J, already taught as a contrast, not a new skill today.

OpenStax Table 14.2: to vaporize 1 g (or 1 kg at 2256 kJ) at 100.0 °C takes 2256 J (or 2256 kJ). That is yesterday’s figure.

You may say, as a comparison with the sources named, that OpenStax’s boiling figure is much larger than Harvey’s melting figure, and that Harvey’s sublimation figure at 0 °C is larger still than OpenStax’s boiling figure at 100 °C. Atelier · A written pause, after the jaw, the mouth, and the check

40 min · Method: a written pause, already taught last week, after this week’s monochrome work on the jaw and mouth

Learn

Key terms

written pause after jaw and mouth
Definition
A written pause, already taught last week, is a short note of what was seen and what was corrected, written before another pass. Today it follows the underplane of the jaw, the mouth as a slot, and the likeness check of mouth and chin together. The note names only what was actually seen or changed; it does not invent a second lamp, a smile, or a color that was not used. Full color remains reserved.
Example
A sound pause might say: the underplane was too light and was brought back into the shadow family; the mouth had been a line and is now a slot; the opening sat a little high and was lowered. It would not say that a color pass was begun, or that Gray’s mandibular foramen had been drawn, if neither was done.
Not this

Modern language · Spanish: pedir and dormir, a two-verb preterite contrast

40 min · Method: pedir and dormir in the preterite, side by side, so i and u are not swapped

Learn

Key terms

pedir and dormir in the preterite, contrasted
Definition
Pedir and dormir, in the preterite, share one shape: the stem vowel changes only in the third persons. Pedir goes e to i (pidió, pidieron). Dormir goes o to u (durmió, durmieron). The other forms keep the original vowel: pedí, dormí; pedimos, dormimos. The two vowels must not be swapped.
Example
Ella pidió ayuda, but el niño durmió. Ellos pidieron la cuenta; ellos durmieron temprano. Ayer pedí un libro; anoche dormí bien.
Not this
Not a first look at either preterite set, already taught this week. Not a present-tense contrast, already done for pedir on Tuesday. Instruction remains in English.
Connects to
Monday’s pedir and Wednesday’s dormir, as a pair.

Two verbs, one shape, two vowels. Write them side by side, then keep them from trading places.

Pedí, pediste, pidió, pedimos, pedisteis, pidieron.

Dormí, dormiste, durmió, dormimos, dormisteis, durmieron.

Pidió is “asked for,” not “slept.” Durmió is “slept,” not “asked for.” Pedimos and dormimos both keep the original vowel.

Word How to say it Meaning
pidió he or she asked for es
durmió he or she slept es
pidieron / durmieron they asked for / they slept es

Remember

Pedir: e to i in the third-person preterite only. Dormir: o to u in the third-person preterite only. Do not swap the vowels. Pedimos and dormimos keep the original vowel.

Key terms

three-year repayment table (invented)
Definition
Last week’s simple interest on a declining principal is written today as a three-year table, on invented figures. Each year, the interest is computed on the principal still outstanding at the start of that year, at the stated simple rate; a labeled invented principal repayment is then subtracted; the next year’s opening principal is what remains. Interest is paid separately and is not added to the principal. This is not last week’s only contrast, the equal installment of a known total, and it is not a monthly bank plan or a first look at compounding, already taught earlier as a different method.
Example
Invented: $300 at 10% a year, with a $100 principal repayment at the end of each year, interest paid separately. Year 1: interest $30, principal repayment $100, remaining $200. Year 2: interest $20, principal repayment $100, remaining $100. Year 3: interest $10, principal repayment $100, remaining $0. Total interest $60. Total principal repaid $300.
