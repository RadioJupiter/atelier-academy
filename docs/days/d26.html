Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 1: ille, and hic against is

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VIII · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

ille, illa, illud
Definition
The far demonstrative: ‘that’ (farther from the speaker, or already distinguished from ‘this’). A working set today: ille, illa, illud (nominative), illum, illam, illud (accusative), and the idea that illīus / illī (genitive and dative singular, all genders) will appear as you read. You need the nominative and accusative fluently today; the other cases will be practiced as they occur in original sentences and in the book.
Origin
From Latin dēmōnstrātīvus (Wiktionary).
Example
Hic puer est Mārcus; ille puer est Quīntus. This boy is Marcus; that boy is Quintus.
Not this
Not hic (this, near). And not the adverb illīc (‘there’), which is a different word. is, ea, id remains the weaker ‘he/she/it / the one just mentioned,’ not a strong pointing word.
Connects to
Yesterday ended Capitulum VII with a first set of hic. Capitulum VIII sets hic and ille next to each other. Original sentences only; do not copy the book’s wording.
is, ea, id (reminder)
Definition
The pronoun of reference: ‘he, she, it / that one just mentioned,’ without the strong near/far pointing of hic and ille.
Example
Mārcus rosam habet. Eam Iūliae dat. Marcus has a rose. He gives it to Julia.
Not this
Not ‘this here’ or ‘that over there.’ If you mean to point, use hic or ille.
Connects to
You have used eum and eam for weeks. Today they stay in the system beside the two demonstratives.
Word How to say it Meaning
ille IL-leh that (masculine nominative)
illa IL-lah that (feminine nominative)
illud IL-lud that (neuter nominative or accusative)
illum IL-lum that (masculine accusative)
illam IL-lahm that (feminine accusative)
illīc IL-leek there (adverb — not the pronoun)
is / ea / id is / eh-ah / id he, she, it; the one just mentioned

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Three jobs, kept apart. hic points near: this. ille points far, or contrasts: that. is refers back without pointing strongly.

Original sentences: Hic puer in hortō est; ille puer in vīllā est. Iūlia illam rosam, nōn hanc, Mārcō dat. Mārcus librum habet; eum Iūliae ostendit.

Neuter illud and hoc end in -d in the nominative and accusative singular, not in -um (compare pulchrum). That is a pronominal signature you can learn to see.

Remember

hic = this (near); ille = that (far or contrasted); is = the one just mentioned. illud / hoc end in -d, not -um. illīc is the adverb ‘there.’

Key terms: ille, illa, illud, is, ea, id (reminder).

Mathematics · Algebra VIII: the sum of a finite geometric progression

60 min · Method: the rS − S subtraction as the reason, then the formula; r = 1 as a separate case

Learn

Key terms

sum of a finite geometric progression
Definition
The sum of the first n terms of a geometric progression with first term a and common ratio r is Sn = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1. When r = 1, every term is a, and Sn = n a. Day 20 defined the GP and the nth term; today adds the finite sum.
Example
2 + 6 + 18 + 54: a = 2, r = 3, n = 4, S = 2(34 − 1)/(3 − 1) = 2(81 − 1)/2 = 80.
Not this
Not the AP pairing formula n/2 · (a + ℓ), which belongs to a constant *difference*. And not an infinite-sum formula, which this course does not use today (and which would need |r| < 1 and a later argument).
Connects to
A reason: S = a + ar + … + ar^{n−1}, rS = ar + … + ar^n, subtract: rS − S = ar^n − a, so S(r − 1) = a(r^n − 1).

The subtraction trick, which is the reason. Write S, then rS, and subtract. Most terms cancel, and you are left with S = a(r^n − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1.

If r = 1, that formula divides by zero and is the wrong tool: the sum is just n a.

Worked numbers. 2 + 6 + 18 + 54 = 80. 5 + 10 + 20 + 40 + 80 = 5(2^5 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 5(32 − 1) = 155. Five terms of 3, 3, 3, … : r = 1, S = 15.

A signed ratio is allowed: 3 + (−6) + 12 + (−24): a = 3, r = −2, n = 4, S = 3((-2)^4 − 1)/(-2 − 1) = 3(16 − 1)/(−3) = 3(15)/(−3) = −15. Check by adding: 3 − 6 + 12 − 24 = −15.

Remember

S = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1; S = n a when r = 1. Do not use the AP pairing formula on a GP. No infinite sums today.

Key terms: sum of a finite geometric progression.

Writing & Rhetoric · The three-paragraph outline

45 min · Method: an extension of the two-paragraph outline (Day 16) with a closing concession and return

Learn

Key terms

three-paragraph outline
Definition
A plan for a short essay of three paragraphs: an opening paragraph that states a thesis, a middle paragraph that develops one line of support (with evidence or a précis of a source), and a closing paragraph that includes a concession or a limit and then returns to the thesis in new words.
Example
Thesis: inscribed angles are half the central angle only on the same arc. Middle: Euclid III.20 and a worked 80°/40° figure. Close: a concession that a different arc gives a different measure, then a restatement of the same-arc rule.
Not this
Not three unconnected paragraphs, and not one long paragraph chopped at two indentations. The jobs are different.
Connects to
Day 16 taught the two-paragraph outline. Today adds a third paragraph for a concession and a return, which Day 19 already practiced in two-paragraph form. The new skill is the three-job plan, not a longer page for its own sake.

The three jobs. (1) Open: thesis, in one or two sentences, plus a brief map of the support. (2) Support: one focused paragraph with a reason and a piece of evidence (a theorem, a short attributed quotation, or a précis of a source). (3) Close: a real concession or limit, then a concluding sentence that returns to the thesis in new words.

What does not change. Topic sentences still head the paragraphs. A quotation still needs an introduction and an explanation (Day 18). A concession is still a real limit, not a fake “some people say” you immediately dismiss without looking.

A school subject. Geometry, a Latin contrast, a science caution, or a literary design from this fortnight — not a new political essay on the Declaration. The Declaration remains a model of form when you need one.

Remember

Three jobs: thesis; one support paragraph with evidence; a close that concedes a real limit and returns to the thesis in new words. Not three unmatched blocks.

Key terms: three-paragraph outline.

Physics · Gravitational potential energy near the Earth

50 min · Method: PE = m g h as a first model with a chosen zero; g = 9.8 N/kg; honest limits

Learn

Key terms

gravitational potential energy (near Earth)
Definition
In this course’s first model, the gravitational potential energy of an object near the Earth, treated as a particle, is PE = m g h, where h is the height above a chosen reference level, g is 9.8 N/kg (or 9.8 m/s²), and PE is in joules. Only differences in PE are physically usable; the zero of h is a choice.
Origin
From Latin potentiālis, from potentia, ‘power’ (Wiktionary).
Example
A 2 kg object at 2.5 m above a chosen zero has PE = 2 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 49 J relative to that zero.
Not this
Not a claim about gravity far from the Earth (no 1/r formula today), and not a third kind of mass. The m is the same mass as in F = m a and in KE = ½ m v².
Connects to
Work against gravity, when you lift an object slowly without changing KE, equals the gain in PE: W = m g Δh (force m g along Δh, upward).
reference level
Definition
The height you choose as h = 0 when you write PE = m g h. Changing the reference changes the number you write for PE, but it does not change a difference PE₂ − PE₁ between two heights.
Example
If a shelf is 2.5 m above the floor, PE is 49 J for a 2 kg mass with the floor as zero, and 0 J if the shelf itself is the zero. The drop from shelf to floor still changes PE by −49 J.
Not this
Not a “true” zero required by nature in this model, and not a reason to mix two zeros in one calculation without converting.
Connects to
Pick one zero and keep it for the problem. The floor, the ground, or the lowest point of a path are common choices.

The model, and its limit. Near the Earth, over heights where g can be treated as constant (9.8 N/kg in this course), PE = m g h is the working formula. We will not pretend this is the most general gravitational energy, and we will not use a variable-g formula today.

A worked value. m = 2 kg, h = 2.5 m, g = 9.8 N/kg: PE = 2 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 49 J relative to h = 0.

Work and PE. To lift that mass slowly (ΔKE ≈ 0) through 2.5 m, you do 49 J of work against gravity, and PE increases by 49 J. If the object then falls, gravity does 49 J of work, and PE decreases by 49 J — energy that can appear as KE, next lesson, if nothing else takes it away.

g = 9.8, not 10, unless a problem says to approximate. This course has used 9.8 consistently.

Remember

PE = m g h near the Earth, with g = 9.8 N/kg and a chosen zero of height. Only differences in PE matter. The model is not a far-from-Earth formula. W against gravity, slowly, equals m g Δh.

Key terms: gravitational potential energy (near Earth), reference level.

  2. With the shelf as zero: h = 0, so PE = 0.
  3. The two numbers differ; a drop from shelf to floor still changes PE by −49 J in either description, once the same zero is used at both ends.

History · The Punic Wars, with Polybius as a source

45 min · Method: a standard chronology, then the source problem; no campaign-by-campaign filler

Learn

Key terms

Punic Wars
Definition
Three wars between Rome and Carthage: the First (264–241 BCE), the Second (218–201 BCE, including Hannibal’s invasion of Italy, Cannae in 216, and Zama in 202), and the Third (149–146 BCE), which ended with the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE. These dates are the standard scholarly chronology and are much better fixed than the traditional years of early Rome.
Example
The Second Punic War is the war of Hannibal and of Scipio; Zama (202 BCE) is the decisive battle in North Africa as the usual textbook marker of that war’s end, with the peace following in 201.
Not this
Not a fourth war, and not a story that has to be told only from a Roman point of view. Carthage was a major power of the western Mediterranean; the sources we have are still largely Greek and Roman.
Connects to
Polybius is a principal literary source, especially for the second war and for Rome’s rise; he is closer to these events than Livy is to the kings, and he still has aims.
Polybius (on the wars)
Definition
A Greek historian of the second century BCE who wrote a history of Rome’s rise, including the Punic Wars. He had Roman patrons and access, and he is an analyst as well as a narrator. Use him as a second-century source, not as a modern staff officer’s report.
Example
A battle account in Polybius is evidence that must still be weighed (distance from the event, sources he used, his argument about Rome’s success).
Not this
Not an eyewitness of every campaign he describes, and not a Carthaginian voice. For early Rome’s constitution, Day 23 already used him as a mid-Republican analyst.
Connects to
Livy also writes the wars, later. When the two differ, the disagreement is itself something to notice, not something to hide.

A better-documented conflict. Compared with the kings, the Punic Wars sit in a clearer chronological frame: 264–241, 218–201 (Cannae 216, Zama 202), 149–146, with Carthage destroyed in 146 BCE. This course will use those dates as the standard chronology.

What to hold, at this lesson’s scale. Rome and Carthage fought for power in the western Mediterranean. The first war was long and naval in large part. The second brought Hannibal into Italy and ended with a Roman victory in North Africa. The third was shorter and ended in the destruction of Carthage. Campaign-by-campaign narrative can wait; the frame and the source problem are today’s work.

The source problem, still in force. Polybius is central and is still a Greek writing with Roman access and an argument. Later Roman writers, including Livy, retell the wars. We have far less in a Carthaginian voice. Write accordingly: “in the Greco-Roman sources,” when that is what you mean.

Remember

Three wars: 264–241, 218–201 (Cannae 216, Zama 202), 149–146 (Carthage destroyed in 146). Dates here are much firmer than 753 or 509. Polybius is central and still an argued, Greek, second-century source. Carthaginian voices are scarce in what we have.

Key terms: Punic Wars, Polybius (on the wars).

Atelier · The eye, in the context of the head

90 min · Method: a feature in the already-blocked head or plaster; sockets and lids before a highlight

Learn

Key terms

eye in context
Definition
A study of an eye (or a pair of eyes) as it sits in the already-blocked head: the brow plane, the socket, the lids, and the value family of the head, not a floating almond drawn first and made “alive” with a highlight.
Example
An eye that sits in a socket, under a brow, on the center line, and in the correct light family is in context, even if the iris is only a quiet shape.
Not this
Not a finished iris and highlight on a head whose tilt is still wrong, and not a second, different head begun from an eye.
Connects to
This week adds features *to the head already begun*, as last Friday’s plan promised.

The order, still. Confirm the tilt, the center line, and the brow line. The socket is a shallow recess in the egg, not a sticker on the surface. The lids have thickness. The far eye, on a turned head, is foreshortened with the face.

Value. If the eye sits in the large shadow, it stays in the shadow family. A bright “wet” highlight that bleaches the socket will pull the eye off the head.

One sitting. Work the pair as a pair, even if one eye is more hidden. Do not finish the near eye and invent the far one later from memory.

Remember

Place the eyes as a pair in the socket and on the brow line of the existing head. Lids have thickness. The far eye foreshortens. A highlight is a late accent, and it must not bleach a shadowed socket.

Key terms: eye in context.

Modern language · Spanish: tener que, hay, and hay que

40 min · Method: three present-tense patterns, contrasted; tener already known (e → ie)

Learn

Key terms

tener que
Definition
A present-tense pattern that expresses obligation: tener que + infinitive, ‘to have to’ do something. The form of tener agrees with the person who is obliged.
Example
Tengo que estudiar. Tiene que repetir la frase. Tenemos que pedir agua.
Not this
Not hay que, which is impersonal (‘one has to / it is necessary to’) and does not change for yo or tú. And still not a past tense.
Connects to
You already have tener and its e → ie change (tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen). Today it is followed by que and an infinitive.
hay / hay que
Definition
hay means ‘there is / there are’ (present). hay que + infinitive means ‘one has to / it is necessary to,’ without naming a particular subject. Hay does not change for plural in this use: Hay un libro; Hay dos libros.
Example
Hay un libro en la mesa. Hay que practicar. Hay que pedir el libro.
Not this
Not *tienen que hay, and not *hayn. And not estoy for mere existence in the ‘there is’ sense taught today: the book’s location can use está, but ‘there is a book’ is hay un libro.
Connects to
Three tools: hay (existence), hay que + infinitive (impersonal obligation), tener que + infinitive (someone in particular has to).

Three short patterns.

  • Someone in particular: Tengo que / tienes que / tiene que / tenemos que… + infinitive.
  • No particular person: Hay que + infinitive.
  • Existence: Hay + a noun phrase. The same hay for one or many.

Original sentences: Tengo que pedir un libro. Hay que servir la comida. Hay una clase hoy. No hay agua. ¿Qué tienes que hacer?

Remember

tener que + infinitive: someone has to. hay que + infinitive: it is necessary to (impersonal). hay: there is / there are (same form for plural). Still the present.

Key terms: tener que, hay / hay que.

Logic · All, some, and none: a first look at A, E, I, and O

40 min · Method: four standard forms, then a first look at the square of opposition, with an existential assumption named

Learn

Key terms

categorical sentences (A, E, I, O)
Definition
Four standard forms of sentence about classes: A, “All S are P”; E, “No S are P”; I, “Some S are P”; O, “Some S are not P.” The letters are traditional names. “Some” here means “at least one,” not “some but not all.”
Example
All squares are rectangles (A). No square is a triangle (E). Some rectangles are squares (I). Some rectangles are not squares (O).
Not this
Not a full system of syllogisms, which this lesson does not teach. And not “some” in the everyday hint of “only a few.” If even one S is P, the I-sentence is true.
Connects to
Day 21’s conditionals are a different form (if–then). Today is about all / none / some, with a first look at which pairs can be true together.
square of opposition (first look)
Definition
A traditional diagram of how A, E, I, and O relate: A and O are contradictories (one true, the other false); E and I are contradictories; A and E are contraries (they cannot both be true, but both can be false); I and O are subcontraries (they cannot both be false, but both can be true), on the standard reading taught here, which assumes there is at least one S (an existential assumption this course will name rather than hide).
Example
“All rectangles are squares” (A, false) and “Some rectangles are not squares” (O, true) are contradictories. “All rectangles are squares” and “No rectangles are squares” (E, also false) are contraries: both false, which contraries allow.
Not this
Not a claim that we have proved every medieval or modern subtlety of the square, and not a license to ignore empty classes in a later course. If there were no S at all, some of these relations would need a more careful treatment, which we only flag today.
Connects to
Use the square to test consistency, not to invent a hidden third category of sentence.

Four forms, in geometry you already know.

  • A: All squares are rectangles. (true, in this course)
  • E: No squares are rectangles. (false)
  • I: Some squares are rectangles. (true — in fact all are, and “some” still holds)
  • O: Some squares are not rectangles. (false)

Contradictories. A and O cannot share a truth value: if A is true, O is false, and conversely. E and I likewise.

Contraries. A and E cannot both be true. They can both be false: “All rectangles are squares” and “No rectangles are squares” are both false.

A named assumption. The traditional square, as used today, treats “All S are P” as implying “Some S are P,” which requires that there is at least one S. We will not build arguments about empty classes in this lesson; we only name the assumption so it is not smuggled.

Remember

A All S are P; E No S are P; I Some S are P; O Some S are not P. “Some” means at least one. A and O, E and I are contradictories. A and E are contraries. The traditional square assumes there is at least one S.

Key terms: categorical sentences (A, E, I, O), square of opposition (first look).
