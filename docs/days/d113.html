Latin · Deponent present, imperfect, and perfect, mixed

40 min · Method: mixed practice of the three already-taught deponent tenses, with the absolute as a contrast

Learn

Key terms

deponent tenses, mixed
Definition
A mixed practice of the three deponent tenses already taught: present loquor, imperfect loquēbar, and perfect locūtus sum, still looking passive and translated as active, with the two already-taught absolutes kept distinct from the finite tenses.
Example
Puer loquitur. Puer loquēbātur. Puer locūtus est. Puerō locūtō, magister respondet.
Not this
Not a first look at any of those forms. Not a new deponent verb beyond loquor and cōnor. The first persons of sequor and ēgredior remain reserved.
Connects to
The present table, last week’s perfect, and yesterday’s imperfect.

No new tense is named today. The work is to choose among the three already-taught tenses, and not to treat an absolute as if it were a main-clause verb.

Word How to say it Meaning

loquēbātur loh-kway-BAH-toor he or she was speaking, or used to speak

cōnābar koh-NAH-bar I was trying, or I used to try

Remember

Present: loquor, I speak. Imperfect: loquēbar, I was speaking or used to speak. Perfect: locūtus sum, I spoke or have spoken. An absolute such as puerō locūtō is a loosely attached phrase, already taught, with no sum.

  1. Puer loquitur: present.
  2. Puer loquēbātur: imperfect, ongoing or habitual past.
  3. Puer locūtus est: perfect, completed.
  4. Puerō locūtō, magister respondet: absolute, not a fourth tense of the main verb.

Hint 2: The absolute

Cōnātō puerō is the already-taught absolute, “with the boy having tried,” not a main-clause perfect with sum.

Mathematics · Geometry LXV: tangent of complementary angles

40 min · Method: tan(90° − A) = 1 / tan A, from the already-taught side swap in a right triangle; degrees only

Learn

Key terms

complementary tangents
Definition
If A and B are the two acute angles of a right triangle, already known to add to 90°, then tan A = 1 / tan B, because the side opposite A is adjacent to B, and the side adjacent to A is opposite B. Equivalently, tan(90° − A) = 1 / tan A. This uses only the already-taught definition of tangent and the already-taught complementary pair. Degrees only.
Example
In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3, tan A = 3/4 and tan(90° − A) = 4/3, and (3/4) × (4/3) = 1.
Not this
Not a first look at sin(90° − A) = cos A, already taught last week. Not a reciprocal name such as cotangent, which remains reserved. Degrees only.
Connects to
Yesterday’s tangent, last week’s complementary sines and cosines, and the angle sum of a triangle.

Last week, sin(90° − A) = cos A, because the two acute angles of a right triangle add to 90°, already taught from the angle sum of a triangle, and the side opposite A is adjacent to 90° − A. The same swap of sides gives a fact about tangent.

If A is opposite 3 and adjacent to 4, then 90° − A is opposite 4 and adjacent to 3, so tan A = 3/4 and tan(90° − A) = 4/3. The two tangents multiply to 1, which is the same as saying tan(90° − A) = 1 / tan A. No new official measured angles are invented. Degrees only. A name for 1 / tan A remains reserved.

Remember

If A and 90° − A are the two acute angles of a right triangle, tan A and tan(90° − A) are reciprocals, because the opposite and adjacent sides swap. In a classroom 3-4-5 triangle, tan A = 3/4 and tan(90° − A) = 4/3. Degrees only.

Watch me

Watch the side swap, then the 3-4-5 numbers, then the product.

  1. The side opposite A is adjacent to 90° − A, and the side adjacent to A is opposite 90° − A.
  2. So tan A = 3/4 and tan(90° − A) = 4/3, in the classroom 3-4-5 triangle.
  3. The product is 1, so tan(90° − A) = 1 / tan A. This is not a first look at last week’s sine and cosine swap.

Together

Hint 1: Tan B

Opposite 5, adjacent 12, so tan B = 5/12.

Hint 2: The complement

Tan(90° − B) = 12/5, and (5/12) × (12/5) = 1.

Physics · Transverse and longitudinal waves, from OpenStax 16.9

40 min · Method: the already-started OpenStax 16.9 wave, now distinguished as transverse or longitudinal; official measured speeds not invented

Learn

Key terms

transverse and longitudinal waves
Definition
In OpenStax 16.9, a transverse wave, or shear wave, has its disturbance perpendicular to the direction of propagation, as in a wave on a cord, a guitar string, or visible light. A longitudinal wave, or compressional wave, has its disturbance parallel to the direction of propagation, as in sound in air or water. Amplitude is independent of the wave speed vw. Water waves are a combination of the two; the simplified water wave used on Monday showed no longitudinal motion of the bird. Sound in solids can be both. Earthquake waves under the surface have a longitudinal or P-wave component and a transverse or S-wave component, which OpenStax notes propagate at different speeds; official measured speeds are not invented here.
Example
A wave on a guitar string is transverse. A sound wave in air, which rattles a sheet of paper along the direction of travel, is longitudinal, as in OpenStax’s illustration.
Not this
Not a first look at λ or vw, already taught on Monday. Not superposition or interference, which remain reserved. Official measured earthquake-wave speeds are not invented.
Connects to
Monday’s wave, wavelength, and wave velocity, from the same OpenStax section.

Monday’s wave velocity still holds for both kinds of wave named today. The new distinction, from the same OpenStax section 16.9, is the direction of the disturbance relative to the direction of propagation.

Transverse, or shear: the disturbance is perpendicular to propagation. Waves on the strings of musical instruments are transverse, and so are electromagnetic waves such as visible light. Longitudinal, or compressional: the disturbance is parallel to propagation. Sound waves in air and water are longitudinal, because fluids do not have appreciable shear strength. Sound in solids can be both. Water waves are actually a combination; the simplified Monday figure showed no longitudinal motion of the bird. Amplitude X is independent of vw. Earthquake waves under Earth’s surface have both components, called compressional or P-waves and shear or S-waves, and OpenStax notes that they propagate at different speeds. Those official speeds are not given as numbers here and are not invented. Superposition remains reserved.

Remember

A transverse wave has its disturbance perpendicular to propagation; a longitudinal wave has its disturbance parallel to propagation. A guitar string is transverse; sound in air is longitudinal. Amplitude is not vw. Official P-wave and S-wave speeds are not invented.

Watch me

Watch one transverse case, one longitudinal case, then the combination warning.

  1. A wave on a cord: the wave travels horizontally, the cord is disturbed vertically — transverse.
  2. Sound in air: the disturbance is a pressure variation along the direction of travel — longitudinal.
  3. A water wave is a combination. Do not invent an official earthquake-wave speed because the two components “propagate at different speeds.”

Together

Hint 1: The string

Hint 2: Sound in air

The disturbance is a pressure variation parallel to the direction of travel, so the wave is longitudinal. Fluids lack appreciable shear strength.

Modern language · Spanish: era and fui, contrasted in use

40 min · Method: already-taught era and fui (and fue), chosen in short invented sentences, with iba kept distinct; instruction in English

Learn

Key terms

era and fui, contrasted in use
Definition
Ser already has two taught past forms that must not be treated as synonyms: fui, the completed preterite, and era, the imperfect. Ir shares fui with ser, but iba is not a form of ser. Today those already-taught forms are chosen in short invented sentences. Instruction remains in English.
Example
Ayer fui el último en llegar is avoided if it forces an identifying scene; prefer: En ese año, el mercado era grande. Un día, el mercado fue cerrado at an invented one-time close, or simply: Entonces era de día. Ayer fui al mercado still uses fui as ir.
Not this
Not a first look at era or fui as forms. Not a claim that ir and ser share an imperfect. Not vía for veía.
Connects to
Last week’s era and iba, the already-taught fui, and yesterday’s tense choice.

Fui can be “I went” or “I was,” already taught, because ir and ser share that preterite. Era is only “I was” or “I used to be,” the imperfect of ser. Iba is only “I was going” or “I used to go.” Today the choice is practiced in short invented sentences, still in English as the language of instruction.

Word How to say it Meaning

hablaba ah-BLAH-bah I was speaking, or I used to speak

El mercado era grande: background or description, imperfect. Un día el mercado fue distinto, if the change is presented as a completed event, uses the preterite of ser, fue, already available from the fui table (fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fuisteis, fueron). Cada mañana iba is still ir. Do not invent identifying characters.

Remember

Era describes or holds a background: the market was large. Fue (or fui) presents a completed state or event of being, or, with ir, a completed going. Iba is not a form of ser. Instruction is in English.

Watch me

Watch a description, a completed event of being, and a going, all with already-taught forms.

  1. El mercado era grande: imperfect of ser, description.
  2. Un día el mercado fue distinto: preterite of ser, a completed change, invented.
  3. Ayer fui al mercado: preterite of ir, completed going. Cada mañana iba: imperfect of ir, not of ser.
