Mathematics · Geometry LXXIII: 30°, 45°, and 60° on the unit circle, a first look

40 min · Method: scale the already-taught 45° and 30°-60° triangles so the hypotenuse is 1

Learn

Key terms

sine and cosine of 30°, 45°, and 60° on the unit circle
Definition
The already-taught 45° isosceles right triangle, and the 30°-60° triangle from half an equilateral triangle, are scaled so that the hypotenuse is 1, and then placed on the unit circle. At 45°, P is (√2/2, √2/2). At 30°, P is (√3/2, 1/2). At 60°, P is (1/2, √3/2). These are exact values, not official measured lengths of a real object.
Example
Sin 30° = 1/2, cos 30° = √3/2; sin 45° = cos 45° = √2/2; sin 60° = √3/2, cos 60° = 1/2.
Not this
Not a decimal approximation treated as an official replacement of the exact value. Not a first look at radians. Not a new triangle; the triangles were already taught, and today they are scaled to hypotenuse 1.
Connects to
The unit circle, and last term’s right-triangle sine and cosine, including tan of an acute angle, which may be checked as sin/cos but is not a new first look today.

The 45° isosceles right triangle, already taught, has equal legs. If the legs are 1, the hypotenuse is √2. To place it on the unit circle, scale so the hypotenuse is 1: each leg becomes 1/√2, which is √2/2. So at 45°, P is (√2/2, √2/2): cos 45° = sin 45° = √2/2.

A 30°-60°-90° triangle comes from half an equilateral triangle of side 2, already used in this form: the side opposite 30° is 1, the hypotenuse is 2, and the side opposite 60° is √3. Scale so the hypotenuse is 1, dividing by 2. At 30°, adjacent is √3/2 and opposite is 1/2, so P is (√3/2, 1/2). At 60°, P is (1/2, √3/2). Radians remain reserved. These are exact values, not official measurements of a real object.

Remember

At 45°, P is (√2/2, √2/2). At 30°, P is (√3/2, 1/2). At 60°, P is (1/2, √3/2). Scale the already-taught triangles to hypotenuse 1; do not treat a decimal as a replacement of the exact value.

  3. So P is (√2/2, √2/2). Cos 45° = sin 45° = √2/2.
  4. Do not replace √2/2 by an official decimal and call that the exact value.

Yesterday’s original narrative is now tightened. Keep the same action if it already works. Restore the order if an event was told too soon. Cut a stated moral, and cut a mid-story lecture. Make when and where easy to find, without a long setting. The ink-jar model may be a contrast, not a story to copy today.

Remember

Shorten, restore the order, keep when and where, and cut a moral or a lecture.

Watch me

A loose draft, then a tighter one, on the invented window.

  1. Loose: Papers matter, so one should close a window, and late in the afternoon a window is open, and then the papers lift, which shows that care is wise, and then the window is closed.

Physics · Intensity under constructive interference, from OpenStax Example 16.10

40 min · Method: I is proportional to amplitude squared, so a doubled amplitude gives four times the intensity

Learn

Key terms

intensity under constructive interference
Definition
In OpenStax Example 16.10, intensity is proportional to amplitude squared. If two identical waves interfere perfectly constructively, the amplitude doubles, so the intensity becomes four times as great, not two times. For two waves each of intensity 1.00 W/m², the resulting intensity at that location is 4.00 W/m². Energy is not created: the area of 4.00 W/m² is smaller than the area the two waves covered before, and there are other places where the intensity is zero.
Example
The book’s official pair: 1.00 W/m² + 1.00 W/m², perfectly constructive, gives 4.00 W/m² at that location, not 2.00 W/m².
Not this
Not a simple sum of the two intensities, 2.00 W/m², which would ignore that intensity follows amplitude squared. Not a claim that energy appears from nowhere.
Connects to
Monday’s I = P/A, last week’s pure constructive interference (amplitude 2X), and the already-taught idea that most superpositions are mixed.

OpenStax Example 16.10 applies Monday’s intensity to last week’s constructive interference. Two identical waves, each of intensity 1.00 W/m², interfere perfectly constructively. The resulting amplitude is 2X, and because I is proportional to amplitude squared, I′ = 4I = 4.00 W/m², not 2.00 W/m². That can look as if energy has appeared, but it has not: the area over which the intensity is 4.00 W/m² is much less than the area the two waves covered before they interfered, and there are other areas where the intensity is zero. An invented practice pair, labeled invented: two identical waves of 2.00 W/m², perfectly constructive, give 8.00 W/m² at that location. Decibels remain reserved. Invent no official living sound-level claim from these figures.

Remember

If the amplitude doubles, the intensity becomes four times as great. Example 16.10: 1.00 W/m² and 1.00 W/m², perfectly constructive, give 4.00 W/m², not 2.00 W/m². Energy is not created.

Watch me

In Example 16.10, why is the result 4.00 W/m², not 2.00 W/m², and why does that not create energy?

  1. Each wave has intensity 1.00 W/m². Perfect constructive interference doubles the amplitude.
  2. Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so I′ = 4I = 4.00 W/m², not 2.00 W/m².
  3. The 4.00 W/m² is only over a smaller area; elsewhere the intensity can be zero. Energy is rearranged, not created.

Together

For an invented pair of identical waves, each 2.00 W/m², perfectly constructive, what is I′ at that location, and why is 4.00 W/m² a miss?

Hint 1: Four times

I′ = 4 × 2.00 = 8.00 W/m².

Hint 2: The miss

4.00 W/m² is the book’s official example, which started from 1.00 W/m², or a mistaken doubling of 2.00.

History · Inter Caetera, May 4: the proviso, and the birthday from which 1493 begins

40 min · Method: the possession proviso in the May 4 clause, in the bull’s own wording, not a later gloss only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Inter Caetera (May 4, 1493) — Day 126

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Monday’s line is now followed by the proviso in the same grant. In Davenport’s wording:

With this proviso however that none of the islands and mainlands, found and to be found, discovered and to be discovered, beyond that said line towards the west and south, be in the actual possession of any Christian king or prince up to the birthday of our Lord Jesus Christ just past from which the present year one thousand four hundred and ninety-three begins.

The lands beyond the line, towards the west and south, are granted provided they are not “in the actual possession of any Christian king or prince up to the birthday of our Lord Jesus Christ just past from which the present year one thousand four hundred and ninety-three begins.” A later sentence adds that no right acquired by any Christian prince in actual possession prior to that same birthday is to be understood as withdrawn. The bull’s own wording for the date is that birthday from which 1493 begins, not a later short name standing alone. Do not convert a hundred leagues into an official modern measurement. The Treaty of Tordesillas and the first-voyage letter remain reserved. A later clause also forbids anyone, even imperial and royal, to go to those lands for trade or any other reason without a special permit from the rulers of Castile; that prohibition can be noticed, but the first job today is the proviso and its date.

Remember

The May 4 proviso uses the bull’s wording: not in actual possession of any Christian king or prince up to the birthday from which 1493 begins. A later sentence keeps a right already acquired before that birthday from being withdrawn.

Seminar · Art history: Dewing, Buildings I.3.6–9, the shrine at Pegê

40 min · Method: close reading of Dewing I.3.6–9; Blachernae already taught; Heraeum reserved

Learn

Key terms

the shrine at Pegê
Definition
In Dewing, Buildings I.3.6–9, the 1940 Loeb translation as posted on LacusCurtius, Justinian “dedicated to the Virgin another shrine in the place called Pegê.” The surroundings are a dense grove of cypresses, a flowering meadow, a park of shrubs, and “a spring bubbling silently forth with a gentle stream of sweet water.” Of the church itself Dewing says “it surpasses most shrines both in beauty and in size.” Both this church and last week’s church at Blachernae were erected outside the city-wall, “in order that both of them may serve as invincible defences to the circuit-wall of the city.” Invent no official length, and no official shrine count.
Example

Not this
Not last week’s church at Blachernae as if it were this second shrine. Not the Heraeum, which begins at I.3.10 and remains reserved. Not an official modern measurement of the church or of the spring.
Connects to
Last week’s Dewing I.3.1–5, the churches of the Mother of God, and the church at Blachernae.

Art history continues Dewing, Buildings I.3, in the 1940 Loeb translation by H. B. Dewing, as posted on LacusCurtius. Last week ended at I.3.5, the church at Blachernae. Today I.3.6–9, the reserved next section, at Pegê. I.3.6: “He dedicated to the Virgin another shrine in the place called Pegê.” Then: “In that place is a dense grove of cypresses and a meadow abounding in flowers in the midst of soft glebe, a park abounding in beautiful shrubs, and a spring bubbling silently forth with a gentle stream of sweet water — all especially suitable to a sanctuary.” I.3.7: “Such are the surroundings of the sanctuary.” Of the church itself, I.3.7–8: “the church itself is not easy to describe in such terms as it deserves, nor can one readily form a mental vision of it, nor do it justice in whispering speech. The Heraeum, from I.3.10, remains reserved. Invent no official length, and no official shrine count. British spelling inside Dewing’s wording, such as “defences,” stays inside the quotation.

Dewing I.3.6–9 describes another shrine of the Virgin at Pegê, in a cypress grove by a silent spring, a church that “surpasses most shrines both in beauty and in size,” outside the wall near the Golden Gate, with Blachernae by the sea. Invent no official length.
