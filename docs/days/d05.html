Latin · Capitulum II, part 2: cuius? and the genitive in use

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. II

Learn

Key terms

cuius
Definition
The question word ‘whose?’, asking for a possessor. The adjective that answers (meus, mea, meum) agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner.
Origin
Latin cuius, the genitive of quis/quid.
Example
Cuius est vīlla? Vīlla est mea. Mea is feminine because vīlla is feminine, even if the owner is not.
Not this
Mea does not agree with the owner’s gender. That is the usual English mistake.
Connects to
This is the genitive idea from Day 4, now in a live question-and-answer. Gender agreement (Day 2) still holds for mea.
possessive adjective
Definition
An adjective such as meus, mea, meum (‘my’) that shows ownership and agrees with the noun it describes.
Example
Vīlla est mea; oppidum est meum.
Not this
It is not a genitive noun ending, and it does not stay in one form for every noun.
Connects to
Agreement and gender, already taught, are the reason mea and meum both exist.

Cuius? means 'whose?'. Possession in the early chapters is often shown by this question and by a genitive form (a possessive ending) that you will see in the book. Learn the question and a few family words, then read.

Notice nouns in -er: puer, puerī (boy) keeps its e; vir, virī (man). They are second-declension masculine nouns, like servus, with the same endings after the nominative singular.

Remember

Cuius est? — whose is it? Meus / mea agree with the thing owned, not with the owner.

Mathematics · Algebra II: quadratic equations

60 min · Gelfand & Shen, Algebra

Learn

Key terms

completing the square
Definition
Rewriting x² + px as a square plus or minus a constant, so a quadratic can be solved or a parabola’s vertex read off.
Example
x² − 8x + 12 = 0 becomes (x − 4)² = 4, so x = 6 or x = 2.
Not this
It is not the same as factoring by inspection, though a completed square can be checked with factors or with Vieta.
Connects to
This is the method; the quadratic formula is what you get by completing the square on ax² + bx + c = 0.
discriminant D = b² − 4ac
Definition
The quantity that decides how many real roots a quadratic has: two if D > 0, one if D = 0, none if D < 0.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin discrīmināns, from discriminare (‘to distinguish’).
Example
For x² − 4x + 5 = 0, D = 16 − 20 = −4, so there are no real roots.
Not this
The discriminant is not the same as the vertex, and a negative D does not mean ‘the equation is illegal’; it means the roots are not real.
Connects to
Completing the square produces D under the square root, which is why its sign matters.
Vieta's formulas r + s = −p, rs = q
Definition
For the roots r and s of x² + px + q = 0, the sum of the roots is −p and the product is q. (Named after François Viète.)
Example
If the roots of x² − 8x + 12 = 0 are 6 and 2, then 6 + 2 = 8 and 6 · 2 = 12.
Not this
Vieta does not find the roots by itself if you do not already have them or an equivalent fact; it checks them and can build an equation from known roots.
Connects to
After completing the square, use Vieta as a check, not as a substitute for the method.

A quadratic equation can be written ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0. Some quadratics factor: x² − 5x + 6 = (x−2)(x−3) = 0, so x = 2 or x = 3. Others need completing the square, or the quadratic formula, which comes from completing the square on the general equation. Always check roots in the original equation.

Completing the square is the method; the formula is its result. For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0:

x² + (b/a)x = −c/a ⇒ (x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/4a² ⇒ x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a.

The discriminant D = b² − 4ac decides the number of real roots: two if D > 0, one (double) if D = 0, none if D < 0.

Vieta's formulas. If r and s are the roots of x² + px + q = 0, then x² + px + q = (x − r)(x − s), so r + s = −p and rs = q. Use them to check answers and to work with roots without finding them.

Worked example. Solve x² − 6x + 5 = 0 by completing the square: (x − 3)² = 4, so x − 3 = ±2, x = 5 or 1. Check with Vieta: 5 + 1 = 6 = −(−6) ✓; 5 · 1 = 5 ✓.

Remember

Bring to ax² + bx + c = 0. Factor if you can. Otherwise complete the square or use x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / (2a). Check by substitution.

Key terms: completing the square, discriminant, Vieta's formulas.

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium I, grammar: the anatomy of a sentence

45 min · Method: the Trivium (grammar → logic → rhetoric). Grammar here means knowing precisely how sentences are built.

Learn

Key terms

grammar
Definition
In the trivium, the craft of accurate sentences: parts of speech, agreement, and punctuation that make the structure visible.
Origin
From Old French gramaire, from Latin grammatica, from Ancient Greek γραμματική, from γράμμα (‘letter’).
Example
In ‘The students who finished the plate left the studio,’ the subject of the main clause is The students; who finished the plate is a relative clause.
Not this
Grammar here is not a list of errors to fear, and it is not ‘style’. It is how you mark what a sentence is doing.
Connects to
Kernel, appositive, and subordinate clause are already grammar, named so they can be marked in a real sentence.
clause
Definition
A group of words with a subject and a verb. A main clause can stand as a sentence; a subordinate clause cannot.
Origin
From Old French clause, from Latin clausa, from claudere (‘to close, conclude’).
Example
Main clause: The students left the studio. Subordinate: who finished the plate.
Not this
A clause is not a phrase (a phrase has no finite verb), and it is not automatically a sentence.
Connects to
Day 3 distinguished appositives (phrases) from subordinate clauses. Today you mark both in a full sentence.

Grammar, in the trivium, is the craft of accurate sentences: parts of speech, agreement, and punctuation that makes the structure visible. Today's work names what a sentence is doing, so later logic and rhetoric have something solid to stand on. Mark the subject and the verb before you polish anything else.

The three arts. In the medieval liberal arts, the trivium was grammar (the structure of language), logic or dialectic (valid reasoning) and rhetoric (effective persuasion). This course teaches them in that order and then keeps all three running together.

Sentence anatomy. - Subject (who or what) and predicate (what is said about it). - Direct object (receives the action: Franklin bought a volume); indirect object (gave me the book); subject complement after a linking verb (the writing was excellent). - Phrase: a group of words without its own subject–verb pair (in the morning). Clause: has a subject and a verb. Independent clauses can stand alone; dependent clauses cannot. - Simple sentence: one independent clause. Compound: two or more independent clauses. Complex: an independent clause plus at least one dependent clause.

Remember

Find the subject and the finite verb first. Check agreement. Fix punctuation so that clauses are visible. Do not start with 'style'.

Key terms: grammar, clause.

Watch me

Sentence: The students who finished the plate left the studio. Subject of the main clause: The students. Verb: left. Who finished the plate is a relative clause describing the students, not a second main sentence.

Together

Hint 1: Subordinate

Hint 2: Main

Physics · Free fall

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Key terms

free fall g ≈ 9.8 m/s², downward
Definition
Motion under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. Near Earth, the acceleration has size g and is directed downward, the same for all objects.
Example
A stone dropped from rest falls with a ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward. After 2 s its speed is about 19.6 m/s, if air is ignored.
Not this
Free fall is not ‘only motion going down’: a ball thrown upward is still in free fall after it leaves the hand, with the same downward acceleration.
Connects to
This is a constant acceleration (Day 3), with a special value and direction given by gravity.
gravity g
Definition
The attraction that gives a free-falling object its downward acceleration g near Earth. In these lessons g is taken as about 9.8 m/s², downward.
Origin
From Latin gravitās (‘weight, heaviness’), from gravis (‘heavy’). The physical-quality sense in English is from the 17th century.
Example
Two objects of different mass, dropped together in free fall (air ignored), have the same g.
Not this
g is not a force, and it is not the same as mass. A larger mass is pulled more strongly, but it also resists acceleration more, so a is the same.
Connects to
Free fall is the motion; g is the acceleration that describes it. Force and mass are named carefully on Day 8.

Near Earth's surface, if air resistance is small enough to ignore, all bodies fall with the same downward acceleration, about g = 9.8 m/s² (often taken as 10 m/s² in a quick estimate). The motion is uniformly accelerated: v = u + at, and s = ut + ½at², with a = g downward. Mass does not appear in these equations, which is the point of the famous (if simplified) falling-bodies claim.

Pre-reading. Without air resistance, all objects near Earth's surface fall with the same acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward, regardless of mass. Galileo argued this from experiments with inclined planes and thought experiments; on the Moon in 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together, and they landed at the same moment. Falling from rest: d = ½gt², v = gt.

ConcepTests

  1. A heavy ball and a light ball of the same size are dropped together from the same height; air resistance is negligible. (A) The heavy one lands first (B) The light one lands first (C) They land together.
  2. One ball is dropped and another is thrown horizontally from the same height at the same moment. (A) The dropped ball lands first (B) The thrown ball lands first (C) They land together.
  3. A ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s (no air resistance). When it returns to the thrower's hand its speed is: (A) less than 12 m/s (B) 12 m/s (C) more than 12 m/s.

Problems

  1. A stone is dropped from a 20 m bridge. How long does it fall, and how fast is it moving when it hits the water?
  2. A ball is thrown up at 15 m/s. Find its maximum height and the time to reach it.
  3. Experiment: reaction time. Have someone hold a ruler vertically with its zero end between your open fingers, then drop it without warning. Catch it, and read the distance d it fell. Do it five times. Your reaction time is t = √(2d/g). If d = 15 cm, what is t?

Remember

g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward (≈ 10 m/s² for estimates). v = u + gt (downward taken positive if you choose that). Mass cancels: heavy and light bodies fall together in this model.

Key terms: free fall, gravity.

Watch me

A stone is dropped from rest (u = 0). How fast is it moving after 2 s, taking g = 10 m/s²? How far has it fallen?

  1. v = 0 + 10×2 = 20 m/s downward.
  2. s = 0 + ½×10×2² = 5×4 = 20 m.

Together

The same stone, still dropped from rest, after 3 s (g = 10 m/s²). Find v and s.

Hint 1: Speed

v = 10×3 = 30 m/s downward.

Hint 2: Distance

s = ½×10×9 = 45 m.

History · Greece: Herodotus and the idea of "inquiry"

45 min · Primary source: Herodotus, Histories, opening sentence (G. C. Macaulay translation, 1890, public domain)

Learn

Key terms

inquiry
Definition
A systematic asking of questions in order to find out and to explain, not only to list events. Herodotus’s word historiē means inquiry.
Origin
From Middle English enquery, from Old French enquerre, from Latin inquīrere, from in- + quaerere (‘to ask, seek’). Later respelled to match the Latin.
Example
Herodotus states that his inquiry is so that deeds are not forgotten, so that great works of Greeks and non-Greeks keep their fame, and especially so that the causes of the war are remembered.
Not this
Inquiry is not the same as a chronicle (a list of what happened) and not the same as a royal inscription that commands and praises.
Connects to
History as a word (from historiē) is this habit of inquiry. You still source and corroborate (Days 1 and 3).
historiē
Definition
The Greek word (ἱστορίη) meaning inquiry, investigation. It is the source of the English word history.
Origin
Ancient Greek ἱστορίᾱ (‘learning through research’), from ἱστορεῖν (‘to research, inquire and record’); English history comes through Latin historia and Old French estoire.
Example
The opening of Herodotus: ‘This is the Showing forth of the Inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassos…’
Not this
It does not yet mean ‘a school subject’ or ‘everything that happened’. In Herodotus it is the investigation itself.
Connects to
The last purpose in his sentence — the causes of the war — is what turns inquiry into historical explanation.

Context. Herodotus of Halicarnassus (born in the 480s BCE, died after about 430 BCE) wrote an account of the wars between the Greeks and the Persian Empire (490 BCE and 480–479 BCE) and of the peoples involved. The Greek word historiē means inquiry. Cicero later called him "the father of history". He traveled, interviewed people and reported what he was told, often saying when he did not believe it.

The source.

This is the Showing forth of the Inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassos, to the end that neither the deeds of men may be forgotten by lapse of time, nor the works great and marvellous, which have been produced some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians, may lose their renown; and especially that the causes may be remembered for which these waged war with one another.

Remember

Herodotus's word historiē means inquiry. He states three purposes: that deeds not be forgotten; that great works of Greeks and non-Greeks keep their fame; and, especially, that the causes of the war be remembered. A history that asks why is different from a chronicle, and from a royal inscription that commands and praises.

Key terms: inquiry, historiē.

Watch me

Reading the opening sentence for purpose, as a model.

  1. Three purposes, in order: memory of deeds; fame of great works; and, 'especially', the causes of the war.
  2. 'Works great and marvellous… some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians' gives both sides credit. Barbaroi means non-Greek speakers; the sentence does not treat their works as inferior.
  3. The last purpose is what makes this inquiry, not a list: causes have to be explained and weighed.

Hint 2: Source class

Atelier · Bargue plate: modeling and completion

90 min

Learn

Key terms

modeling
Definition
Using value to turn a shape so that it reads as a form in light, not as a flat cut-out.
Origin
From model (from Middle French, ultimately from Latin modulus, ‘measure’) + -ing; in studio use it means working the form, not making a miniature.
Example
Once the contour is true, you place the large lights and darks, then the halftones, so a limb looks rounded.
Not this
Modeling is not coloring in, and it is not adding outlines. If the values are wrong, more line will not fix the form.
Connects to
The nine-step scale (Day 4) is the range you use. The Bargue plate is finished only when the form reads.
form
Definition
The three-dimensional shape of the subject, as it is turned by light and as it must read on a flat page.
Origin
From Old French forme, from Latin forma (‘shape, form’).
Example
A correctly modeled shoulder looks as if it turns away from the light, even though the paper is flat.
Not this
Form is not the same as outline, and it is not ‘style’.
Connects to
Sight-size and contour get the shape in place; modeling makes the form visible.

Modeling means turning the flat contour into a form that looks solid, by shading in values you have already trained on the scale. Start with the big light and the big shadow, then the terminator (the edge where light turns to shadow), then only a few darker accents. Do not polish a cheek before the large light is in the right place.

Modeling the form. With the shadow shapes in place as a flat tone, model in the light side: the halftones, which darken gradually as the surface turns away from the light, reaching their darkest just at the terminator. Keep the shadow side simple and unified: darks in the shadow are fewer and quieter than they look. Reserve the lightest lights (the paper) for the planes facing the light most directly.

Order of work: (1) darken the core of the shadow near the terminator; (2) lay the darkest halftones; (3) work outward toward the light; (4) soften edges where the form turns gradually, and keep them sharp where it turns abruptly.

Remember

Big light, big shadow, then the terminator. Match values to the scale by squinting. Leave the smallest darks until last.

Key terms: modeling, form.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Squint at the subject (or plate) until you see only two big regions: light and shadow. Map those two on the drawing with a light gray for the shadow family.
  2. Find the terminator and soften or sharpen it as the form requires (a sudden turn is a sharper edge; a gentle turn is softer).
  3. Compare a mid-gray to step 5 on your scale. Adjust.
  4. Add a few darker accents last, never first.

Together

Why squint when you start the shading?

Hint 1: Squinting

It hides small patches and leaves the two big families of value.

Hint 2: Use

You can then place the large shadow without fussing over pores and nicks.

Modern language · Spanish: present tense of -ar verbs

25 min

Learn

Key terms

-ar verb
Definition
A Spanish verb whose infinitive ends in -ar. In the present tense it follows a regular pattern of endings once the -ar is removed.
Example
Hablar: yo hablo, tú hablas, ella habla, nosotros hablamos.
Not this
The infinitive hablar is not a present-tense form, and hablarse is not needed for these first sentences.
Connects to
Ser (Day 3) was an irregular verb of identity. Regular -ar verbs are a pattern you can reuse.
infinitive
Definition
The dictionary form of the verb (in Spanish, the form ending in -ar, -er, or -ir), naming the action without a subject.
Origin
From Latin īnfīnītīvus, from īn- + fīnītus (‘bounded, limited’): the form not limited to a person.
Example
Hablar means ‘to speak’. Hablo means ‘I speak’.
Not this
The infinitive is not the same as the yo form, even when they look close.
Connects to
Once you can spot the infinitive ending, you can tell -ar verbs from the -er and -ir verbs of Day 8.

Regular -ar verbs in the present follow one pattern. Learn hablar (to speak) as the model: hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan. The ending tells you who is speaking; you often do not need a pronoun.

Word How to say it Meaning
hablar ah-BLAR to speak
hablo AH-bloh I speak
hablas AH-blahs you speak (tú)
habla AH-blah he/she speaks
hablamos ah-BLAH-mohs we speak
hablan AH-blahn they speak
estudiar es-too-DYAR to study
dibujar dee-boo-HAR to draw

hablar (to speak): hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan. Drop -ar and add -o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an. Subject pronouns are usually left out, because the ending shows the person: Hablo español. Negate with no before the verb: No hablo alemán.

Useful -ar verbs: estudiar, dibujar, pintar, trabajar, escuchar, mirar, practicar, necesitar.

Elective · Artistic anatomy 1: the skeleton under the skin

30 min · Seminar rotation: Elective (Fridays). See the Electives page for the full list; this term's Friday sessions begin with artistic anatomy and poetry.

Learn

Key terms

subcutaneous landmark
Definition
A place where bone lies just under the skin and does not change shape with fat or muscle, so it is a reliable measuring point in figure drawing.
Origin
Subcutaneous is from Latin sub (‘under’) + cutis (‘skin’). Landmark is from land + mark, a visible point of reference.
Example
The olecranon (the point of the elbow) and the malleoli (the ankle bones) can be found by touch and then marked on a standing figure.
Not this
A landmark is not a soft tissue bulge that moves when the person gains or loses weight, and it is not a guessed ‘middle of the arm’.
Connects to
Sight-size and proportion (atelier) need points that stay put. Anatomy supplies those points.
clavicle
Definition
The collarbone: a subcutaneous landmark running from the sternum to the point of the shoulder (near the acromion).
Origin
From Latin clāvicula, diminutive of clāvis (‘key’), because of the bone’s shape.
Example
The jugular notch lies between the two clavicles at the base of the throat.
Not this
The clavicle is not the jaw, and the acromion is not the same as the whole clavicle: it is the bony tip of the shoulder blade that meets it.
Connects to
Landmarks are learned by touch on your own body, then marked on a simple standing figure.

Why artists study bones. Where bone lies just under the skin, it fixes the form: these subcutaneous landmarks do not change shape with fat or muscle, so they are the reliable measuring points of figure drawing.

Key landmarks. The clavicles (collarbones) and the jugular notch between them; the acromion at the point of the shoulder; the spinous process of the seventh cervical vertebra (the prominent bump at the base of the neck); the sternum; the olecranon (point of the elbow); the iliac crest and the anterior superior iliac spine at the front of the hip; the patella; the malleoli (ankle bones, the inner one higher than the outer).

Proportion of the head. In an adult head seen from the front, the eyes lie at about half the height of the head (chin to top of the skull), lower than beginners usually place them.

Remember

Subcutaneous landmarks (bone just under the skin) are the reliable measuring points of a figure: clavicles and the jugular notch, acromion, seventh cervical vertebra, sternum, olecranon, iliac crest and ASIS, patella, malleoli (inner higher than outer). In an adult head from the front, the eyes lie at about half the height from chin to crown — lower than beginners usually place them.

Key terms: subcutaneous landmark, clavicle.

Watch me

Why the malleoli matter, and where the eyes go, as a model. (You still find each landmark by touch on your own body.)

  1. The inner ankle bone sits higher than the outer, so the line joining them slopes down toward the outside. That line shows the direction of the foot and the axis of the ankle.
  2. Beginners often place the eyes too high. From chin to the top of the skull, the eyes are at about half — a measurement, not a guess.
  3. These points do not drift with fat or muscle, so they can be marked on a simple standing figure and used again tomorrow.
