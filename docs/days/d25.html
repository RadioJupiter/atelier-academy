Latin · Capitulum VII: hic, haec, hoc, and a Friday check

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. VII · Friday check of the week’s skills · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

hic, haec, hoc
Definition
The near demonstrative: ‘this’ (near the speaker), introduced in Capitulum VII. You will meet the full set of forms over this week and next; today you learn a first working set: hic (masc. nom.), haec (fem. nom.), hoc (neuter nom./acc.), and the accusatives hunc, hanc.
Origin
From Latin dēmōnstrātīvus (Wiktionary).
Example
Hic puer est Mārcus. Haec puella est Iūlia. Hoc est oppidum.
Not this
hic the pronoun is not the adverb hīc (‘here’), which has a long ī. And ‘this’ is not yet ‘that’ (ille), which arrives in Capitulum VIII.
Connects to
English uses one word ‘this’ for all genders. Latin changes the form to agree with the noun.
Word How to say it Meaning
hic hik this (masculine nominative)
haec hike this (feminine nominative)
hoc hok this (neuter nominative or accusative)
hunc hunk this (masculine accusative)
hanc hank this (feminine accusative)
hīc heek here (adverb, long ī — not the pronoun)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

A first set, not the whole table. Point to what is near: hic puer, haec puella, hoc oppidum. For objects already in play: Mārcus hunc puerum videt; Iūlia hanc rosam dat Mārcō.

Original sentences: Hic puer in hortō est. Haec puella rosam habet. Mārcus hunc librum Iūliae dat. Hoc nōn est oppidum, sed vīlla.

This Friday also checks the dative, sē, and in + acc./abl. from the week. Close the notes and write from memory first.

Remember

hic / haec / hoc = this (near). hunc / hanc are the masc./fem. accusatives. hīc with a long ī means ‘here.’ This week’s other skills: dative -ae/-ō/-īs; sē vs eum/eam; in + acc. vs in + abl.

Mathematics · Algebra VII: the sum of an arithmetic progression

60 min · Method: pairing first with last (a pictorial Gauss pairing), then the formula; nth term from Day 20

Learn

Key terms

sum of an arithmetic progression
Definition
The sum of the first n terms of an arithmetic progression with first term a and last term ℓ is Sn = n/2 · (a + ℓ). Equivalently, Sn = n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d), where d is the common difference. Day 20 already defined the progression and the nth term; today adds the sum.
Example
The first ten terms of 3, 7, 11, … have last term 3 + 9×4 = 39, and sum 10/2 × (3 + 39) = 210.
Not this
This is not the nth term itself, and it is not the sum of a geometric progression (next week). Do not add n × the middle term unless n is odd and you know the middle term, or unless you are really using n times the average of the first and last, which is the same formula.
Connects to
A pictorial reason: pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on. Each pair sums to a + ℓ. There are n/2 such pairs (if n is odd, the middle term is the average, and the same formula still holds).

Day 20 defined an arithmetic progression: each term after the first is found by adding the same common difference d. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d.

The sum, by pairing. Write the sum forwards and backwards:

S = a + (a+d) + … + ℓ

S = ℓ + (ℓ−d) + … + a

Adding, 2S = n(a + ℓ), so S = n/2 · (a + ℓ).

If you do not yet know ℓ, use ℓ = a + (n − 1)d, and S = n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d).

A picture. Ten terms 3, 7, 11, …, 39. Five pairs each summing to 42, and 5 × 42 = 210.

Worked examples you will use: first ten of 3, 7, 11, … = 210; first eight of 5, 9, 13, … : last term 5+7×4=33, S=4×(5+33)=152; twenty terms with a=6, d=3: S=10×(12+19×3)=10×(12+57)=690.

Remember

Sn = n/2 · (first + last), or n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d). Pairing is the reason. This is a sum, not the nth term alone.

Key terms: sum of an arithmetic progression.

Writing & Rhetoric · Précis of the week’s model

45 min · Method: a one- or two-sentence précis (The Writing Revolution / week-3 skill) of a public-domain model

Learn

Key terms

précis
Definition
A short complete statement, in your own words, of the meaning (and, if asked, of the structure) of a passage, without examples, decoration, or your opinion of the passage.
Example
A précis of the long “When in the Course…” sentence: When a people must break a political connection and take a separate, equal station, respect for public opinion requires that they state their reasons.
Not this
Not a hint-list (which stores joints, not meaning), not a quotation, and not a response (“I agree that…”).
Connects to
Day 15 already taught the précis. Today you apply it to this week’s model, in one or two sentences, after the cards are closed.

A précis answers: what does the passage say, in shorter form, without leftover quotation and without your evaluation?

A worked précis of the first model sentence (close it and try yours first if you can). When a people must break political ties and take a separate and equal station, respect for the opinion of others requires that they declare their reasons for the separation.

What was dropped. The 1776 diction (“Course of human events,” “Laws of Nature and of Nature’s God”) is compressed; the two infinitives become two verbs in a when-clause; the main requirement remains.

What must not be added. “This is still relevant,” “Jefferson writes…,” or a history lecture. The précis is of the sentence you were given.

If you précis the second sentence instead, the claim and the series of that-clauses must still be visible in short form.

Remember

A précis restates meaning in short, complete sentences, in your own words. No quotation leftovers, no hints-only list, no opinion.

Key terms: précis.

  1. (A) is quotation, not a précis.

Physics · Friday mix: work, friction, and kinetic energy

50 min · Method: add the works, then W_net = ΔKE; friction from weeks 3–4 as a force that does negative work

Learn

Key terms

net work
Definition
The sum of the work done by every force acting on the object along the path, equal (in this course) to the work done by the net force along the path, and equal to the change in kinetic energy.
Example
A 20 N pull along 3 m, with a 5 N friction force opposite the motion: W_pull = 60 J, W_friction = −15 J, W_net = 45 J = ΔKE.
Not this
Not the work of one force alone, unless that force is the only one (or the only one with a component along the path).
Connects to
Monday and Wednesday already computed single-force work and ΔKE. Today you add the works and then use the theorem.

A Friday mix, not a new law. You already have W = F s cosθ, KE = ½ m v², and W_net = ΔKE. Today you use them together, including friction as a force that usually does negative work (it opposes the displacement).

A worked combination. A 4 kg block is pulled 2 m along a straight path by a 20 N force along the path. Kinetic friction is 9.8 N (for example, μ_k = 0.25 and N = 4 × 9.8, which you already know how to compute). Then:

  • W_pull = 20 × 2 = 40 J
  • W_friction = −9.8 × 2 = −19.6 J
  • W_net = 20.4 J = ΔKE

If the block started at rest, KE_final = 20.4 J, so ½ × 4 × v² = 20.4, v² = 10.2, v = √10.2 m/s. You may leave a square root, or remark that the number is a little more than 3.2 m/s, if a decimal is asked for and labeled as approximate.

A cleaner check you will see: 4 kg, 2 m, friction 9.8 N only (no pull), starting from a KE of 19.6 J: W_net = −19.6 J, and the block just stops.

This is still a first look at energy, beyond the Term 1 list. Next week adds gravitational potential energy near the Earth, as a model with m g h.

Remember

Add every force’s work (signs included). W_net = ΔKE. Friction opposite the displacement does negative work. Perpendicular forces still do zero work.

Key terms: net work.

Watch me

  1. W_pull = 20 × 3 = 60 J (along the displacement).
  2. W_friction = 5 × 3 × cos180° = −15 J.
  3. W_net = 60 − 15 = 45 J. That is also ΔKE.

Hint 1: The theorem

Hint 2: Speed

History · The Twelve Tables as a class of source

45 min · Method: a fragmentary legal tradition, dated carefully; no invented clauses

Learn

Key terms

Twelve Tables
Definition
Rome’s early written law code, in the tradition dated to the mid-fifth century BCE (often 451–450 BCE). The Tables do not survive as a complete original inscription; later writers quote or paraphrase fragments. This course treats them as a class of source: a remembered written law, known in pieces, not as a book you can open and read straight through.
Example
A later author quotes a rule as being “in the Twelve Tables”: that is a fragment, and it has passed through the quoting author’s language and purpose.
Not this
Not a complete surviving bronze or stone text we can photograph end to end, and not a modern statute book. The traditional date is a tradition, even if it is a widely used one.
Connects to
Monday and Wednesday asked how we know early Rome. Today the same habit is applied to law. Next week turns to a better-documented conflict: the Punic Wars, with Polybius among the sources.

What the tradition says. The Romans told that, in the mid-fifth century BCE, the law was written down on twelve tables and published, so that it was not only in the keeping of a few. Later Romans appealed to that written law as a foundation.

What survives. Not the complete original tables. What we have are fragments and reports in later authors. That is still evidence — of later memory, of legal language, and sometimes of an early rule — but a fragment is not the whole code, and a quotation is not an unfiltered photograph of the fifth century.

How to write about them. Prefer: “A later writer reports that the Twelve Tables said…” or “In the tradition, the Tables were written down around the mid-fifth century BCE.” Avoid: “Table III, line 2, in the original, states…” unless you actually have a surviving original, which this course does not put in front of you.

We will not invent a clause. No made-up law is offered here as if it were a table. The skill is the type of source, not a memorized list of rules.

Remember

The Twelve Tables are early written Roman law in the tradition (mid-fifth century BCE). They survive as later fragments, not as a complete original text. Quote the quoting author; do not pretend to have the bronze in front of you. The date is traditional.

Key terms: Twelve Tables.

Watch me

  2. What we have are fragments reported by later writers.

You may still say “in the mid-fifth century BCE, in the tradition,” which is honest and specific enough for this course.

Atelier · Friday: a value pass on the head

90 min · Method: a limited-value pass on the same head or plaster; stand back, then accents sparingly

Learn

Key terms

value pass (head)
Definition
A sitting devoted to organizing light and shadow on the head (or plaster) in a few values — usually a clear light, a clear large shadow, and one or two middles — rather than to adding new outline.
Example
Grouping the side plane, the jaw, and the neck’s shadow into one family of darks, then checking that the lit forehead is a family of lights, is a value pass.
Not this
Not a new drawing, and not a sudden rendering of every eyelash. The block-in stays; the values are brought into order.
Connects to
Week 4’s accents and lost edges belong here, used sparingly once the families of value are right.

Friday work, on the same head or plaster. Stand back. Half-close the eyes. How many values do you really see in the large form? Aim for a readable three or four, not twenty.

A practical order. (1) Confirm the terminator. (2) Make the whole shadow family sit together (no accidental hole of light in the unlit cheek unless the light actually makes one). (3) Keep the lit family together, with the forehead often among the lighter planes. (4) Only then, a few accents: an eyelid’s thin dark, a nostril, a found edge of the jaw against a quieter lost edge.

Critique, short and specific. What is the largest false value? Fix that before a smaller one.

Remember

Organize the head into a few values: light family, shadow family, one or two middles. Confirm the terminator first. Accents and lost edges come after, sparingly. Fix the largest false value first.

Key terms: value pass (head).

Watch me

  2. An eyelash is an accent, and it will not hold if the cheek’s family of values is wrong.
  3. Friday’s order is large families first, small accents later.

Together

Hint 1: Fewer values

Small contrasts drop out, and the large light and large shadow are easier to see.

Hint 2: A test

If you can still name only one value on the whole head, look again; if you see twenty, you are not grouping yet.

Modern language · Spanish: week-5 review (e → i, progressive, IOP, gustar)

40 min · Friday check of the week’s present-tense skills; no new tense

Learn

Key terms

week-5 Spanish check
Definition
A short review of this week’s present-tense skills: e → i stems, the present progressive, indirect-object pronouns, and the gustar pattern. No new tense is introduced.
Example
Pido un libro; estoy pidiendo agua; le doy el libro a Ana; me gustan los libros.
Not this
This is not a preterite, and it is not a vocabulary hunt. If you do not know a word, use one you were taught.
Connects to
A two-week composition comes on Day 30. Today is a Friday check of week 5 only.

Four skills, still in the present.

  1. e → i: pido, sirves, repetimos (no change in nosotros), vestís (no change).

Elective · Artistic anatomy 3: the torso — rib cage and pelvis

40 min · Method: two bony volumes and a soft middle; contrapposto as a tilt between blocks; no medical drills

Learn

Key terms

rib cage (artistic)
Definition
The bony thorax as an artistic form: a rounded cage, wider than it is deep in a typical academic model, attaching to the spine, with a lower opening (the costal margin) that is not a straight cut-off. In the figure it is a volume, not a flat shield.
Example
A front view that shows the cage tapering up to the shoulders and down toward the waist, with a soft inverted-U or inverted-V at the lower front, is starting to treat it as a solid.
Not this
Not a medical anatomy course, and not a count of every rib you must memorize as a drawing drill. You need the volume, the tilt, and the way the cage meets the abdomen and the shoulders.
Connects to
Anatomy 1 treated the skeleton as a frame; Anatomy 2 treated origin, insertion, and a few muscles. Today the torso’s bony container is the subject.
pelvis (artistic)
Definition
The bony basin at the base of the trunk, a volume that carries the abdomen and meets the thigh at the hip, with a front plane (including the anterior superior iliac spines as useful landmarks) and a tilt of its own.
Example
The “block” of the pelvis can tilt independently of the rib cage — that independent tilt is a large part of contrapposto in the torso, not only in the legs.
Not this
Not a second rib cage, and not a flat bow-tie drawn on the surface. Like the head’s egg, it is a solid first.
Connects to
The abdominal wall and the oblique forms sit between cage and pelvis; they are softer, but they follow those two bony volumes.

The torso as two volumes and a soft middle. The rib cage (above) and the pelvis (below) are bony solids. The abdomen and the obliques fill the space between them and change shape as the two blocks tilt.

Landmarks you can actually use, without a fake complete map. The notch at the top of the sternum, the lower front margin of the cage, the navel as a soft midpoint (not a rigid fraction), and the front points of the pelvis (the anterior superior iliac spines). If a particular plate or diagram disagrees, the observed form wins — the same rule as the head.

Contrapposto, now in the trunk. When the weight is on one leg, the pelvis usually drops on the free-leg side, and the rib cage often tilts the other way, a little, so the spine compensates. You have seen this in the Doryphoros discussion; today you feel it as two blocks.

No living, named model is required. A simple plaster torso, a careful academic diagram, or a short self-study of masses in the mirror (no photographs for a site, and no identifying description) is enough. The course still contains no identifying personal information.

Remember

The torso is a rib-cage volume and a pelvic volume, with a soft abdomen between them. The two blocks can tilt in opposite ways in contrapposto. Landmarks are tools; the observed form wins. No identifying live-model records.

Key terms: rib cage (artistic), pelvis (artistic).

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is an inscribed angle different from a central angle, and why does the same arc matter?
  2. An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle; a central angle has its vertex at the center. On the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle (Euclid III.20).
  3. If the vertices stand on different arcs, the half-or-double relation for that particular central angle does not apply.
  4. A near-miss is to treat any angle drawn in a circle as half of any other angle in the figure, without checking the arc.

If the displacement is zero, the work is zero, even if the effort feels large.
