Latin · The perfect of a deponent, a first look

40 min · Method: locūtus sum and cōnātus sum, the perfect tense of a deponent. The present table and last week’s absolutes are contrasts, not first looks

Learn

Key terms

perfect of a deponent
Definition
The perfect of a deponent is formed with the already-seen perfect participle plus sum: locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt. The forms still look passive and are translated as active: I spoke, or I have spoken. Cōnātus sum, cōnātus es, cōnātus est, and the rest, follow the same pattern: I tried, or I have tried. The participle agrees in number: locūtus est (singular), locūtī sunt (plural).
Example
Ego locūtus sum. Tū cōnātus es. Puer locūtus est. Nōs locūtī sumus.
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s present table (loquor, loqueris, loquitur). Not a first look at the perfect-participle ablative absolute (urbe captā, puerō locūtō), already taught: that is a loosely attached phrase, not the perfect tense with sum. Classroom sentences only, not copied from Ørberg.
Connects to
Last week’s present deponents, and the already-taught perfect participle in the absolute, as a contrast.

Last week’s present table was loquor, loqueris, loquitur, loquimur, loquiminī, loquuntur: I speak, you speak, and so on. Today the perfect tense of a deponent is taught: the participle locūtus, already seen in the absolute, now joins sum as a tense.

Locūtus sum, locūtus es, locūtus est, locūtī sumus, locūtī estis, locūtī sunt. The meaning is active: I spoke, or I have spoken. Cōnātus sum, cōnātus es, cōnātus est, cōnātī sumus, cōnātī estis, cōnātī sunt: I tried, or I have tried.

The participle agrees: locūtus est is singular; locūtī sunt is plural. This is not the ablative absolute puerō locūtō, which has no sum and is a loosely attached phrase already taught.

Mathematics · Geometry LIX: sine of an acute angle, a first look

45 min · Method: sine of an acute angle in a right triangle is the opposite side over the hypotenuse, from already-taught similar triangles. Degrees only; radians remain reserved

Learn

Key terms

sine of an acute angle
Definition
In a right triangle, the sine of an acute angle A, written sin A, is the length of the side opposite A divided by the length of the hypotenuse. The ratio is the same for a given angle in any right triangle, because those triangles are similar, from the already-taught AA similarity and scale factor. A classroom 3-4-5 right triangle, invented lengths, has sin A = 3/5 if A is opposite the side of 3. Sine is a ratio, not a length. Degrees only; the radian remains reserved. The unit circle remains reserved.
Example
In that 3-4-5 triangle, if A is opposite 3 and adjacent to 4, sin A = 3/5. A second right triangle with the same angle A, sides 6, 8, and 10, has sin A = 6/10, which is the same ratio 3/5.
Not this
Not a first look at similar triangles, the scale factor, or the Pythagorean theorem, already taught. Not cosine, which is tomorrow. Not a trigonometric table of official measured angles, and not a unit-circle definition. Classroom lengths are invented.
Connects to
Day 61’s similar triangles, Day 63’s scale factor, and the already-taught right triangle with a² + b² = c².

A right triangle has one right angle. Each of the other two angles is acute. Today a new name is given to a ratio already sitting in that triangle.

The sine of an acute angle A, written sin A, is the opposite side over the hypotenuse. Why is the ratio the same whenever the angle is the same? Two right triangles that share an acute angle also share the right angle, so they are similar by AA, already taught. Corresponding sides are in the same ratio, already taught as a scale factor. So opposite over hypotenuse does not change when the triangle is enlarged or reduced.

A classroom 3-4-5 triangle, invented lengths: if A is opposite 3, sin A = 3/5. A similar triangle with sides 6, 8, and 10 has the same angle A, and sin A = 6/10 = 3/5. Sine is a ratio, not a length. Cosine waits until tomorrow. Degrees only; the radian and the unit circle remain reserved.

Physics · Forced oscillations and resonance, a first look

40 min · Method: OpenStax College Physics 2e, section 16.8. A driving force, the natural frequency, and resonance when those frequencies are equal. Last week’s damping is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

forced oscillation and resonance
Definition
A forced oscillation, in OpenStax 16.8, is an oscillation driven by a periodic driving force. The driving force puts energy into the system at a certain frequency, not necessarily the system’s natural frequency. The natural frequency is the frequency at which the system would oscillate if there were no driving and no damping force. Resonance is the phenomenon of driving a system with a frequency equal to its natural frequency; a system so driven is said to resonate. Energy is transferred most efficiently at resonance, and the amplitude is then at its greatest for that system.
Example
OpenStax’s piano-string illustration: if you sing a loud brief note toward a piano with the dampers off, the strings that share your note’s frequency resonate and sing the note back. That is a kind of motion, not an official measured frequency invented here. Wednesday treats how damping changes the height and width of the resonance peak.

Not a first look at last week’s damped motion, or at underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped motion, already taught, except as the damping that will be used on Wednesday. Not an official measured wind speed for a named bridge, and not a first look at MRI as a medical procedure beyond OpenStax’s brief mention of resonant nuclei, which waits if it is used at all until a later sourced contrast. Forced oscillations in a later quantitative formula remain reserved if 16.8 does not give one.

Seminar · Logic: Ferison, a first look at a third-figure EIO

40 min · Method: Ferison (EIO-3). No M are P; some M are S; therefore some S are not P. Last week’s Felapton is a contrast, not a first look

Learn

Key terms

Ferison
Definition
Ferison is a valid third-figure mood, EIO-3: No M are P; some M are S; therefore some S are not P. The middle term is the subject of both premises, as in last week’s Felapton and in Darapti. The conclusion is particular and negative, like Felapton’s, but the second premise is particular (some M are S), not universal (all M are S). So Ferison does not need two universal premises. Invented terms are classroom terms, not official classifications.
Example
No island-built temples are wooden; some island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. The sentence is invented so that the figure can be seen; it is not a historical claim about a named building.
Not this
Not last week’s Felapton (EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P), which has a universal second premise. Not Darapti (AAI-3), and not first-figure Ferio, already taught, which has a different placement of the middle term. Other third-figure moods remain reserved.
Connects to
The third figure, already taught, last week’s Felapton, and first-figure Ferio, as contrasts.

Last week’s Felapton, still in the third figure, had two universal premises: no M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. Today a second third-figure particular negative begins, Ferison, whose second premise is particular.

No M are P. Some M are S. Therefore some S are not P. The middle term is still the subject of both premises. The conclusion is still particular and negative, not “no S are P.” Because only some M are said to be S, the premises already name some S that are not P, namely those M that are S.

An invented classroom example, not a historical claim: no island-built temples are wooden; some island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. First-figure Ferio, already taught, has a different placement of the middle term and is not today’s mood.
