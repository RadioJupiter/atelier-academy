Latin · The deponent pluperfect, used in short sentences

40 min · Method: cōnātus eram in short original sentences, with agreement of the participle

Learn

Key terms

pluperfect of a deponent
Definition
The same pluperfect pattern holds for other already-taught deponents: cōnātus eram, I had tried, uses the participle cōnātus with eram. Cōnāta erat, she had tried, follows the already-taught agreement of the participle.
Example
Ego cōnātus eram. Puella cōnāta erat. Nōs cōnātī erāmus.
Not this
Not cōnābar, I was trying or I used to try, and not cōnātus sum, I tried or I have tried. Not a new verb beyond the already-taught deponents.
Connects to
Yesterday’s locūtus eram, and the already-taught perfect and imperfect of cōnor.

Yesterday locūtus eram used a participle plus eram. Today the same method on cōnor, already taught in the present, imperfect, and perfect. Cōnātus eram, I had tried. The participle agrees, as it did in the perfect: a feminine subject takes cōnāta, a masculine plural cōnātī. Sentences are original, not copied from Ørberg. The first persons of sequor and ēgredior remain reserved.

Word How to say it Meaning

cōnāta erat koh-NAH-tah EH-raht she had tried
cōnātī erāmus koh-NAH-tee eh-RAH-moos we had tried
locūtus eram lo-KOO-toos EH-rahm I had spoken
loquēbar loh-KWAY-bar I was speaking, or I used to speak (imperfect)
locūtus sum lo-KOO-toos soom I spoke, or I have spoken (perfect)

A short pair: Puella cōnāta erat, et tum rīsit. She had tried, and then she laughed. The trying is already completed before the laugh.

Remember

Cōnātus eram, I had tried, is the pluperfect of cōnor. The participle agrees with the subject. It is not cōnābar and not cōnātus sum.

Watch me

  3. If the subject is feminine, the participle is cōnāta: Puella cōnāta erat.
  4. Do not write cōnābar, which is the imperfect, or cōnātus sum, which is the perfect.

Mathematics · Geometry LXVIII: a line perpendicular to a plane, a first look

40 min · Method: a line perpendicular to two intersecting lines in the plane through the foot

Learn

Key terms

a line perpendicular to a plane
Definition
A line is perpendicular to a plane if it meets the plane at a point P, the foot, and is perpendicular to every line in the plane that passes through P. In practice it is enough, and it is the method used today, to check two intersecting lines in the plane through P: if the given line is perpendicular to both, it is perpendicular to the plane.
Example
On the invented rectangular box, the vertical edge DA meets the floor at A and is perpendicular to the two floor edges AB and AE that meet at A, so DA is perpendicular to the floor plane.
Not this
Not a line that merely meets the plane at one point, which may be oblique. Not perpendicular only to one line in the plane. Not an official claim about a real wall being square.
Connects to
Yesterday’s three relations of a line and a plane. Two planes in space wait until tomorrow.

Yesterday a line could meet a plane at one point without being perpendicular to it; that meeting may be oblique. Today a first look at the perpendicular case. If a line meets a plane at P, and is perpendicular to two lines in the plane that intersect at P, then it is perpendicular to every line in the plane through P, and we say the line is perpendicular to the plane. P is called the foot.

On the invented rectangular box, DA meets the floor at A and makes a right angle with both AB and AE, the two floor edges through A. So DA is perpendicular to the floor. The box is still an invented model; invent no official height of a room. An oblique meeting, and two planes in space, wait until later.

Remember

A line is perpendicular to a plane when it meets the plane at P and is perpendicular to two intersecting lines in the plane through P. Meeting the plane at one point is not enough.

Watch me

On the invented rectangular box, why is DA perpendicular to the floor, and why would a line that merely meets the floor at A not automatically be perpendicular?

  1. DA meets the floor at A, the foot.
  2. AB and AE lie in the floor and intersect at A. Because the box is rectangular, DA is perpendicular to both AB and AE.
  3. So DA is perpendicular to the floor plane.
  4. A different line through A, leaning toward B, would still meet the floor at one point, but it would not be perpendicular to AE, and so would not be perpendicular to the floor.

Hint 1: The foot

Hint 2: Two lines

The floor edges through B are BA and BF. If CB is perpendicular to both, it is perpendicular to the floor.

Science · Ochre, from rock to pigment, a first look

40 min · Method: USGS sources on earthy iron-oxide pigments; hematite red, hydrated oxides yellow to brown

Learn

Key terms

ochre
Definition
Ochre, as the term is used in USGS mineral sources, is an earthy, often clayey form of an iron-oxide mineral used as a pigment after milling and only minimal processing. Hematite gives red ochres; limonite or goethite, which are hydrated iron oxides, give yellow, buff, or brown ochres. Impurities also influence the shade.
Origin
From Greek ōkhra, from ōkhros, pale yellow, through Latin ochra and Old French ocre (Etymonline). The further origin of the Greek word is unknown.
Example
A yellow earth, ground and used as a paint, whose color comes from a hydrated iron oxide, is a yellow ochre in this sense.
Not this
Not a synthetic modern convenience color from a catalog, and not lapis lazuli, which remains reserved. Not an official iron percentage invented for a classroom lump of earth.
Connects to
Term 3 science begins the module From rock to pigment. Hematite, as the red anhydrous oxide, is Thursday’s first look. The limited palette in the atelier uses yellow ochre as one of four paints.

Term 3 science begins the module From rock to pigment. The first look is ochre, an earth pigment, not a synthetic catalog color. The U.S. Geological Survey, in the 2019 Minerals Yearbook article on iron oxide pigments (Amanda S. Brioche), and in the earlier Bulletin 430-G on mineral paints, treats natural iron-oxide pigments as minerals that can be used as colors after milling and minimal processing. Hematite (Fe2O3) is the usual red; goethite or limonite, hydrated iron oxides, vary from yellow to brown and include ochres. Bulletin 430-G says, as ordinarily used, “ocher” is applied to the earthy and pulverulent forms of hematite and limonite, made more or less impure by other oxides and by clay, and that hematites give red ochers while limonites give yellow, buff, or brown. The natural color depends on hydration, oxidation, and impurities.

Invent no official iron percentage for a classroom lump, and do not treat a store paint name as a mineral identification. Lapis lazuli and ultramarine remain reserved. Thursday takes hematite more closely, including the already-noted fact from Bulletin 430-G that a red ocher can be made by calcining common yellow ocher.

Remember

Ochre is an earthy iron-oxide pigment. Hematite gives red; hydrated iron oxides (limonite or goethite) give yellow, buff, or brown. Invent no official percentage for a lump of earth.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Volume I, Chapter 1: the opening, a first look

40 min · Method: the 1813 opening two sentences; a claimed universal truth, then where it is already fixed

Learn

Key terms

ironic opening (Pride and Prejudice)
Definition
The novel opens with a sentence that presents a “truth universally acknowledged” as if it were settled, and the next sentence shows that the “truth” is what surrounding families have already decided. The irony is that a claim offered as universal is, in the book’s own wording, a fixed idea about a “rightful property.” The set text is the 1813 wording.
Example
“It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.” The next sentence already places that “truth” in “the minds of the surrounding families.”
Not this
Not a statement the course is asking you to adopt as a fact about living people. Not an official income, and not an identification of a character with a historical person. British spelling inside the quotation stays inside the quotation.
Connects to
Term 3 literature begins with Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, Volume I, Chapter 1, in the 1813 wording (Wikisource, and the 1813 Gutenberg text). The dialogue about Netherfield waits until Thursday.

Term 3 literature begins Jane Austen’s Pride and Prejudice, from the 1813 wording as posted on Wikisource from the first edition, which agrees in these sentences with the 1813 Gutenberg text. Today only the first two sentences of Volume I, Chapter 1, are the set text. British spellings such as “neighbourhood” stay inside the quotation; the lesson around them uses US spelling. Do not add an official identification of a character with a living or historical person, and do not treat the opening sentence as a fact about living people.

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

However little known the feelings or views of such a man may be on his first entering a neighbourhood, this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families, that he is considered as the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.

The first sentence offers a “truth universally acknowledged.” The second already shows where that “truth” is “so well fixed”: “in the minds of the surrounding families,” so that the man “is considered as the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.” The irony is in that turn: a claim announced as universal is, in the book’s own next sentence, a fixed family idea, and even a claim of “rightful property.” Thursday takes up the dialogue that follows, still in Chapter 1, including the novel’s own figure “four or five thousand a year,” which is Mrs. Bennet’s report in the book, not an official salary.

Seminar · Music: binary form, a first look

40 min · Method: two strains, A then B, on a short invented figure; ternary form reserved

Learn

Key terms

binary form
Definition
Binary form is a two-part musical form, A then B. Each part is a complete strain; B is a second strain, not only a repeat of A, and not a return of A as a third large part. Today’s examples are short invented classroom figures, not a copyrighted score.
Example
An invented eight-bar figure in two strains of four bars each: A in C major, ending on the dominant; B starting from that dominant and ending back on C, still invented, is one short binary shape.
Not this
Not the already-taught musical period (antecedent and consequent), which may sit inside a strain. Not ternary form, ABA, which remains reserved. Not a copyrighted tune named as a classroom example.
Connects to
Term 3 music begins form and listening. The already-taught period, cadences, and invented figures remain in use inside a strain, but binary form is today’s first look at a two-part shape.

Term 3 music begins form. Today a first look at binary form: two parts, A then B. Each part is a complete strain. B is a second strain, not merely a copy of A, and not a return of A as a third large part. Ternary form, ABA, remains reserved. A musical period, already taught, may sit inside a strain, but a period is not by itself binary form. Today’s figures are invented: a short eight-bar example, two strains of four bars, in C major, written in scale degrees, not a copyrighted tune. Strain A: 1–3–5–5 | 6–5–4–3 | 2–4–3–2 | 1–7–1–5, ending on the dominant. Strain B: 5–6–5–4 | 3–2–1–2 | 3–5–4–2 | 1–7–1–1, ending on C. Do not treat those degrees as an official work, and do not substitute a copyrighted melody.

Remember

Binary form is A then B, two complete strains. B is a second strain, not a repeat of A only, and not a return of A as a third part. Today’s eight-bar figure is invented.

Watch me

In the invented eight-bar figure, where does A end, where does B end, and why is the figure binary rather than a single period or a ternary form?

  1. A is the first four bars and ends on the dominant, scale degree 5.
  2. B is the second four bars and ends on C, scale degree 1.
  3. It is binary because there are two strains, A then B. A single period would be one antecedent-and-consequent pair, not two strains. Ternary form would come back to A as a third large part, and that form is reserved.
  4. The figure is invented, not a copyrighted work.

Together

If someone played A and then played A again, would that be binary form as taught today? Why or why not?

Hint 1: Two parts

Binary form is A then B, a second strain, not only a repeat.

Hint 2: A repeat

A repeated strain is a repeat, not a second part named B.

Use today's key terms (binary form). For each term, close the card and then: (1) explain it in your own words; (2) give a new example, not the one on the card; (3) name a near-miss — something it is easily confused with — and say how you would tell them apart.

Hint 1: Own words

Start from the definition, but do not copy it. If you can only repeat the card, close it and try again from the example you invent.

Hint 2: Near-miss

The "Not this" line on the card names a common mix-up. Explain why that mix-up would change an answer in this lesson.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. What is binary form, as taught today, and how does it differ from a musical period already taught?
  2. In the invented eight-bar figure, where does A end, and where does B end?
  3. Why is a repeat of A not binary form, and what form remains reserved?
  4. For each of today's key terms (binary form), write one sentence in your own words and one near-miss you must not confuse it with. Then apply one of the terms to a fresh case of your own (not the example on the card).

Exercises

  1. Write, in scale degrees or in words, a short invented two-strain figure of your own, and label A and B.
  2. Why must you not use a copyrighted tune as the classroom example?
  3. How can an already-taught period sit inside a strain without becoming the whole form?
