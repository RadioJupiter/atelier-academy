Latin · Capitulum XII, part 4: the fourth declension (exercitus, metus)

40 minutes · Method: a new declension, recognized first in the nominative and the genitive. Instruction in English. Original sentences.

Learn

Key terms

fourth declension
Definition
A declension whose nominative singular often ends in -us and whose genitive singular ends in -ūs (with a long ū), not the second-declension genitive -ī. A first look here is exercitus, -ūs, masculine, “army,” and metus, -ūs, masculine, “fear.”
Example
Exercitus magnus est. Metus nōn magnus est.
Not this
Not a second-declension noun such as gladius, gladiī. The nominative exercitus can look like gladius, but the genitive is exercitūs, not *exercitī.
Connects to
Most fourth-declension nouns in this first look are masculine. You do not need every case today; the job is to recognize the -us / -ūs dictionary pair and not treat it as second declension.
Word How to say it Meaning
exercitus eks-KEH-ri-tus an army (nominative singular, masculine)
exercitūs eks-KEH-ri-toos of an army (genitive singular)
metus MEH-tus fear (nominative singular, masculine)
Exercitus magnus est eks-KEH-ri-tus MAG-nus est The army is large

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Some nouns that end in -us in the nominative singular are not second declension. The fourth declension has a genitive singular in -ūs (a long ū): exercitus, exercitūs, an army; metus, metūs, fear. Both are masculine in this first look.

Gladius, gladiī (already in use this week) is second declension: genitive -ī. Exercitus, exercitūs only looks similar in the nominative. The dictionary pair tells the difference.

Original sentences: Exercitus magnus est. Metus nōn magnus est. Exercitus nōn parvus est. Metus magnus nōn est.

You do not need every case of the fourth declension today. The job is to recognize the -us / -ūs pair and to keep a first-and-second-declension adjective such as magnus in agreement with these masculine nouns.

Remember

The fourth declension is recognized by the genitive singular -ūs, not by the nominative -us alone. exercitus, exercitūs and metus, metūs are masculine in this first look. They are not second-declension nouns like gladius, gladiī.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXII: the converse, and more than one integer triple

40 minutes · Method: if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right; 3-4-5 is one triple, not the only one

Learn

Key terms

converse of the Pythagorean theorem
Definition
If the sides a, b, and c of a triangle satisfy a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right-angled at the angle opposite c. This is the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, not the same sentence as the theorem itself.
Example
3² + 4² = 5², so a 3-4-5 triangle is right-angled opposite the side 5. 5² + 12² = 13², so a 5-12-13 triangle is right-angled opposite the side 13.
Not this
Not “every right triangle is 3-4-5.” Many right triangles are not integer-sided, and other integer triples exist (5-12-13, and scaled copies such as 6-8-10).
Connects to
The theorem says: if the triangle is right, then a² + b² = c². The converse says: if a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right. Today you may use the converse to test a triangle whose sides are known. A 3-5-6 triangle fails the test, as 9 + 25 is not 36.

The Pythagorean theorem says: if a triangle is right, with hypotenuse c, then a² + b² = c².

The converse says the other direction: if a triangle has sides a, b, and c that satisfy a² + b² = c², then the triangle is right-angled at the angle opposite c.

Those are two different sentences. You may now use the converse as a test when the three sides are known.

A 3-4-5 triangle passes: 9 + 16 = 25. A 5-12-13 triangle also passes: 25 + 144 = 169. A 6-8-10 triangle passes as well: 36 + 64 = 100; it is a scaled 3-4-5 triangle, scale factor 2. A 3-5-6 triangle fails: 9 + 25 = 34, which is not 36.

3-4-5 is not the only right triangle. Some right triangles have integer sides in other triples. Many others have a side that is not an integer (for example, legs 1 and 1, hypotenuse √2). The converse does not require a memorized list. It requires the equality of the squares.

Remember

If a² + b² = c², the triangle is right-angled opposite c. That is the converse, not the theorem itself. 3-4-5, 5-12-13, and 6-8-10 are integer examples; they are not the only right triangles, and 3-5-6 is not one of them.

Key terms: converse of the Pythagorean theorem.

Science · Water: capillary rise

40 minutes · Method: a narrow tube, a meniscus, and the already-taught surface tension

Learn

Key terms

capillary rise
Definition
The rise (or, in some liquids, the fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube, caused by the liquid’s surface tension together with the way the liquid meets the wall. For water in a clean glass tube, the surface curves up at the wall and the water rises.
Example
A narrow clean glass tube standing in a dish of water shows a meniscus that curves up, and the water in the tube stands higher than the water in the dish.
Not this
Not a pump, and not a new official IAPWS surface-tension figure. The rise is a consequence of surface tension and wetting; a classroom tube’s height is invented unless a sourced height is named.
Connects to
A narrower tube gives a larger rise, for the same liquid and wall, because the same tension acts around a smaller circumference, supporting a taller column. Today’s job is that qualitative dependence, not a derived official formula with an invented height.

Capillary rise is what you see when a narrow tube stands in a liquid and the liquid climbs (or, for some liquids, drops) inside the tube. For water in a clean glass tube, the surface meets the glass in a curve that is higher at the wall — a meniscus that turns up — and the water in the tube stands higher than the water in the dish.

The cause is the surface tension already taught, together with the way water wets clean glass (it is attracted to the wall). The tension around the contact line can support the weight of a short column. In a narrower tube, that column is taller, for the same liquid and the same wall, because the same kind of tension is now acting around a smaller circumference, on a smaller cross-section of water.

Classroom tube widths and the height of a rise in a demonstration are invented unless a sourced height is named. This sitting does not invent an official rise from the IAPWS 72.74 mN/m figure, and it does not treat a formula as if a measured official height had been opened. The IAPWS number remains a surface tension, not a capillary height.

A liquid that does not wet the wall in this way can show a meniscus that turns down, and a column that falls rather than rises. That case is noted, not worked as today’s main picture.

Remember

For water in a clean glass tube, the meniscus turns up and the water rises; a narrower tube gives a larger rise. Surface tension and wetting are the cause. A classroom height is invented unless a sourced height is named. The IAPWS 72.74 mN/m figure is a surface tension, not a height.

Definition

40 minutes · Method: place the ear on the side plane, then review sockets, wedge, mouth, and ear as one head

Learn

Key terms

the ear, placed from the sockets and the nose
Definition
The ear on the blocked-in head, first placed as a simple form whose height, on a level front-facing head, runs roughly from the brow or the top of the socket down toward the base of the nose, and which sits back on the side plane, not as a copied seashell of inner lines.
Example
A first pass sets the ear on the side plane, in the same light, with its top and bottom checked against the socket and the nose already placed.
Not this
Not a full inner cartography drawn first, and not an ear that slides onto the cheek as if it were a front-plane feature. The far ear, if the head is turned, is smaller or more hidden.
Connects to
A turned head changes how much ear is seen. The check is still this head’s sockets and nose, and the side plane already blocked in.

The ear sits on the side plane of the head, not on the cheek as a front feature. On a fairly level, front-facing head, a first height-check runs from about the brow or the top of the socket down toward the base of the nose. You confirm that on this drawing, rather than forcing a memorized measure that fights what you see.

Economics · Simple interest, in a labeled invented example

30 minutes · Method: I = P × r × t, with invented principal and rate, labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

simple interest
Definition
Interest computed only on the original principal, as I = P × r × t, where P is the principal, r is the interest rate per time unit as a decimal, and t is the time in those same units (here, years). The amount at the end is P + I.
Example
A labeled invented principal of 200, at a labeled invented rate of 0.05 a year, for 3 years, gives I = 200 × 0.05 × 3 = 30, and an amount 230.
Not this
Not compound interest (interest on interest), which is not today’s job, and not an official bank rate. Classroom principals and rates are invented and labeled invented.
Connects to
Last week’s credit and bank (deposits and loans) are the setting. Today names the interest on a loan or a deposit, in this simple model only. No official rate is invented for a named bank.

Last week a bank was a place that holds deposits and makes loans, in labeled invented figures. Interest is the extra amount paid for the use of money. Simple interest is computed only on the original principal P:

I = P × r × t

r is the rate per year, written as a decimal (5% is 0.05). t is the time in years. The amount at the end is P + I.

A labeled invented example: principal 200, rate 0.05 a year, time 3 years. I = 200 × 0.05 × 3 = 30. The amount is 230.

This is not compound interest, which adds interest to the principal as you go; that is a later job. Classroom principals, rates, and times are invented and labeled invented. This lesson does not invent an official rate for a named bank, and it does not treat 0.05 as a sourced market figure.

Remember

Simple interest is I = P × r × t, on the original principal only. The amount is P + I. 5% is 0.05. Classroom numbers are invented and labeled. Compound interest is not today’s job, and no official bank rate is invented.

Key terms: simple interest.

Watch me

A labeled invented loan has P = 400, r = 0.05 a year, t = 2 years. Find the interest and the amount.

  1. I = 400 × 0.05 × 2 = 40. Invented.
  2. The amount is 400 + 40 = 440. Invented.

Together

A labeled invented deposit earns 18 in simple interest in 3 years, at r = 0.03 a year. What was the principal?

Hint 1: The formula

18 = P × 0.03 × 3 = P × 0.09.

Hint 2: The principal

P = 18 ÷ 0.09 = 200. Invented.
