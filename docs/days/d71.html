Latin · Capitulum XIII: the year and the months

25 minutes · Method: annus, mēnsis, and the twelve month names, from Allen and Greenough §630

Learn

Key terms

mēnsis
Definition
The Latin word for a month. It is a third-declension noun (mēnsis, mēnsis, masculine).
Example
Annus duodecim mēnsēs habet: a year has twelve months.
Not this
Not a day (diēs), and not the year (annus). Mēnsis names the month as a span, not a single named day of it.
Connects to
Capitulum XIII begins with the year and the months. The month names (Iānuārius, Februārius, and the rest) will be used with Kalendae, Nonae, and Īdūs later this week.
the Latin month names
Definition
The twelve month names in Latin: Iānuārius, Februārius, Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September, October, November, December.
Example
Mārtius, Maius, Iūlius, and October are the four months whose Nonae and Īdūs fall later than in the other months, as Allen and Greenough §631 states.
Not this
Not the English names used as if they were Latin, and not a claim that Iūlius and Augustus were always the names of those months. Allen and Greenough §630 notes that Quīntīlis was renamed Iūlius, and Sextīlis was renamed Augustus.
Connects to
These names will agree, as adjectives, with Kalendae, Nonae, and Īdūs when dates are formed later this week.

A year, in Latin, is annus, -ī, masculine. A month is mēnsis, mēnsis, masculine, a third-declension noun. A year has twelve months: Annus duodecim mēnsēs habet.

The twelve names, in order, are Iānuārius, Februārius, Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September, October, November, December. They are used as adjectives when they agree with a named day later this week. For today, learn the names and the two nouns.

Allen and Greenough §630 notes that the month once called Quīntīlis received the name Iūlius, and Sextīlis was called Augustus. This lesson does not invent a story around that renaming; it records the grammar book’s note so that Iūlius and Augustus are not treated as if they had always been the names.

Mathematics · Geometry XXX: the area of a triangle

40 minutes · Method: a triangle as half a rectangle; A = ½bh, with h perpendicular

Learn

Key terms

area of a triangle
Definition
The number of unit squares a triangle covers. A triangle is half of a rectangle that has the same base and the same perpendicular height, so its area is one-half of base times height: A = ½bh, where h is perpendicular to the base.
Example
A triangle with base 8 and perpendicular height 5 has area ½ × 8 × 5 = 20.
Not this
Not one-half of base times a slanted side. The height must meet the base at a right angle. The area is also not the perimeter.
Connects to
Tomorrow a height that is not given will be found, when the figure allows it, by last week’s Pythagorean theorem. Circle area remains reserved.
perpendicular height
Definition
The distance from a vertex of a triangle to the line of the opposite side, measured along a line that meets that side at a right angle.
Example
In the figure with base AB = 8 and vertex C, the dashed line from C to D on AB is the height only if angle at D is right.
Not this
Not a slanted side from C to A or C to B, even if that side is labeled with a length.
Connects to
This is the same idea as an altitude, already used last week to the hypotenuse. Here it is used to find area, not to prove the Pythagorean theorem again.

A rectangle that is 6 units long and 4 units tall covers 6 × 4 = 24 unit squares. That is its area. A diagonal cuts the rectangle into two triangles that cover the same amount, so each triangle has area 12.

The same fact, stated for any triangle, is: the area is half of the base times the perpendicular height,

A = ½bh.

The height h must be perpendicular to the base (it meets the base, or the line of the base, at a right angle). A slanted side is not the height.

In this figure the base is 8 and the dashed height is 5, so A = ½ × 8 × 5 = 20. The lengths are measures in the figure, not an official survey.

Last week’s Pythagorean theorem is not retaught as a new claim today. It will be used tomorrow when a height has to be found before the area can be written.

Remember

A triangle’s area is ½bh, with h perpendicular to the chosen base. A triangle is half of a rectangle with the same base and the same perpendicular height. A slanted side is not h.

Key terms: area of a triangle, perpendicular height.

  2. Each triangle has area 12, because the two triangles fill the rectangle equally.

Writing & Rhetoric · Informal fallacies: a first look

40 minutes · Method: a fallacy is a flaw in reasoning, not merely a false claim

Learn

Key terms

fallacy
Definition
A flaw in the reasoning of an argument: the support does not actually establish the claim in the way the argument pretends, even if some statement in it happens to be true, or happens to be false for another reason.
Origin
From Middle English, from Old French fallace, from Latin fallācia, “deception, deceit,” from fallāx, from fallere, “to deceive.”
Example
An invented example: “This triangle has sides 3, 5, and 6, and I like the number 6, so it is a right triangle.” Liking 6 does not establish the claim; the squares should be checked (9 + 25 = 34, not 36).
Not this
Not merely a false claim, and not a typo or a wrong calculation by itself. A true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacious route, and a false claim can be false without this week’s named patterns.
Connects to
Last week’s précis restates a passage faithfully. This week asks whether an argument’s reasoning holds. Week 13’s warrant is the step that a fallacy often fakes or skips.

A fallacy is a flaw in the reasoning. The support does not actually establish the claim in the way the argument pretends.

That is not the same thing as a false claim. A claim can be false because a fact is wrong, without this week’s named patterns. A claim can even be true, and the route to it can still be fallacious: the conclusion was not earned by the reasons given.

An invented example, labeled invented:

“This triangle has sides 3, 5, and 6, and I like the number 6, so it is a right triangle.”

Liking the number 6 is not a warrant that the triangle is right. Last week’s converse is the test that was skipped: 3² + 5² = 9 + 25 = 34, which is not 6² = 36. The argument is fallacious, and (in this case) the claim is also false. The two facts are distinct.

A second invented example: “6-8-10 is a right triangle because I said so first.” Being first to speak is not a reason. The squares still have to be checked (they do hold: 36 + 64 = 100). The conclusion happens to be true; the reasoning is still a fallacy.

The classroom arguments in this week’s writing are invented examples, labeled invented. They are for practicing the method. No contemporary political case is used as the set example. A précis, already taught last week, is not retaught as a first look.

Remember

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning, not merely a false statement. A true conclusion can still have been reached by a bad route. This week’s later sittings name three patterns: a straw man, a false dilemma, and ad hominem.

Key terms: fallacy.

Physics · Stable, unstable, and neutral equilibrium

40 minutes · Method: classify rest by what a small displacement does

Learn

Key terms

stable equilibrium
Definition
A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a restoring effect: the object tends to return to that position.
Origin
From Latin aequilībrium, from equal + lībra, “balance.”
Example
An invented demonstration: a marble at the bottom of a bowl, slightly nudged, rolls back down to the bottom.
Not this
Not merely “not moving.” An object can also rest in an unstable or a neutral position. Last week’s “does not tip” is related, but today’s names are about what a small displacement does.
Connects to
The word comes from Latin aequilībrium, from equal + lībra, “balance,” as the English Wiktionary entry states. Last week’s line from the center of mass and the base of support is the tool that still explains tipping; today’s three names classify the rest.
unstable equilibrium
Definition
A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a further move away: the object does not return, and it tends to leave that position.
Example
An invented demonstration: a marble balanced at the top of a smooth hill, slightly nudged, rolls down away from the top.
Not this
Not simply “moving.” At the exact top the marble can be at rest; the position is unstable because a small nudge ends the rest.
Connects to
This is the counterpart of stable equilibrium. A vertical line from the center of mass that is only barely kept over the base, as in last week’s tipped block, is the same family of idea.
neutral equilibrium
Definition
A rest position from which a small displacement leads to a new rest nearby, without a tendency to return or to run away.
Example
An invented demonstration: a marble on a flat, level table, slightly moved, stays at rest in the new place.
Not this
Not stable (it does not return to the old place) and not unstable (it does not run away down a slope). Flat and level is part of this invented picture; a slanted table is a different case.
Connects to
The three names — stable, unstable, and neutral — classify rest. They do not replace last week’s test for tipping of a block.

Last week an object at rest could tip, or not, according to whether a vertical line from the center of mass fell inside the base of support. That test still holds. Today adds three names for kinds of rest, according to what a small displacement does.

Stable equilibrium: a small nudge leads to a restoring effect; the object tends to return. Unstable equilibrium: a small nudge leads to a further move away. Neutral equilibrium: a small nudge leads to a new rest nearby, without a tendency to return or to run away.

These three invented demonstrations, labeled invented, are only pictures of the three names: a marble at the bottom of a bowl (stable), at the top of a hill (unstable), and on a flat, level table (neutral). They are not official laboratory measurements, and they do not invent a force in newtons.

“At rest” alone is not enough to name the kind. The question is what happens after a small displacement.

The bowl, hill, table, and hanging card in this week’s physics are invented classroom demonstrations, labeled invented. They are for the method, not official laboratory measurements. Last week’s base of support is used, not retaught as a first look.

Remember

Stable: a small displacement tends to be reversed. Unstable: a small displacement tends to grow. Neutral: a small displacement leads to a new rest nearby. Last week’s tipping test is still the tool for a block and its base; it is not retaught as new.

Key terms: stable equilibrium, unstable equilibrium, neutral equilibrium.

History · Anthemius of Tralles and Isidorus of Miletus

40 minutes · Method: the builders named in Buildings I.1.23–24, in Dewing

Learn

Key terms

Anthemius of Tralles
Definition
The master-builder named by Procopius in Buildings I.1.24, in Dewing’s translation: “the most learned man in the skilled craft which is known as the art of building,” who regulated the artisans’ tasks and prepared designs of the future construction.
Example
In the set extract, Anthemius “ministered to the Emperor’s enthusiasm” and prepared designs in advance.
Not this
Not Isidorus of Miletus, who is associated with him in the same section as “another master-builder,” and not a modern architect’s name substituted for Dewing’s wording.
Connects to

Isidorus of Miletus
Definition
The second master-builder named by Procopius in Buildings I.1.24, in Dewing: “another master-builder, Isidorus by name, a Milesian by birth, a man who was intelligent and worthy to assist the Emperor Justinian.”

Atelier · A value pass across the features already placed

90 minutes · Method: light, mid-tone, and dark on the features last week placed

Learn

Key terms

a value pass across the features
Definition
A sitting that assigns light, mid-tone, and dark to the features already placed (sockets, nose, mouth, ear), according to how each plane faces the light, without moving those features to a new place.
Example
The well of a socket, already placed last week as a plane, often takes a darker value than the forehead above it, because it turns away from the light.
Not this
Not a new placement of the features, not a first value scale as if weeks 5–8 had not happened, and not a change of medium.
Connects to
Last week placed the sockets, the nose as a wedge, the mouth as two masses, and the ear on the side plane. This week’s job is value on those same forms.

Last week the sockets, the nose as a wedge, the mouth as two masses, and the ear on the side plane were placed on the blocked-in head. Today does not move them. Today is a value pass: light, mid-tone, and dark, according to how each already-placed plane faces the light.

A plane that faces the light is lighter; a plane that turns away is darker; the terminator, already used in the earlier head work, is the boundary where the form turns out of the light. The well of a socket often goes darker than the forehead above it. The front plane of the nose, if it faces the light, stays lighter than the side plane of the same wedge.

This is not a new medium and not a first value scale. Weeks 5–8 already taught light on simple forms and a value pass on the head. This week applies that habit to the features now in place. No identifying person is used as a model.

This week is a value pass across the features already placed last week on the blocked-in head. It is not a new medium, not a first head, and not a first feature-pass. No identifying person is used as a model. Weeks 5–8 on light and planes, and last week’s sockets, nose, mouth, and ear, are used, not retaught as first looks.

Remember

A value pass assigns light, mid-tone, and dark to features already placed, according to the planes and the light. It does not move the features, and it is not a new medium.

Key terms: a value pass across the features.

Elective · Logic: Ferio, a first-figure EIO syllogism

25 minutes · Method: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P

Learn

Key terms

Ferio
Definition
A valid first-figure syllogism of the form EIO: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P. The major premise is universal negative, the minor particular affirmative, and the conclusion particular negative.
Example
No whales are insects; some sea animals are whales; therefore some sea animals are not insects.
Not this
Not Darii (AII), already taught, and not Celarent (EAE), already taught. Ferio’s conclusion stays particular and negative: “some S are not P,” not “no S are P,” which the particular minor does not give.
Connects to
Barbara, Celarent, and Darii have already been taught as first-figure forms. Ferio is the remaining standard first-figure form of that set. Informal fallacies, taught this week in writing, are a different kind of flaw, not a fourth syllogism.

Last week taught Darii (AII): all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. Earlier, Celarent (EAE) and Barbara were taught. Today is the remaining standard first-figure form in that set: Ferio (EIO).

  • Major (E): No M are P.
  • Minor (I): Some S are M.
  • Conclusion (O): Some S are not P.

The middle term M still joins the premises. The conclusion stays particular and negative. The particular minor does not license “no S are P,” which would be Celarent’s kind of conclusion with a universal minor that Ferio does not have.

An invented example, for the form only: no whales are insects; some sea animals are whales; therefore some sea animals are not insects.

Do not turn the conclusion into a universal, and do not swap EIO for AII (Darii) or EAE (Celarent).

Remember

Ferio is EIO in the first figure: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P. The conclusion stays particular and negative. It is not Darii (AII) and not Celarent (EAE).
