Latin · Capitulum XIII: the named day, and prīdiē

25 minutes · Method: ablative on the named day itself; prīdiē with the accusative

Learn

Key terms

the ablative of the named day
Definition
The Kalends, Nones, or Ides themselves are expressed in the ablative, with the month adjective agreeing: Kalendīs Mārtiīs, Nonīs Aprīlibus, Īdibus Octōbribus.
Example
Īdibus Mārtiīs means “on the Ides of March,” that is, on 15 March.
Not this
Not the accusative, which is used after prīdiē and in ante-diem phrases, and not a singular ablative *Kalendā. These words stay plural.
Connects to
Allen and Greenough §631 and the usual dating construction: the named day itself is ablative. Prīdiē is taught in the same sitting.
prīdiē
Definition
“On the day before,” used with the accusative of Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs (and the agreeing month): prīdiē Īdūs Mārtiās is 14 March.
Example
prīdiē Kalendās Maiās is 30 April, the day before 1 May.
Not this
Not the named day itself (that is ablative), and not “two days before,” which is a later inclusive count. Prīdiē is only the day immediately before.
Connects to
Tomorrow treats days counted inclusively backward (ante diem). Today stops at the named day and the day before it.

When you mean the named day itself, Latin uses the ablative, and the month agrees as an adjective:

  • Kalendīs Mārtiīs — on the Kalends of March (1 March)
  • Nonīs Mārtiīs — on the Nones of March (7 March)
  • Īdibus Mārtiīs — on the Ides of March (15 March)

The day immediately before a named day is prīdiē, used with the accusative:

  • prīdiē Īdūs Mārtiās — the day before the Ides of March, which is 14 March
  • prīdiē Nonās Aprīlēs — the day before the Nones of April, which is 4 April (the Nonae of April are the 5th)
  • prīdiē Kalendās Maiās — the day before the Kalends of May, which is 30 April

Prīdiē is only that one day immediately before. “Two days before,” counted the Roman way, is tomorrow’s job, not today’s.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXII: the area of a right triangle

40 minutes · Method: A = ½ab when a and b are the legs

Learn

Key terms

area of a right triangle
Definition
In a right triangle the two legs are perpendicular, so either leg may be taken as the base and the other as the height. The area is therefore ½ab, where a and b are the legs.
Example
A 5-12-13 triangle has area ½ × 5 × 12 = 30.
Not this
Not ½ times a leg times the hypotenuse, and not the product of the two legs without the one-half.
Connects to
This is yesterday’s method, stated cleanly for the right-triangle case. It does not replace ½bh for other triangles.

In a right triangle the two legs meet at a right angle. So if one leg is taken as the base, the other leg is already the perpendicular height. The area is

A = ½ab,

where a and b are the legs. The hypotenuse is not used as a factor in this product.

A 3-4-5 triangle has area ½ × 3 × 4 = 6. A 6-8-10 triangle, similar to it and already used yesterday, has area ½ × 6 × 8 = 24, which is 4 times 6, as the scale factor 2 would lead you to expect for area (2² = 4). That check uses similarity from week 13 and the theorem from week 14; it is not a new theorem today.

The formula ½bh is still the general rule. ½ab is that rule in the special case of a right triangle, with the legs as b and h.

Remember

In a right triangle, A = ½ab with a and b the legs. The hypotenuse is not a factor in that product. This is ½bh, with the legs as base and height.

Writing & Rhetoric · The false dilemma

40 minutes · Method: do not treat two options as the only ones when a third remains

Learn

Key terms

false dilemma
Definition
A fallacy in which two options are presented as if they were the only ones, when at least one other genuine option has been left out.
Example
An invented example: “Either this triangle is a 3-4-5 triangle, or it is not a right triangle.” A 5-12-13 triangle is right-angled and is not 3-4-5; a third option was left out.
Not this
Not a genuine either–or, in which the two options really do exhaust the cases (for example, a triangle either is or is not right-angled). Leaving out a real option is the flaw.
Connects to
Last week treated 3-4-5 as one integer triple, not the only right triangle. A false dilemma often hides a third case of that kind.

A false dilemma (sometimes called a false either–or) is a fallacy in which two options are presented as if they were the only ones, when at least one other genuine option has been left out.

Physics · Finding a center of mass by two suspensions

40 minutes · Method: two plumb lines from two hanging-points; the crossing is the center of mass

Learn

Key terms

locating a center of mass by suspension
Definition
A method for finding the center of mass of a flat object: hang it from one point, mark the vertical plumb line through that point; hang it from a second point, mark that second plumb line; the center of mass lies where the two lines meet.
Example
An invented card of uneven shape, hung from a pin at one corner and then from another, has two plumb lines drawn on it; their crossing is treated as the center of mass of the card.
Not this
Not a single hanging, which only gives one line that the center of mass lies on, and not a claim that the crossing is an official laboratory measurement of a named object.
Connects to
Last week the center of mass was a balance point, with mass × distance on a light bar. Today is a method for locating that point on a flat card. The idea of a vertical line is the same family as last week’s line from the center of mass.

Last week the center of mass of a light bar was the balance point at which the mass × distance products agreed. Today is a method for locating that point on a flat object, such as a card.

Hang the object from a pin, so that it can swing freely, and let it come to rest. A plumb line (a vertical line from the pin, in an invented classroom picture a thread with a small weight) marks one line that the center of mass lies on. The card in rest hangs so that its center of mass is vertically below the pin.

One line is not enough to name the point. Hang the same object from a second point, and mark a second plumb line. The center of mass lies on that line too, so it lies at the crossing.

The card and the pin positions are invented demonstrations, labeled invented. The method does not invent an official mass in kilograms, and it does not replace last week’s mass × distance rule for a bar.

The bowl, hill, table, and hanging card in this week’s physics are invented classroom demonstrations, labeled invented. They are for the method, not official laboratory measurements. Last week’s base of support is used, not retaught as a first look.

Remember

Hang a flat object from one point and mark the vertical plumb line; hang it from a second point and mark that line; the center of mass is at the crossing. One hanging gives only a line, not the point.

So the church has become a spectacle of marvellous beauty, overwhelming to those who see it, but to those who know it by hearsay altogether incredible. Atelier · Value on the nose, as a wedge

90 minutes · Method: front plane and side plane of the already-placed nose, in the same light

Learn

Key terms

value on the nose, as a wedge
Definition
The already-placed nose, treated as a wedge, takes different values on its front plane and its side plane, with a terminator at the turn, according to the same light used on the sockets.
Example
If the front plane faces the light, it stays lighter; the side plane turns away and goes down in value.
Not this
Not a new placement of the nose, not a central ridge drawn as a line down the middle with no planes, and not a single flat tone over the whole wedge.
Connects to
Last week’s card was the nose as a wedge. Today is the value of those planes. The sockets, already valued yesterday, stay in place.

Do not move the nose. Do not restart the sockets. The value pass continues across forms that are already there.

Ser/ir share fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fuisteis, fueron. Hacer uses hice, hiciste, hizo, hicimos, hicisteis, hicieron. Fue and hizo are different verbs; hice is yo, hizo is él/ella/usted.

The z belongs to hizo (él/ella/usted), not to a made-up *hizieron.

Elective · Art history: light and marble in the church, from Dewing I.1.29–30

25 minutes · Method: a building described in words of light, without invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

light and marble in the church (Procopius I.1.29–30)
Definition
Procopius’s description, in Dewing I.1.29–30, of the church as abounding “in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble,” so that the radiance might seem to come into being within it.
Example
The set art-history extract uses those words as a building described in light, not as a numbered lighting study.
Not this
Not an official luminance, window-count, or plan measurement, and not a reteaching of last week’s fire and rebuilding as a first look.

History this Wednesday reads Buildings I.1.27–30 in Dewing, including height “to match the sky” and the proportion of breadth and length, still without numbers. This seminar stays with the light.

The set extract, I.1.29–30, includes:

and it abounds exceedingly in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble. Indeed one might say that its interior is not illuminated from without by the sun, but that the radiance comes into being within it, such an abundance of light bathes this shrine.

The church “abounds exceedingly in sunlight and in the reflection of the sun’s rays from the marble.” Dewing’s speaker suggests that “the radiance comes into being within it.” The description is in words: sunlight, reflection from marble, an abundance of light. This lesson invents no window-count, no height, and no plan. A later editor’s plan on the LacusCurtius page is not used as a measurement.
