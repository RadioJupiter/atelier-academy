Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes the week: a triangle as half a rectangle; A = ½bh with h perpendicular; a height found by the Pythagorean theorem; A = ½ab for a right triangle; and an isosceles triangle whose altitude bisects the base. Circle area remains reserved. No new formula is begun.

Remember

A = ½bh, with h perpendicular. In a right triangle, A = ½ab with a and b the legs. In an isosceles triangle, the altitude to the base bisects the base; find h from a² + b² = c², then use ½bh.

Watch me

  1. 6-8-10: A = ½ × 6 × 8 = 24. 5-5-6: h = 4, A = ½ × 6 × 4 = 12.

Together

13 is the hypotenuse.

Hint 2

The legs 5 and 12 are perpendicular; A = ½ × 5 × 12 = 30.

Check

A short mix of ½bh, ½ab, and an isosceles height.

  1. A triangle has base 10 and perpendicular height 9. What is its area?
  2. A rectangle 8 by 5 is divided by a diagonal. What is the area of one of the triangles?

Exercises

  1. An isosceles triangle has equal sides 10 and 10 and base 12. Find h and the area.

The bowl, hill, table, and hanging card in this week’s physics are invented classroom demonstrations, labeled invented. They are for the method, not official laboratory measurements. Last week’s base of support is used, not retaught as a first look.

Remember

Stable: returns after a small nudge. Unstable: runs away after a small nudge. Neutral: rests in the new nearby place. A center of mass on a flat card is at the crossing of two plumb lines from two hangings.

Watch me

A marble at the top of a hill, slightly nudged, rolls down. Name the kind of equilibrium, and say what a second invented marble at the bottom of a bowl would be called.

Together

Hint 1

One plumb line is only a line of possible points.

Hint 2

A second hanging supplies a second line; the crossing is the point.

Check

A short mix of the three names and the two-hanging method.

  1. A marble on a flat, level table is moved a little and stays there. Which kind of equilibrium is that?
  2. Where do you mark the center of mass after two suspensions?

Exercises

  1. Why is “at rest” not, by itself, enough to name the kind of equilibrium?
  2. Why must the two hanging-points be different?

Elective · Anatomy: the hand — carpus, metacarpals, and phalanges

25 minutes · Method: the wrist as a group, the palm, and the bones of the digits, palm-forward

Learn

Key terms

carpus
Definition
The wrist, treated in this lesson as a group of short bones between the forearm and the metacarpals, not as a list of eight names to be memorized today.
Origin
Borrowed from New Latin carpus, from Ancient Greek καρπός (karpós), “wrist.”
Example
In a palm-forward view, the carpus sits just beyond the distal ends of the radius and the ulna, already named last week.
Not this
Not a metacarpal, and not a phalanx. The eight individual carpal names remain reserved; today’s job is the group.
Connects to
Last week named the arm: humerus, radius, and ulna, palm-forward. Today continues into the hand, still palm-forward. The word is borrowed from New Latin carpus, from Ancient Greek καρπός, “wrist,” as the English Wiktionary entry states.
metacarpals
Definition
The long bones of the palm, lying between the carpus and the phalanges; one metacarpal leads toward each digit.
Example
In a palm-forward view, the five metacarpals form the body of the palm, distal to the carpus.
Not this
Not the carpus (the wrist group), and not the phalanges (the bones of the digits). “Meta-” here marks that they come after the carpus, not that they are a second wrist.
Connects to
They continue last week’s palm-forward view from the radius (thumb side) and the ulna (little-finger side).
phalanges of the hand
Definition
The bones of the digits: each finger has three phalanges (proximal, middle, and distal); the thumb has two (proximal and distal).
Example
In a palm-forward view, the thumb, on the radius side, shows two phalanges; the other four digits show three each.
Not this
Not a metacarpal, and not a first look at a battle-array sense of the older word “phalanx.” Today’s job is the bones of the digits of the hand.
Connects to
The English word phalanx is borrowed from Latin phalanx or Ancient Greek φάλαγξ, “battle order; array,” as Wiktionary states; the origin of the anatomical sense is left without a further claim, rather than guessed. The lesson uses “phalanges of the hand” as the name of these bones.

Last week named the arm, palm-forward: humerus; radius on the thumb side; ulna on the little-finger side. Today continues into the hand, still palm-forward.

Three groups, not a first list of every small name:

  • The carpus is the wrist, treated today as a group of short bones between the forearm and the palm. The eight individual carpal names remain reserved.
  • The metacarpals are the long bones of the palm; one metacarpal leads toward each digit.
  • The phalanges are the bones of the digits. Each of the four fingers has three (proximal, middle, distal); the thumb has two (proximal and distal).

The thumb sits on the radius side, already taught. This lesson does not invent official lengths in centimeters, and it does not begin the muscles of the hand.

Remember

Palm-forward: carpus (the wrist, as a group), metacarpals (the palm), phalanges (the digits). The thumb, on the radius side, has two phalanges; the other four digits have three each. The eight carpal names remain reserved.

Key terms: carpus, metacarpals, phalanges of the hand.

Vocabulary review

15–20 min · Spaced review of weeks 1–15 terms · Use your own words; then check the key

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the area of a triangle, and why is “½ times base times a slanted side” a miss?
  2. The area is ½bh, where h is the perpendicular height to the chosen base. A triangle is half of a rectangle with the same base and the same perpendicular height.
  3. A slanted side is not h, because it does not meet the base at a right angle. A 6-8-10 right triangle has area ½ × 6 × 8 = 24; the hypotenuse 10 is not the height to a leg.
  4. A near-miss is to drop the one-half (area 48 for that triangle), or to use 5 as the height of a 5-5-6 isosceles triangle instead of finding h = 4.

Together

In your own words, what is a fallacy, and how is it different from a merely false claim?

Hint 1: The reasoning

A fallacy is a flaw in the reasoning: the support does not establish the claim in the way the argument pretends.

Hint 2: True or false

A conclusion can be true and the route still fallacious, and a claim can be false without this week’s named patterns.
