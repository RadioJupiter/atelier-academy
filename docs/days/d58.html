Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Word How to say it Meaning
Puer est. Puella eum videt POO-ehr est. PWEH-lah EH-um WEE-det There is a boy. The girl sees him
Cūr puella rīdet? koor PWEH-lah REE-det Why does the girl laugh?

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Puer est. Puella eum videt. eum is accusative: the boy is the one seen, already named. Puella est. Puer eam videt. eam points back to the girl, as object.

Cūr puella rīdet? Puella rīdet, quia rosam habet. cūr asks why; quia gives the reason. quia is not sed (contrast) and not et (a join).

We still stay in Capitula I–X; we do not begin a relative clause today.

Remember

is / ea point back; eum / eam are the accusative forms. cūr asks why; quia gives a reason.

Mathematics · Composition, and a line with a parabola

40 min · Review: composition, and a line and a parabola solved by substitution · check every point in both equations

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • composition of functions — Day 41
  • iteration of a function — Day 46
  • a line and a parabola, as a system — Day 48

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Two algebra methods you already know, used again today.

Composition. (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f to that result. Iteration is composition of a function with itself: f(f(x)), also written (f ∘ f)(x). It is not the product f(x) · f(x). The inverse is a different job: it undoes f.

A line and a parabola, as a system. Substitute the line into the parabola (or the other way when it is easier). Solve the resulting equation. Then check each candidate in both original equations. A point that fits only one equation is not a solution of the system.

A worked system. y = x² and y = x + 2. Then x² = x + 2, so x² − x − 2 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0, x = 2 or x = −1. The points are (2, 4) and (−1, 1). Check: 2² = 4 and 2 + 2 = 4; (−1)² = 1 and −1 + 2 = 1. Both hold.

Keep this picture separate from a quadratic inequality (which asks where a graph is above or below the axis) and from III.36.

Remember

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). f(f(x)) is iteration, not a product. A line and a parabola: substitute, solve, check both equations.

Watch me

If f(x) = x + 1 and g(x) = 2x, find (f ∘ g)(3) and (g ∘ f)(3), and say why they need not be equal.

  1. (f ∘ g)(3) = f(6) = 7.
  2. (g ∘ f)(3) = g(4) = 8.
  3. Composition is not automatically commutative. The order is part of the method.

Together

Solve the system y = x² and y = 2x by substitution, and check the points.

Hint 1: Substitute

x² = 2x, so x² − 2x = 0, x(x − 2) = 0, x = 0 or x = 2.

Hint 2: Check

(0, 0): 0 = 0 and 0 = 0. (2, 4): 4 = 4 and 2 · 2 = 4. Both hold.

Check

Use the two methods from this lesson.

  1. If f(x) = 3x, what is f(f(2)), and why is the answer not 6 · 6?
  2. The line y = 6 and the parabola y = x²: find the points, and check them.

Exercises

  1. Solve y = x² and y = x + 6, and check both points.
  2. Why is a check required, not optional?
  3. How is this system different from the inequality x² < x + 2?

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin’s hints

40 min · Franklin’s hints, then a check against the set pine extract only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • Franklin’s method, with hints from the set extract — Day 53
  • Franklin's method — Day 4
  • the pine-tree cloud — Day 53

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Franklin’s method: make short hints from a set extract; write from the hints (extract closed); then open the extract and mark what you preserved, what you dropped, and what you invented.

Set extract, still attributed as yesterday:

Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified).

Hints, not the lines. Watchers at some distance; the mountain not at first known; afterwards, Vesuvius; a likeness to a pine-tree; a long, high trunk, then a spread of branches.

Write from the hints first. Then check. Inventing Pompeii, a death toll, or the reserved voyage is a miss: those are not in the set extract.

Remember

Hints from the set extract; write; then check. Mark preserved, dropped, and invented. Do not add a reserved later scene.

  1. Hints in view: watchers at some distance; the mountain not at first known; afterwards, Vesuvius.
  2. A reconstruction might be: People watching from some way off could not tell which mountain the cloud rose from; it was later found to be Vesuvius.
  3. Check against Firth. Mark “from some distance,” “afterwards discovered,” and “Mount Vesuvius” if you dropped them; do not add Pompeii.

Together

Finish the reconstruction for the pine likeness, then list one wording you dropped and one thing you must not add.

Hint 1: The likeness

A trunk of great length and height, then a spread of branches, like a pine-tree.

Hint 2: The check

  2. Write the attribution you still owe after the reconstruction.

Physics · Weight from g, for a labeled invented mass

40 min · Review: W = m g · invented masses labeled · g from figures you already have

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • weight — Day 38
  • weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass — Day 53
  • g on Mars, from the same formula — Day 51

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Weight is the force W = m g, in newtons when m is in kilograms and g is in m/s² (or N/kg). Mass is a different job: the amount of matter, in kilograms, which does not change just because the place changes in this course’s model.

Every mass in the exercises is a labeled invented classroom number, not a measured object. The values of g are the ones you already have: Earth 9.8, the Moon about 1.6, Mars about 3.7, each in m/s².

A worked invented example. A labeled invented mass of 2 kg on Mars: W = 2 × 3.7 = 7.4 N. The same mass on Earth would be 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N. The mass is still 2 kg in both places, in this model.

We will not invent a g, and we will not invent an orbital speed.

Remember

W = m g. Mass is a different job. Invented masses stay labeled. Use g you already have: 9.8, about 1.6, about 3.7. No invented orbital speed.

Watch me

A labeled invented mass of 3 kg on the Moon: find the weight, using about 1.6 m/s².

  1. W = 3 × 1.6 = 4.8 N.
  2. The 3 kg is invented and labeled. The 1.6 is the Moon figure you already have.
  3. The mass is still 3 kg, in this model.

Together

The same invented 3 kg on Mars: find the weight, and compare it with the Moon result.

Hint 1: Mars’s g

About 3.7 m/s².

Hint 2: The comparison

W = 3 × 3.7 = 11.1 N, larger than the 4.8 N on the Moon, still for the same mass.

Check

Use W = m g and the values of g from this lesson.

  1. A labeled invented mass of 5 kg on Earth: find the weight.
  2. Why is “the mass on Mars is 3.7 kg” a miss in this lesson?

Exercises

  1. A labeled invented mass of 4 kg: find the weight on Earth and on Mars.
  2. Why must the mass be labeled invented in these exercises?

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • two cast shadows, one lamp — Day 53
  • a few accents, and a stop — Day 54

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

A cast shadow is thrown on another surface when the form blocks the lamp. It is not the form’s own dark plane. Under one lamp, the two shadows lean the same way. Neither needs to be a hole in the paper.

Then a few accents, and a stop. Two or three darker or lighter notes are enough: a slightly darker contact, or a slightly cleaner light. Friday’s examination needs a page that still shows planes, a turn, and agreeing shadows. Polishing the cube into a soft ball, or adding a face to the sphere, is not a finish this course is asking for.

Still not a head.

Remember

Two shadows, one lamp, same direction. A few accents, then a stop. Still not a head. Do not invent a hole in the paper or a face on the sphere.

Art history · Later Flavian buildings, from Platner

30 min · Review of two Platner and Ashby entries · later buildings, not photographs

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum) — Day 53
  • Arch of Titus, a later monument — Day 48

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Two Platner and Ashby (1929) entries have already been taught, as presented on LacusCurtius. Today you gather the habit, not a third building.

The Arch of Titus: a later commemoration of a siege, not finished and dedicated until after Titus’s death (Platner, citing CIL VI.945). In 1822 it was taken down and rebuilt by Valadier, so a modern view is not simply the Flavian fabric.

The Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum): built by Vespasian on the site of the stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea; carried to the second arcade and dedicated before his death in 79; Titus added the third and fourth stories, and Platner places the dedication of the enlarged building in 80, with games lasting one hundred days (Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among other authorities). This course cites Platner, and notes that citation; it does not pretend to have read Suetonius independently.

Neither building is a photograph of 70 or of 79. This sitting will not invent a seating capacity that has not been quoted from Platner here.

Remember

Arch of Titus: later commemoration, dedicated after Titus’s death; 1822 Valadier rebuild. Colosseum: Vespasian to the second arcade, dedicated before his death in 79; Titus adds stories; enlarged dedication in 80 (Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among others). Later buildings, not photographs.

Watch me

  1. To the top of the second arcade of the outer wall, dedicated before his death in 79.
  2. Titus added the third and fourth stories.
  3. The dedication of the enlarged building is placed in 80, with games that lasted one hundred days.

Hint 1: Dedication

Hint 2: A modern view

  2. How does this course handle the hundred-day games?

Exercises

  1. Why are these two buildings not photographs of 70 or 79?
  2. Why will this sitting not invent a seating number?
  3. A short because / but / so about 79 and 80 at the Colosseum.
