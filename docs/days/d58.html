Latin · is, ea, and quia: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught is / ea / eum / eam, and cūr / quia; a close, not a first look; Capitula I–X only

Learn

Key terms

is, ea, and quia, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of already-taught pointing words and a reason-word: is / ea (and the accusative eum / eam) point back to a person already named, and quia gives a reason, often after cūr. They were reviewed last week; today gathers them for the term, still in Capitula I–X.
Example

Not this

Mathematics · Composition, and a line with a parabola: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught composition and substitution, gathered; check every point in both equations

Learn

Key terms

composition, and a line with a parabola, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of two already-taught algebra methods: composition (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), including iteration f(f(x)), which is not a product; and a line and a parabola as a system, solved by substitution, with every candidate point checked in both equations.
Example
If f(x) = x + 1 and g(x) = 2x, then (f ∘ g)(3) = f(6) = 7. If y = x² and y = x + 2, substitution gives x² − x − 2 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 2 or x = −1; the points (2, 4) and (−1, 1) must be checked in both.
Not this
Not f(x) · f(x) for f(f(x)), not a guess of a point without a check, and not Euclid III.36.
Connects to
Weeks 9–10 taught composition, iteration, and a line with a parabola. Today gathers those methods for the term.

Two already-taught algebra methods, gathered. They are not new today.

Composition. (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f to that result. Iteration is composition of a function with itself: f(f(x)), also written (f ∘ f)(x). It is not the product f(x) · f(x). The inverse, already taught as a different job, undoes f.

A line and a parabola, as a system. Substitute the line into the parabola (or the other way when it is easier). Solve the resulting equation. Then check each candidate in both original equations. A point that fits only one equation is not a solution of the system.

A worked system, in this course’s already-taught shape. y = x² and y = x + 2. Then x² = x + 2, so x² − x − 2 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0, x = 2 or x = −1. The points are (2, 4) and (−1, 1). Check: 2² = 4 and 2 + 2 = 4; (−1)² = 1 and −1 + 2 = 1. Both hold.

Keep this picture separate from a quadratic inequality (which asks where a graph is above or below the axis) and from III.36.

Remember

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). f(f(x)) is iteration, not a product. A line and a parabola: substitute, solve, check both equations. Not III.36.

Key terms: composition, and a line with a parabola, a Term 1 close.

Watch me

  2. (g ∘ f)(3) = g(4) = 8.
  3. Composition is not automatically commutative. The order is part of the method.

Physics · Weight from g, for a labeled invented mass: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught W = m g; invented masses labeled; g from already-sourced figures

Learn

Key terms

weight from g, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the already-taught weight W = m g, using a labeled invented mass and an already-sourced g (Earth 9.8, Moon about 1.6, or Mars about 3.7, each in m/s²). The mass is invented and labeled; the g is not. Weight is not the same job as mass.
Example
A labeled invented mass of 2 kg on Mars: W = 2 × 3.7 = 7.4 N, using about 3.7 m/s² already taught.
Not this
Not an invented g, not an invented orbital speed, and not a claim that the invented mass is a measured object.
Connects to
Week 11 used W = m g on Mars with labeled invented masses. Weight as a force, and mass as a different job, were already taught earlier. Today gathers them for the term.

Weight, already taught, is the force W = m g, in newtons when m is in kilograms and g is in m/s² (or N/kg). Mass is a different job: the amount of matter, in kilograms, which does not change just because the place changes in this course’s model.

Today is a close of that method. Every mass in the exercises is a labeled invented classroom number, not a measured object. The values of g are the ones already sourced: Earth 9.8, the Moon about 1.6, Mars about 3.7, each in m/s².

A worked invented example. A labeled invented mass of 2 kg on Mars: W = 2 × 3.7 = 7.4 N. The same mass on Earth would be 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N. The mass is still 2 kg in both places, in this model.

We will not invent a g, and we will not invent an orbital speed.

Remember

W = m g. Mass is a different job. Invented masses stay labeled. Use already-sourced g: 9.8, about 1.6, about 3.7. No invented orbital speed.

Learn

Key terms

two shadows and a few accents, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of two already-taught late-sitting habits on the same group: two cast shadows that agree with one lamp, then a few accents and a stop, so Friday’s examination has a finished-enough page rather than an overworked one. Still not a head.
Example
A slightly darker contact where a form meets its shadow, or a slightly cleaner light on a light plane, then a stop.

Connects to
Week 11 taught the shadows, the accents, and the stop on this group. Today gathers them for the term.

Still not a head.

Art history · Later Flavian buildings, from Platner: a Term 1 close

30 min · Method: two already-taught Platner and Ashby entries, gathered; later buildings, not photographs

Learn

Key terms

later Flavian buildings, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of two already-taught Platner and Ashby (1929) entries: the Arch of Titus, a later commemoration dedicated after Titus’s death, with an 1822 Valadier rebuild; and the Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum), carried by Vespasian to the second arcade and dedicated before his death in 79, with Titus adding the third and fourth stories and an enlarged dedication in 80. Both are later buildings, not photographs of 70 or 79.
Example
Platner on the amphitheater: Vespasian “dedicated it before his death in 79 A.D.” Titus “added the third and fourth stories” and “celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with magnificent games that lasted one hundred days” (Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among others).

Two Platner and Ashby (1929) entries have already been taught, as presented on LacusCurtius. Today gathers the habit, not a third building.

The Arch of Titus, already taught: a later commemoration of a siege, not finished and dedicated until after Titus’s death (Platner, citing CIL VI.945). In 1822 it was taken down and rebuilt by Valadier, so a modern view is not simply the Flavian fabric.

The Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum), already taught: built by Vespasian on the site of the stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea; carried to the second arcade and dedicated before his death in 79; Titus added the third and fourth stories, and Platner places the dedication of the enlarged building in 80, with games lasting one hundred days (Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among other authorities). This course cites Platner, and notes that citation; it does not pretend to have read Suetonius independently.

Neither building is a photograph of 70 or of 79. This sitting will not invent a seating capacity that has not been quoted from Platner here.

Remember

Arch of Titus: later commemoration, dedicated after Titus’s death; 1822 Valadier rebuild. Colosseum: Vespasian to the second arcade, dedicated before his death in 79; Titus adds stories; enlarged dedication in 80 (Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7, among others). Later buildings, not photographs.
