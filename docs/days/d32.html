Latin · Capitulum IX, part 2: the accusative of the new family

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. IX · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

third-declension accusative
Definition
For many third-declension nouns, the accusative singular adds -em to the stem (the stem is seen in the genitive: rēg- from rēgis, so rēgem; vōc- from vōcis, so vōcem).
Example
Rēx canem videt. The king sees the dog. canem is accusative singular, the object.
Not this
Not the genitive rēgis, and not a first-declension -am (that is puellam). Do not write *rēxam or *canis as the object if the sentence needs “the dog” as object.
Connects to
Yesterday’s nominative and genitive stay. Today the object form of the same family is added, still with original sentences.
Word How to say it Meaning
rēgem RAY-gem king (accusative singular)
vōcem WOH-kem voice (accusative singular)
canem KAH-nem dog (accusative singular)
Rēx canem videt rayks KAH-nem WEE-det The king sees the dog
Canis rēgem videt KAH-nis RAY-gem WEE-det The dog sees the king

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

The stem comes from the genitive: rēgis → rēg- + -em = rēgem. Original sentences: Rēx canem videt. Canis rēgem videt. Puella vōcem rēgis audit.

For the guide: original sentences only. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

Accusative singular of many third-declension nouns: stem + -em (rēgem, vōcem, canem). The stem is taken from the genitive. Nominative and genitive from yesterday stay.

Key terms: third-declension accusative.

Mathematics · Algebra IX: graphs of linear functions

60 min · Method: a table of values, then a straight graph; slope as rise over run

Learn

Key terms

linear function
Definition
A function whose graph is a straight line. In the form f(x) = mx + b, b is the y-intercept (the output when x = 0), and m is the slope (the rise over the run between any two points on the line).
Example
f(x) = 2x + 1 has intercept 1 and slope 2: from (0, 1) to (2, 5) the run is 2 and the rise is 4, and 4/2 = 2.
Not this
Not every graph is a line (tomorrow’s parabola is not). And m is not automatically the y-intercept; that job belongs to b in this form.
Connects to
Yesterday’s f(x) is what we plot. A table of inputs and outputs becomes points; a line through those points is the graph of a linear function.
slope
Definition
The constant rate of change of a linear function: rise over run, (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), between any two points on the line.
Example
On y = 2x + 1, from (0, 1) to (1, 3) the slope is (3−1)/(1−0) = 2, the same m.
Not this
Not the intercept, and not a turn in degrees (that was an angle). A vertical line would have an undefined slope; we do not need that case today.
Connects to
The same m appears in the formula f(x) = mx + b and in the rise-over-run calculation. That is a check, not a coincidence.

To graph a linear function, make a short table, plot the points, and draw the line through them. For f(x) = 2x + 1:

x f(x)
0 1
1 3
2 5

The point (0, 1) sits on the y-axis: that is the intercept b = 1. From (0, 1) to (2, 5) the run is 2 and the rise is 4, so the slope m is 4/2 = 2, which matches the 2 in 2x + 1.

A first warning. Two points already determine a straight line; a third point is a check. If a plotted point misses the line, recompute the table before “bending” the graph.

Remember

Linear: graph is a straight line. f(x) = mx + b: b is the intercept at x = 0; m is slope, rise over run. A table becomes points; a third point checks the line.

Key terms: linear function, slope.

Watch me

Graph y = 2x + 1 from the table above. What is the slope between (0, 1) and (2, 5), and why should it equal 2?

  1. Run 2, rise 4, slope 4/2 = 2.
  2. The 2 is the m in 2x + 1.
  3. The point (1, 3) should also lie on the same line: that is the check.

Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: Bacon, “Of Studies,” the opening series

45 min · Method: imitatio from a public-domain model (Francis Bacon, “Of Studies,” 1625); a short attributed quotation only

Learn

Key terms

imitatio of a series
Definition
Imitatio here means writing a new sentence that keeps the grammatical shape of a model — today, a three-part series — without copying the model’s content or lifting a long passage.
Example
Bacon’s opening (1625), in a short attributed quotation, is: “Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability.” A new sentence might read: “A graph serves for a table, for a check, and for a picture of the rule.” The shape is three “for” phrases; the content is new.
Not this
Not copying the sentence and changing one noun, and not a paraphrase presented as your own without attribution of the model. The quotation itself stays short and named.
Connects to
Week 5 already used parallelism in a series, and a different public-domain model (the Declaration). Today a new model, one sentence, attributed.

Francis Bacon’s essay “Of Studies” (1625) is in the public domain. We use one short attributed sentence as a model of form, not as a claim you must agree with about study:

“Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability.”

The shape is a verb, then a three-part series of for-phrases, with and before the last. Your imitatio keeps that shape and changes the content. You must name Bacon and the year when you quote the model; you must not quote more of the essay than this opening unless a later lesson sets a new, still short, passage.

Remember

Quote the model in full attribution (Bacon, “Of Studies,” 1625). Write a new three-part for-series with the same shape and different content. Do not lift a longer passage.

Science · How we see: the eye as a kind of camera

50 min · Method: a labeled picture (cornea, pupil, lens, retina) and a careful analogy, not a measured optical bench

Learn

Key terms

cornea
Definition
The clear front surface of the eye, which begins the bending of incoming light. The name comes from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn,” for a tough, horn-like tissue — not because the eye is made of horn.
Example
Light from a lamp first meets the cornea, then the pupil and the lens. The cornea does much of the eye’s bending of light; this lesson does not assign a measured focal length to it.
Not this
Not the colored iris, and not the retina at the back. The cornea is at the front, clear, and in contact with the outside air (under the tear film).
Connects to
The camera analogy: the cornea and lens together play a role like a camera’s lens; the analogy is a first map, not a claim that the eye is a manufactured camera.
retina
Definition
The light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, where an image is formed and a signal begins. The name comes from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.”
Example
Light that has been bent by the cornea and lens reaches the retina. We do not invent a count of cells, or a measured focal length, in this lesson.
Not this
Not the pupil (an opening), and not the optic nerve itself (the path of the signal leaving the eye — a first name today, treated more carefully on Thursday).
Connects to
The camera analogy: the retina is like a film or a sensor, a place where the pattern of light is received. It is living tissue, not a manufactured film.

A first map of the eye, by analogy with a camera, and then the limits of the analogy:

  • Light enters through the cornea (clear front), then the pupil (an opening in the iris), then the lens.
  • An image is formed on the retina at the back.
  • A signal then leaves toward the brain (Thursday’s lesson names the path more carefully).

The analogy helps you remember the order. It does not give you a measured focal length, a shutter speed, or a count of receptor cells. This course will not invent those numbers.

Remember

Order: cornea, pupil, lens, retina. The cornea begins the bending of light; the retina receives the image. The camera analogy is a map, not a set of measurements.

Key terms: cornea, retina.

Watch me

Atelier · Hair as masses, not as strands first

50 min · Method: large hair masses from the plate or plaster, value first, strands later if at all

Learn

Key terms

hair as mass
Definition
Hair is first drawn or painted as a few large shapes of light and dark that sit on the skull, not as a collection of single hairs.
Example
A dark mass over the crown, a lighter mass where the light hits, and a simpler edge against the background will read as hair before any strand is marked.
Not this
Not a net of lines drawn one hair at a time from the start, and not a copied ‘hairstyle’ that ignores this head’s skull and the light on the plate.
Connects to
The ear, already placed, is often partly covered or framed by a mass of hair. Keep the ear’s placement; let the mass overlap it honestly.

Squint. Group the hair into two or three masses of value. Draw or lay in those masses as shapes on the skull. Only after the masses sit well may you suggest a few strands at an edge or a parting — and only if the plate shows them.

If you begin with strands, you will usually lose the skull and the light. The plate wins if a ‘style’ in your head disagrees with what you see.

Remember

Hair is two or three value masses on the skull first. Strands, if any, come after. The plate wins. Do not bury the ear’s placement under an invented style.

Key terms: hair as mass.

Watch me

  2. The lightest and darkest masses named (for example, highlight on the crown; dark in the parting or the far side).
  3. The ear still findable, even if a mass overlaps it.

Together

Modern language · Spanish: the present of ir, dar, ver, and hacer

40 min · Method: four irregular presents, contrasted with yesterday’s ir a + infinitive; English remains the language of instruction

Learn

Key terms

present of ir, dar, ver, hacer
Definition
Four high-frequency irregular presents, taught as a set today: ir (voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van); dar (doy, das, da, damos, dais, dan); ver (veo, ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven); hacer (hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen).
Example
Voy a la clase. Doy el libro. Veo la mesa. Hago la tarea. (tarea may be used here as ‘homework / the task.’)
Not this
Not *yo va, *yo do, *yo vo, or *yo haco. And still not a past tense (fui, di, vi, hice are not today’s forms).
Connects to
Yesterday used voy / vas / va / vamos / vais / van inside ir a + infinitive. Today those forms are learned as the verb ir itself, with three companions.
Word How to say it Meaning
voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van BOY, BAHS, BAH, BAH-mohs, BICE, BAHN I go, you go, he/she goes, we go, you (pl.) go, they go
doy, das, da, damos, dais, dan DOY, DAHS, DAH, DAH-mohs, DICE, DAHN I give, you give, he/she gives, we give, you (pl.) give, they give
veo, ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven BEH-oh, BEHS, BEH, BEH-mohs, BAYSS, BEHN I see, you see, he/she sees, we see, you (pl.) see, they see
hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen AH-go, AH-ses, AH-seh, ah-SEH-mohs, ah-SEH-ees, AH-sen I do/make, you do, he/she does, we do, you (pl.) do, they do

Four presents, irregular in the way shown. Learn them as sets, then use them in short original sentences. Hacer has hago in the yo form; ver has veo; dar has doy; ir has voy. Do not invent a regular -o on those yo forms.

Original sentences: Voy a la mesa. Doy un libro a Ana. Veo dos clases. ¿Qué haces? Hacemos la tarea.

Key terms: present of ir, dar, ver, hacer.

Music theory · Intervals: the perfect fifth and the major third

40 min · Method: two named intervals from a major scale already taught, sung or played, then written

Learn

Key terms

interval
Definition
The distance in pitch between two notes. The word comes from Latin intervallum (a space between, from inter, “between,” and vallum, “rampart”). In this lesson an interval is named by a number (a third, a fifth) counted along the notes of a scale, inclusive of both ends.
Example
From C up to G is a fifth (C, D, E, F, G: five letter names). From C up to E is a third (C, D, E).
Not this
Not the everyday sense of a break in time, and not a claim that every fifth is the same size in every tuning system. Today we name two intervals in a major scale as this course has used it.
Connects to
Last week’s I, IV, and V already used the first, fourth, and fifth degrees of a major scale. Today we name the distance from the first degree to the third, and to the fifth.
perfect fifth and major third (first look)
Definition
In a major scale, the interval from the first degree to the fifth is a perfect fifth (for example, C up to G). The interval from the first degree to the third is a major third (C up to E). These are first names; other qualities (minor, diminished, augmented) are not taught as a full system today.
Example
In C major, C–G is a perfect fifth, and C–E is a major third. A first authentic cadence last week moved V to I; the fifth of the scale is in that V chord, but today’s job is to name the intervals, not to redo the cadence.
Not this
Not “any two notes five letter-names apart are always a perfect fifth in every context,” if an accidental has changed the size — a caution named, not a full accidentals lesson. In the unaltered major scale taught here, 1-to-5 is a perfect fifth and 1-to-3 is a major third.
Connects to
Count inclusively: C to G is C, D, E, F, G — a fifth, not a skip of five steps beyond G.

Count letter names inclusively along a major scale you already know (C major is a fair classroom default):

  • C–E: a major third (three letter names: C, D, E).
  • C–G: a perfect fifth (five letter names: C, D, E, F, G).

Sing or play them if you can (C–E, C–G), then write them. This lesson does not give a full table of minor and diminished intervals, and it does not invent a beat-frequency or a measured frequency ratio as a classroom “proof.” The names are the point, on the scale you have.

Remember

An interval is a distance in pitch. In a major scale, 1-to-3 is a major third; 1-to-5 is a perfect fifth. Count letter names inclusively. No full table of other qualities today.

Key terms: interval, perfect fifth and major third (first look).

Watch me

In C major, name C–E and C–G, and show the inclusive counts.

  1. C–E is a major third (C, D, E).
  2. C–G is a perfect fifth (C, D, E, F, G).
  3. The counts include both ends.
