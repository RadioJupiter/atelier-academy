Latin · The future of sequor, a first look

40 min · Method: sequar, sequēris, sequētur; original sentences, not Ørberg

Learn

Key terms

future of sequor
Definition
The future of sequor is a deponent future of the third conjugation: sequar, sequēris, sequētur, sequēmur, sequēminī, sequentur, I shall follow, you will follow, and so on. It is not the present sequor, and it is not secūtus eram.
Example
Puer mē sequētur: he will follow me. The following is still ahead.
Not this
Not sequor, I follow. Not secūtus eram, I had followed. Not loquar, I shall speak, which is the future of loquor.
Connects to
Last week’s present and pluperfect of sequor, and any already-taught deponent future of loquor.

Last week the present of sequor was sequor, sequeris, sequitur, and the pluperfect was secūtus eram. Today the future of the same verb. Sequor is a deponent of the third conjugation, so its future is sequar, I shall follow; sequēris, you will follow; sequētur, he or she will follow; sequēmur, we shall follow; sequēminī, you (pl.) will follow; sequentur, they will follow. Ego tē sequar marks the following as still ahead, not as a present act and not as a completed past. Loquar would be I shall speak, from loquor, not a form of sequor. Ēgrediar, the future of ēgredior, waits until tomorrow. Sentences are original, not copied from Ørberg.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXIX: the law of cosines, a first look

40 min · Method: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, in degrees; invented figures labeled invented

Learn

Key terms

the law of cosines
Definition
The law of cosines says that in any triangle, c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, where c is the side opposite angle C, and a and b are the other two sides. When C is 90°, cos 90° = 0, and the formula becomes c² = a² + b², the already-taught Pythagorean theorem. It is not the law of sines.
Example
On an invented triangle with sides 5 and 5 and included angle 60°, c² = 25 + 25 − 2·5·5·(1/2) = 25, so c = 5. The figures 5, 5, and 60° are invented classroom numbers.
Not this
Not the law of sines, a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Not a claim that the formula holds only in a right triangle. Not a conversion into radians.
Connects to
Last week’s law of sines and the already-taught Pythagorean theorem. Radians and the ambiguous case remain reserved.

Last week the law of sines related a side to the sine of the opposite angle. Today a second relation, the law of cosines, uses the cosine of the included angle. In any triangle, if c is the side opposite angle C, then c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C. The work stays in degrees; radians remain reserved.

When C is 90°, cos 90° = 0, so the last term drops, and c² = a² + b². That is the already-taught Pythagorean theorem, recovered as a special case, not as a new theorem. On an invented right triangle with legs 3 and 4, c² = 9 + 16 − 2·3·4·0 = 25, so c = 5. On an invented triangle with sides 5 and 5 and included angle 60°, cos 60° = 1/2, and c² = 25 + 25 − 25 = 25, so c = 5; the three sides are equal, as they must be in an equilateral triangle. The 3, 4, 5 and the 5, 5, 60° figures are invented classroom numbers, even though 3-4-5 is also a familiar integer right triangle. The law of sines is not replaced; it answers a different question. The ambiguous case remains reserved.

Remember

In any triangle, c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C. When C is 90°, this is the Pythagorean theorem. The work stays in degrees.

Physics · Sound intensity level, and the decibel, a first look, from OpenStax 17.3

40 min · Method: β = 10 log10(I/I0), I0 = 10^−12 W/m²; official table and definition only

Learn

Key terms

sound intensity level (the decibel)
Definition
The sound intensity level β, in decibels, is defined in OpenStax 17.3 by β = 10 log10(I/I0), where I is the sound intensity in watts per square meter and I0 = 10^−12 W/m² is the reference intensity, the threshold of hearing at 1000 Hz. It is not the intensity I itself, and it is not a sound pressure level in water.
Example
If I = I0, then log10(1) = 0, so β = 0 dB, the threshold of hearing in Table 17.2. If I is ten times I0, β = 10 dB. These are official values from the definition and from Table 17.2, not invented official levels of a place the table does not name.
Not this
Not the intensity I in W/m². Not a sound pressure level, a different decibel scale used especially in water, which OpenStax 17.3 sets aside. Not an invented official decibel level of a real place not in Table 17.2.
Connects to
Last week’s sound and intensity of a wave. The bel, OpenStax notes, is named for Alexander Graham Bell.

OpenStax 17.3, “Sound Intensity and Sound Level,” at https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level, starts from intensity already taught: I is the power per unit area carried by a wave, I = P/A, in W/m². How the ear perceives sound is more accurately described by the logarithm of the intensity than by the intensity itself. The sound intensity level β in decibels is defined to be β = 10 log10(I/I0), where I0 = 10^−12 W/m² is the lowest, or threshold, intensity of sound a person with normal hearing can perceive at 1000 Hz. Because β is defined in terms of a ratio, it is a unitless quantity; the units of decibels (dB) mark that the ratio is multiplied by 10. The bel, OpenStax notes, is named for Alexander Graham Bell.

If I = I0, log10(1) = 0, so β = 0 dB: the threshold of hearing is 0 decibels. Table 17.2 gives official pairs: 0 dB with 1×10^−12 W/m², threshold of hearing at 1000 Hz; 10 dB with 1×10^−11 W/m², rustle of leaves; 60 dB with 1×10^−6 W/m², normal conversation; 80 dB with 1×10^−4 W/m², loud radio, classroom lecture. Each factor of 10 in intensity corresponds to 10 dB. Do not invent an official decibel level of a real place the table does not name. The sound pressure level used especially in water remains reserved.

Remember

β = 10 log10(I/I0), with I0 = 10^−12 W/m². When I = I0, β = 0 dB. Intensity I and level β are not the same quantity.

Watch me

  2. Table 17.2 lists 60 dB, 1×10^−6 W/m², as normal conversation.
  3. That 60 dB is the book’s official pair, not an invented official level of a room we have not measured.

History · The first-voyage letter to Luis de Santangel, a first look

40 min · Method: R. H. Major, 1870 Hakluyt Society translation; the letter’s own wording, not later identifications

Learn

Key terms

the first-voyage letter (to Luis de Santangel)
Definition
The first-voyage letter in this lesson is the English of R. H. Major, Select Letters of Christopher Columbus, 2nd ed., Hakluyt Society, 1870, “A Letter sent by Columbus to [Luis de Santangel] Chancellor of the Exchequer [of Aragon].” It is the voyage report, not the Treaty of Tordesillas, and not Inter Caetera.
Example
The letter’s opening, in Major’s wording, says that “in thirty-three days' time I reached the Indies” and found “very many islands thickly peopled.” The thirty-three days are the letter’s figure in this translation, not a modern official conversion.
Not this
Not the Treaty of Tordesillas of June 7, 1494, already taught. Not Inter Caetera of May 3 or May 4, 1493. Not Major’s footnotes identifying islands with later names such as Watling’s Island, which are not taught as official identifications.
Connects to

The set text is Christopher Columbus’s letter to Luis de Santangel, in R. H. Major, Select Letters of Christopher Columbus, 2nd ed. (London: Hakluyt Society, 1870), pages 1–18, the English version headed “A Letter sent by Columbus to [Luis de Santangel] Chancellor of the Exchequer [of Aragon], respecting the Islands found in the Indies, enclosing another for their Highnesses.” Major’s wording, including “marvellously” and “thirty-three days' time,” stays inside quotations. Teaching prose uses US spelling.

The letter opens: “SIR,—Believing that you will take pleasure in hearing of the great success which our Lord has granted me in my voyage, I write you this letter, whereby you will learn how in thirty-three days' time I reached the Indies with the fleet which the most illustrious King and Queen, our Sovereigns, gave to me, where I found very many islands thickly peopled, of all which I took possession without resistance, for their Highnesses by proclamation made and with the royal standard unfurled.” The thirty-three days are the letter’s own figure in this translation. A footnote in Major, dating that interval from 8 September to 11 October, is the editor’s note, not the letter’s body, and is not taught as the letter’s own dating. The letter is a voyage report. It is earlier than last week’s Treaty of Tordesillas (June 7, 1494) and earlier than Inter Caetera. Do not identify the islands with later official modern names; Major’s footnotes such as “Watling’s Island” are not taught as official identifications. The names Columbus gives wait, in the letter’s wording, until the rest of today’s extract and Thursday.

“To the first island that I found I gave the name of San Salvador, in remembrance of His High Majesty, who hath marvellously brought all these things to pass; the Indians call it Guanaham. To the second island I gave the name of Santa-Maria de Concepcion; the third I called Fernandina; the fourth, Isabella; the fifth, Juana; and so to each one I gave a new name.” “Guanaham” is the wording of this 1870 edition, in both the English and the facing Spanish of the Ambrosian text as Major prints it, and it stays inside the quotation. Do not correct it to a later spelling, and do not add an official modern identification of the island.

Modern language · Spanish: the present subjunctive of a regular -ar verb, a first look

40 min · Method: hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen; contrasted with the indicative, future, and conditional

Learn

Key terms

present subjunctive of a regular -ar verb
Definition
The present subjunctive of a regular -ar verb is formed from the present first-person singular stem, with the endings -e, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en: hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen. It is not the present indicative habla, not the future hablará, and not the conditional hablaría.
Example
In an invented sentence, “Quiere que yo hable.” The hable is the present subjunctive, “that I speak,” not the indicative “hablo” or “habla.” The sentence is invented classroom Spanish, not an official quotation.
Not this
Not habla, the present indicative. Not hablará, the future. Not hablaría, the conditional. Not the present subjunctive of an -er or -ir verb, which waits until tomorrow. Not an irregular subjunctive.

Last week’s work was the simple conditional: hablaría, “I would speak.” Today a first look at the present subjunctive of a regular -ar verb. The forms are hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen. They are not the present indicative (hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan), not the future (hablaré, hablarás, hablará), and not the conditional (hablaría). A common invented classroom frame is “quiere que” plus the subjunctive: “Quiere que ella hable,” he or she wants her to speak. The sentences are invented classroom Spanish, not official quotations, and not copied from a copyrighted reader. Irregular subjunctives, and a fuller treatment of every trigger, remain reserved. Regular -er and -ir forms wait until tomorrow.

Seminar · Logic: independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), a first look

40 min · Method: invented fair coins and cubes; independence distinguished from mutual exclusivity

Learn

Key terms

independent events
Definition
Two events are independent when the happening of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Independence is not the same as mutually exclusive events, which cannot both happen.
Example
On two invented fair coins, tossed independently, P(heads on both) = (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4. The coins and the tosses are invented classroom figures, not an official experiment.
Not this
Not mutually exclusive events, which cannot both happen, so P(A and B) = 0, not P(A)P(B). Not a claim that every pair of events is independent. Not an official probability of a real event.
Connects to
Last week’s mutually exclusive events, and earlier complementary and equally likely outcomes. Inclusion-exclusion remains reserved, except as last week’s overlap miss.

Last week, mutually exclusive events could not both happen, and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) when they were mutually exclusive. Today a different relation: independent events. Two events are independent when the happening of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

On two invented fair coins, tossed independently, P(heads on the first) = 1/2 and P(heads on the second) = 1/2, so P(both heads) = 1/4. On an invented fair cube and an invented fair coin, independent of each other, P(6 and heads) = (1/6) × (1/2) = 1/12. The coins, the cube, and the tosses are invented classroom figures, not official probabilities of a real event.

Mutual exclusivity is not independence. On one invented fair cube, “4” and “5” are mutually exclusive: they cannot both happen, so P(4 and 5) = 0, which is not (1/6)×(1/6). Independent events can both happen. Do not use last week’s addition rule as a substitute for today’s product rule, and do not treat an overlap miss as this week’s first look. Inclusion-exclusion remains reserved.

Remember

Independent events satisfy P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen, so their “and” probability is 0, not the product.
