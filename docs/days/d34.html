Latin · Capitulum IX, part 4: quot? and a large or small number

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. IX · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

quot
Definition
An interrogative word meaning “how many?” It does not decline in the use taught today.
Example
Quot canēs in hortō sunt? Trēs canēs in hortō sunt.
Not this
Not quis (who) and not quod (the relative or the neuter of qui, already taught). quot asks for a number.

Mathematics · Geometry XVI: loci — a circle, and the perpendicular bisector

60 min · Method: a concrete string or a fold, then the definition of a locus as a set of points satisfying a condition

Learn

Key terms

locus
Definition
The set of all points that satisfy a stated condition. (The plural is often written loci.) From Latin locus, “place.”
Example
The locus of points at a fixed distance r from a point O is the circle with center O and radius r.
Not this
Not a single point chosen at random, and not a graph of a function unless that graph is being described as a set of points (x, f(x)). A locus is named by a condition, not by a plotted table alone.
Connects to
The circle as a set of points was already used informally in the circle chapters. Today the same idea is named, and the perpendicular bisector is recovered as a locus.
perpendicular bisector as a locus
Definition
The locus of points equidistant from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of the segment AB.
Example
If P is on the perpendicular bisector of AB, then PA = PB. Conversely, if PA = PB, then P lies on that bisector.
Not this
Not “any line through the midpoint,” and not the circle with diameter AB (that is a different locus: the angle in a semicircle, already taught, is a property of points on that circle, not this equidistance condition).
Connects to
The construction of the perpendicular bisector (compass arcs from A and from B with the same opening) is exactly a way of locating points equidistant from A and B.

A locus is a set of points described by a condition. Two conditions you already have the pictures for:

  1. A circle: all points at a fixed distance r from a fixed center O. That is the definition of the circle, now named as a locus.
  2. The perpendicular bisector of AB: all points P such that PA = PB. A fold of the paper that takes A to B has that line as its crease. The compass construction — the same opening from A and from B — finds two such points, and the line through them is the whole locus.

A first contrast. The circle with diameter AB is a different set of points (points from which AB subtends a right angle, Euclid III.31). Do not call that circle “the locus PA = PB.”

A classroom string. If one end of a string is held at O and a pencil is held at a fixed length, the path is a circle, the locus of “distance r from O.” If the string is slack, the condition has failed, and the path is not that locus.

Remember

Locus: the set of points that satisfy a condition. Circle: distance r from O. Perpendicular bisector of AB: PA = PB. The compass construction with a same opening finds that bisector. The circle on diameter AB is a different locus.

Key terms: locus, perpendicular bisector as a locus.

Watch me

Name the locus of points at 4 cm from a point O, and say what 4 cm is in that figure.

  1. A circle with center O and radius 4 cm.
  2. The 4 cm is the radius, the constant distance in the condition.
  3. Every point of that circle, and no point off it, satisfies the condition.

Hint 2: The construction

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin’s method: hints from the Bacon opening

Franklin hint
Definition

Example
Hints might read: Studies / delight / ornament / ability. From those, reconstruct the three-part for-series, then check the exact wording and the attribution.
Not this
Not copying the sentence with the model still in view, and not pretending you have memorized a longer essay. Only the opening sentence is today’s model.
Connects to
The comparison step is the point: where your reconstruction drifted, repair it, then write a new imitatio from the same hints.

Science · From the retina to a signal

50 min · Method: continue the path honestly, without invented cell counts

Learn

Key terms

optic nerve
Definition
The nerve that carries a signal from the retina toward the brain. In this first look, it is named as the path of that signal, not as a counted bundle of fibers.
Example
Light reaches the retina; a signal then leaves by the optic nerve. This lesson does not invent a travel time or a fiber count.
Not this
Not the cornea, and not a second “eye” at the back. It is the path out, not the receiving layer.
Connects to
The camera analogy now fails in a useful way: a camera’s cable is manufactured; the optic nerve is living tissue, and there is a place on the retina where that nerve leaves — the blind spot, named below.
blind spot
Definition
A place on the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eye, and where there are no light-sensitive receptors, so no image is received there.
Example
Each eye has a blind spot. You do not ordinarily notice it, because the two eyes and the brain fill in the gap; that “filling in” is named, not explained in detail, today.
Not this
Not a dark patch you should see in ordinary vision, and not a disease. It is a normal consequence of the nerve’s exit.
Connects to
This is a limit of the camera analogy: a manufactured sensor need not have a cable-exit gap of this kind. The eye does.

The path continues: the retina receives the image; a signal leaves by the optic nerve. Where that nerve exits, there is a blind spot — a place with no receptors. We do not invent a count of rods or cones, a measured size of the blind spot, or a travel time for the signal. Those numbers, if they appear in other books, are not today’s lesson unless a later, sourced lesson sets them.

The useful conclusion is small and honest: seeing is not only “a picture on the back of the eye.” A signal must leave, and the receiving surface has a gap where that happens.

Remember

The optic nerve carries a signal from the retina toward the brain. The blind spot is where that nerve leaves, with no receptors. No invented counts or times.

Key terms: optic nerve, blind spot.

Watch me

Hint 2: The fact

Atelier · A second-pass value on the whole head

50 min · Method: one more value pass over the whole head (masses already begun), from the plate or plaster

Learn

Key terms

second-pass value
Definition
A second, slower pass of light and dark over the whole head, after the first block-in, so that the features, hair masses, ear, and neck share one light.
Example
Darken the shadow family together; keep the light family simpler. A face that is fully modeled while the neck is still a flat mid-tone usually looks pasted.
Not this
Not a new drawing, and not a start from individual eyelashes or hairs. The pass is for the whole head, under the same light as the plate.
Connects to
Lost-and-found edges (week 6) still apply: some edges stay soft, some may be a little sharper, as the plate shows.

Squint again. Sort the head into a shadow family and a light family. Work across the forehead, the features, the ear, the hair, and the neck in the same pass, so one light explains them all.

If one feature is far ahead of the rest, stop and bring the rest up. The plate is the judge of how dark the darks go.

Remember

A second value pass covers the whole head under one light. Shadow family together; lights simpler. Do not finish one feature and leave the neck flat.

Key terms: second-pass value.

Modern language · Spanish: dates

40 min · Method: one present-tense date pattern, contrasted with telling the time; English remains the language of instruction

Learn

Key terms

dates in Spanish (present)
Definition
A present-tense way to give a date: Es el + number + de + month (for the first, often Es el primero de + month). The year may be added after de, if needed, as a number.
Example
Es el 5 de mayo. Es el primero de enero. Hoy es el 17 de noviembre.
Not this
Not *Es el uno de mayo for the first (use primero), and not an English ‘November 17th’ word order as the Spanish pattern. Months are not capitalized in Spanish the way English month names are.
Connects to
Telling the time (week 6) is a different pattern (Es la una, Son las dos). Today is the date, not the clock.
Word How to say it Meaning
Hoy es el 5 de mayo oy ess el SEENG-koh deh MY-oh Today is May 5
Es el primero de enero ess el pree-MEH-ro deh eh-NEH-ro It is January 1
Es el 17 de noviembre ess el dee-ee-SYEH-teh deh noh-BYEM-breh It is November 17
¿Qué fecha es hoy? keh FEH-chah ess oy What is today’s date?

The pattern. Es el + number + de + month. For the first of the month, Es el primero de + month is the form taught today. Months: enero, febrero, marzo, abril, mayo, junio, julio, agosto, septiembre, octubre, noviembre, diciembre — not capitalized as in English.

This is not telling the time (Son las dos). Original sentences: Hoy es el 3 de marzo. ¿Qué fecha es hoy? Es el 17 de noviembre. Mi clase es el 2 de abril. (You may invent a class date; do not invent a historical date as if it were a source.)

Remember

Es el + number + de + month. The first of the month: Es el primero de + month. Months are not capitalized as in English. This is not Son las dos.

Economics & personal finance · A labeled budget (an invented example)

40 min · Method: one invented, fully labeled household month; no real person’s figures, and no unsourced etymology of the word budget

Learn

Key terms

budget (labeled example)
Definition
A plan that matches expected income to planned spending, with a leftover (or a shortfall) named honestly. Today’s figures are invented and labeled as an example, not taken from a real household.
Example
In the classroom example: income 800; needs 500; wants 200; leftover 100 (same units). The leftover is income minus (needs + wants), here 800 − 700 = 100.
Not this
Not a forecast presented as a measured fact about a real family, and not a claim that every good plan must use these three headings. Needs and wants can be argued; the headings must still be labeled.
Connects to
Last week’s three jobs of money (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value) are in the background: a budget is a plan in a unit of account, not a new definition of money.

A budget, in this first look, is a plan: expected income, planned needs, planned wants, and a leftover or a shortfall. We use an invented month, labeled as invented:

  • Income: 800
  • Needs: 500
  • Wants: 200
  • Leftover: 800 − (500 + 200) = 100

The units are “classroom units,” not a real currency claim about a real person. If wants rise to 400 and income and needs stay the same, the leftover becomes a shortfall: 800 − (500 + 400) = −100, a shortfall of 100. The arithmetic is ordinary subtraction. The lesson is to label the rows and not to pretend the numbers are someone’s private accounts.

Remember

A budget plans income against needs and wants, and names a leftover or a shortfall. Today’s 800 / 500 / 200 / 100 example is invented and labeled. No real person’s figures.

Key terms: budget (labeled example).

Watch me
