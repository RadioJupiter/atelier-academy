Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday mixes this week’s perfects with the already-taught presents, futures, and pluperfects of sequor and ēgredior, and with the already-taught tenses of loquor when a contrast is needed. Secūtus sum is not locūtus sum. Ēgressus sum is not ēgressus eram. Keep identity with Puer or Puella. Sentences stay original.

Remember

Secūtus sum, I followed, or I have followed; ēgressus sum, I went out, or I have gone out; neither is a future, and neither is a pluperfect.

Watch me

  2. Ēgredior: ēgressus sum, ēgressus es, ēgressus est, ēgressī sumus, ēgressī estis, ēgressī sunt.
  3. A fair pair: Amīcī mē secūtī sunt. Puella ēgressa est.

Hint 2: Ēgredior

Mathematics · Friday mix: the radian, arc length s = rθ, and exact values in radians

40 min · Friday mix · exact values in π; invented figures labeled invented

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the radian — Day 141

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday mixes the definition of a radian, the conversion 2π radians = 360°, arc length s = rθ with θ in radians, and the exact sines and cosines at 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2, and π, including related angles such as π − θ. The law of cosines, already taught in degrees, may be used only if any angle is first named consistently; today’s first look is the radian, not a new cosine law. The ambiguous case remains reserved. Invented radii stay labeled invented.

Remember

A radian is the central angle whose arc equals the radius. s = rθ when θ is in radians. The exact unit-circle values are unchanged when the angles are named in radians.

Watch me

State 90° and 180° in radians, give s on an invented circle of radius 4 with central angle π/2, and give sin(π/2) and cos π.

  1. 90° = π/2; 180° = π.
  2. s = 4 × (π/2) = 2π. The radius 4 is invented.
  3. sin(π/2) = 1; cos π = −1.

Together

Why is s = r × 90 a miss for a right central angle, and why is sin(π − π/6) equal to 1/2?

Hint 1: The unit

90 is 90° until it is converted to π/2. s = rθ needs radians.

Hint 2: Related angles

sin(π − θ) = sin θ, so sin(5π/6) = sin(π/6) = 1/2.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. What is a radian, and how many radians are in a full turn?
  2. On an invented circle of radius 6 with central angle 60°, what is the arc length?
  3. Give sin(π/6), cos(π/3), and sin(π − π/4).

Exercises

  1. Convert 270° to radians, then give its sine and cosine from the unit circle already taught.

Writing · Friday mix: a refutation distinguished from a maxim, a chreia, a narrative, and a fable

40 min · Friday mix · set invented claim, plus a short original refutation

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • refutation (progymnasmata) — Day 141
  • maxim (progymnasmata) — Day 136

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday distinguishes the progymnasmata already taught. A fable is a short story, often with animals, that ends with a stated moral. A narrative tells a complete event in order, without a required moral or precept. A chreia needs a person, an occasion, and a saying or act. A maxim is a short general precept. A refutation examines a claim and gives reasons why the claim should not be accepted. The set invented claim remains “A story is true because it has been told many times.” An original refutation stays labeled invented. Confirmation remains reserved.

Remember

A refutation examines a claim. A maxim is a precept. A chreia needs a person and an occasion. A narrative tells an event in order. A fable carries a moral, often after an animal story.

Watch me

Sort five short items: the 1733 line “Eat to live, and not live to eat”; Diogenes’ saying to Alexander; a retelling of Townsend’s Crow and the Pitcher with its moral; a walk told in order with no moral; and a short examination of the invented claim “A story is true because it has been told many times.”

  1. The 1733 line is a maxim. Diogenes’ saying, on that occasion, is a chreia. The Crow and the Pitcher with its moral is a fable. The walk in order with no moral is a narrative. The examination of the invented claim is a refutation.
  2. Do not call the 1733 line a refutation only because it could be argued about.
  3. Do not call the invented claim a maxim; it is a claim to be examined, not a precept.

Together

Hint 2: Reserved

A confirmation would defend a claim, and remains reserved.

Physics · Friday mix: the Doppler effect, Example 17.4, and a first look at a sonic boom

40 min · Friday mix · official OpenStax 17.4 figures; sonic boom from the same section; Cerenkov reserved

Learn

Key terms

a sonic boom
Definition
A sonic boom, in OpenStax 17.4, is a constructive interference of sound created by an object moving faster than sound, a shock wave along a cone. It is not the ordinary Doppler shift of a source still slower than sound, and it is not an official measurement of a particular aircraft not in the book.
Example
When the source’s speed exceeds the speed of sound, constructive interference along a cone forms a shock wave called a sonic boom. OpenStax notes that an aircraft creates two sonic booms, one from its nose and one from its tail. Bow wakes and Cerenkov radiation remain reserved.
Not this
Not the Doppler shift of Example 17.4, whose train is slower than sound. Not an official recorded boom of a particular flight. Not Cerenkov radiation, which remains reserved, and not an invented official value of the speed of light.
Connects to
Today’s Doppler effect, and the already-taught constructive interference. Bow wakes beyond the sonic boom, and Cerenkov radiation, remain reserved.

Friday mixes the Doppler effect, the two official formulas, and official Example 17.4 (150 Hz, 35.0 m/s, 340 m/s, 167 Hz approaching, 136 Hz receding, 150 Hz for the engineer). It also takes a first look at the sonic boom, still from OpenStax 17.4. If a source moves faster than sound, no sound is received by an observer ahead until the source has passed; constructive interference along a cone forms a shock wave called a sonic boom, “a constructive interference of sound created by an object moving faster than sound.” “An aircraft creates two sonic booms, one from its nose and one from its tail.” These are the book’s statements, not official measurements of a particular flight. Bow wakes more generally, and Cerenkov radiation, remain reserved, and no official speed of light is invented. Official frequencies of vehicles not in Example 17.4 are not invented.

Remember

Example 17.4: 167 Hz approaching, 136 Hz receding, 150 Hz for the engineer. A sonic boom is a shock wave from a source faster than sound, not the ordinary Doppler shift of a slower source.

Watch me

Give the official approaching and receding frequencies of Example 17.4, and say in OpenStax’s wording what a sonic boom is.

  1. 167 Hz approaching, 136 Hz receding, from 150 Hz, 35.0 m/s, and 340 m/s.
  2. A sonic boom is “a constructive interference of sound created by an object moving faster than sound.”
  3. The Example 17.4 train is slower than sound, so its Doppler shift is not a sonic boom.

Together

Why does the engineer hear 150 Hz, and why is Cerenkov radiation not today’s job?

Hint 1: The engineer

The relative velocity between engineer and horn is zero, so f_obs = f_s.

Hint 2: Reserved

Cerenkov radiation, and an official speed of light, remain reserved.

Seminar · Anatomy: the thoracic vertebrae, from Gray 1918, a first look

40 min · Method: Gray 1918 wording via Theodora; rib facets; no official lengths; not a diagnosis; T1 and T9–T12 reserved

Learn

Key terms

the thoracic vertebrae (Gray, 1918)
Definition
In Gray’s 1918 Anatomy, via Theodora, the thoracic vertebrae “are intermediate in size between those of the cervical and lumbar regions; they increase in size from above downward.” They are “distinguished by the presence of facets on the sides of the bodies for articulation with the heads of the ribs, and facets on the transverse processes of all, except the eleventh and twelfth, for articulation with the tubercles of the ribs.” Official lengths are not invented, and the lesson is not a diagnosis.
Example
Gray: “The bodies in the middle of the thoracic region are heart-shaped.” “The spinous process is long, triangular on coronal section, directed obliquely downward, and ends in a tuberculated extremity.” The first, ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth “present certain peculiarities, and must be specially considered”; those peculiarities are not this first look.
Not this
Not last week’s seventh cervical vertebra, vertebra prominens, which is cervical, not thoracic. Not a lumbar vertebra, not yet taught in full. Not an official length, and not a diagnosis of a living person. Not a body being described from life.
Connects to
Already-taught cervical vertebrae, including the atlas, the axis, and vertebra prominens. Official lengths remain reserved. The peculiarities of T1 and T9–T12, and the lumbar vertebrae, remain reserved or later.

Gray’s 1918 Anatomy of the Human Body, as posted by Theodora, is still the artistic-anatomy set text. Last week ended with the seventh cervical vertebra, vertebra prominens. Today the thoracic vertebrae, in Gray’s wording. “The thoracic vertebræ are intermediate in size between those of the cervical and lumbar regions; they increase in size from above downward, the upper vertebræ being much smaller than those in the lower part of the region.” “Vertebræ” is Gray’s spelling and stays inside the quotation. Teaching prose uses US spelling, “vertebrae.”

“They are distinguished by the presence of facets on the sides of the bodies for articulation with the heads of the ribs, and facets on the transverse processes of all, except the eleventh and twelfth, for articulation with the tubercles of the ribs.” “The bodies in the middle of the thoracic region are heart-shaped, and as broad in the antero-posterior as in the transverse direction.” “The spinous process is long, triangular on coronal section, directed obliquely downward, and ends in a tuberculated extremity.” Gray does not give official classroom lengths here, and none are invented.

“The first, ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth thoracic vertebræ present certain peculiarities, and must be specially considered.” Those peculiarities are not today’s first look and remain reserved. The lesson is artistic anatomy from a public-domain text, not a medical diagnosis, and no body is being described from life. Official lengths remain reserved. Lumbar vertebrae remain reserved.

Remember

Gray’s thoracic vertebrae are intermediate in size, increase from above downward, and are marked by rib facets on the bodies, and on the transverse processes of all except the eleventh and twelfth. Official lengths are not invented, and the lesson is not a diagnosis.
