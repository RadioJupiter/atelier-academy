Latin · Capitulum XI, part 4: AcI with esse

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. XI · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

AcI with esse
Definition
The same accusative-and-infinitive construction, with esse as the infinitive. A predicate word that describes the accusative subject is itself accusative: Putō corpus sānum esse, “I think that the body is healthy.”
Example
Putō corpus sānum esse. Dīcō caput magnum esse.
Not this
Not a nominative predicate as in Corpus sānum est, which has a finite form of esse and no AcI. After putō or dīcō, both the subject and the describing word are accusative, and esse is infinitive.
Connects to
Yesterday’s AcI after vidēre, dīcere, and putāre is still the construction. Today the infinitive is esse, which you already know, and the predicate follows the accusative.

The same construction works when the second verb is esse. The person or thing you are talking about still stands in the accusative, and esse is the infinitive.

A describing word that belongs with that accusative — a predicate adjective — is also accusative, and it agrees in gender and number: Putō corpus sānum esse. I think that the body is healthy. Dīcō caput magnum esse. I say that the head is large. Puella crūs longum esse dīcit. The girl says that the leg is long.

Keep this apart from the ordinary sentence Corpus sānum est, which has a nominative subject and a finite verb. Once you are inside an AcI, you do not go back to est and a nominative.

Remember

After putō or dīcō, esse takes an accusative subject, and a predicate adjective is also accusative: Putō corpus sānum esse. This is not the same as Corpus sānum est.

Key terms: AcI with esse.

Mathematics · Geometry XXVIII: a height from similar shadows

40 min · Method: AA in a labeled shadow picture, then a proportion

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • AA similarity — Day 62
  • scale factor (similarity) — Day 63

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Similar triangles are a tool for an indirect measurement: a height you cannot stand a tape against, found from a shadow you can measure, or from a second, smaller object standing in the same light.

A pole 2 m tall casts a 3 m shadow. A tree, nearby, casts a 9 m shadow in the same light. The sun’s rays are parallel, so the two right triangles — object, ground, ray — have the same angles, and they are similar by AA. The proportion is 2/3 = h/9, so h = 6 m.

The numbers here are a labeled classroom example. The method is the same whenever the light is the same and the two objects stand upright on level ground. Today does not use a new named theorem about right triangles; it uses AA and a scale factor, which you already have.

Remember

Two upright objects in the same light make similar right triangles with their shadows (AA: both have a right angle, and the sun’s rays are parallel, so the acute angles match). A known height and shadow find an unknown height from the same scale factor.

Watch me

The pole is 2 m tall and casts a 3 m shadow; the tree’s shadow is 9 m. Find the height of the tree.

  1. The triangles are similar by AA (a right angle at the ground, and equal acute angles from the same rays).
  2. 2/3 = h/9.
  3. h = 6 m. Check: the scale factor from pole to tree is 9/3 = 3, and 2 × 3 = 6.

Together

A 1.5 m stick casts a 2.0 m shadow. A flagpole in the same light casts an 8.0 m shadow. Find the height of the flagpole.

Hint 1: The proportion

1.5/2.0 = h/8.0.

Hint 2: The height

h = 1.5 × 8.0 / 2.0 = 6.0 m.

Check

Assume the objects stand upright in the same light on level ground, as in the lesson. Label your units.

  1. Why are the pole-and-shadow and tree-and-shadow triangles similar?
  2. A 2.5 m post casts a 4.0 m shadow. A building’s shadow is 20 m. Find the height of the building.
  3. A student writes 2/9 = 3/h for the Learn figure. What has gone wrong?
  4. If a 3 m pole casts a 3 m shadow, what can you say about a nearby tree whose shadow is 12 m?

Exercises

  1. A 0.8 m rod casts a 1.2 m shadow. A tower in the same light casts a 15 m shadow. Find the height of the tower.
  2. A tree is 8 m tall and casts a 6 m shadow. How long is the shadow of a 2 m post in the same light?
  3. What would go wrong if the two shadows were not cast in the same light (for example, at two different times of day)?

Science · Water: the triple point, and the ice point at one atmosphere

50 min · Method: the unique three-phase point, set beside Tuesday’s ice point at 1 atm

Learn

Key terms

triple point
Definition
The unique pressure and temperature at which the solid, liquid, and vapor of a substance all coexist in equilibrium. For water, this point is exactly 273.16 K on ITS-90, which is 0.01 °C. It is not the same condition as the ice point at one atmosphere.
Example
At the triple point of water, ice, liquid water, and water vapor are all present together, at 0.01 °C (273.16 K). That is a different pressure from one atmosphere, and a slightly different temperature from the familiar ice point of about 0 °C at 1 atm.
Not this
Not the melting point at one atmosphere, and not the boiling point at one atmosphere. Those are two-phase points at a stated ordinary pressure. The triple point is the unique three-phase condition. Do not treat 0.01 °C as a typo for 0 °C, or treat them as two names for the same point.
Connects to
Tuesday’s ice point and boiling point were at one atmosphere. The IAPWS FAQ is again the source: the triple point is the unique condition where solid, liquid, and vapor coexist, and on ITS-90 it is exactly 273.16 K.

Tuesday’s figures were for one atmosphere: the familiar ice point, about 0 °C, and the boiling point, about 100 °C, with the sourced ITS-90 boiling temperature of about 99.974 °C. Those are two-phase points at a stated pressure: solid with liquid, or liquid with vapor, at 1 atm.

The triple point is a different condition. It is the unique pressure and temperature at which solid, liquid, and vapor all coexist. For water, the IAPWS FAQ gives that point as exactly 273.16 K on ITS-90. Since 0 °C is 273.15 K, the triple point is 0.01 °C.

That is why the ice point at one atmosphere and the triple point must be kept apart. The familiar “about 0 °C” describes ice and liquid water at 1 atm. The triple point is a unique three-phase condition at 0.01 °C, and the pressure there is not one atmosphere. Do not treat 0.01 °C as a typo for 0 °C, and do not blend the two into one official point.

The kelvin itself is no longer defined by locking the triple point at 273.16 K (the 2018 change, which the IAPWS FAQ also notes), but the best estimate of water’s triple point is still 273.16 K, and that is the figure this lesson uses.

Remember

The triple point of water is the unique condition where ice, liquid, and vapor coexist: 273.16 K on ITS-90, which is 0.01 °C. The ice point at one atmosphere is a different condition, about 0 °C. They are not two names for the same point. The source is the IAPWS FAQ.

Literature · Inferno, Canto I: mid-life, the forest, and the promise, in the set lines

40 min · Method: a closer reading of the already-set nine lines, not a new extract

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • canto — Day 62

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

The same nine set lines, read more slowly. No new extract is added, and later of the canto remains reserved.

Midway upon the journey of our life
I found myself within a forest dark,
For the straightforward pathway had been lost.
Ah me! how hard a thing it is to say
What was this forest savage, rough, and stern,
Which in the very thought renews the fear.
So bitter is it, death is little more;
But of the good to treat, which there I found,
Speak will I of the other things I saw there.

Dante Alighieri, Inferno, Canto I, lines 1–9, translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, 1867, as posted at Wikisource, Divine Comedy (Longfellow 1867), Volume 1, Canto I. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_1 Later lines of this canto, and later cantos, remain reserved.

Three things to notice, still inside these lines. First, the time: “Midway upon the journey of our life” — not a street name, and not yet a year on a calendar, but a place in a life. Second, the place: a forest that is dark, and then “savage, rough, and stern,” so bitter that death is little more. Third, the promise: he will treat of the good he found there, and will speak of the other things he saw. The fear is real in the lines, and so is that promise.

Keep Longfellow’s wording when you quote. “Forest dark” is his inversion, not “dark forest,” if you are quoting the second line. A remembered other translation is not this text.

Modern language · Spanish: ayer, anoche, and el lunes pasado, with the preterite

30 min · Method: time words that mark a completed past, used with regular -ar preterite forms

Learn

Key terms

ayer, anoche, el lunes pasado
Definition
Spanish time words that place a completed action in the past, and so often accompany the preterite: ayer, “yesterday”; anoche, “last night”; el lunes pasado, “last Monday” (and likewise el martes pasado, and so on). They do not themselves conjugate; the verb still carries the preterite ending.
Example
Ayer yo hablé. Anoche ella cantó. El lunes pasado nosotros compramos pan.
Not this
Not a new verb tense, and not a present-tense time word such as hoy (“today”) or ahora (“now”). The nosotros form still looks like the present; these time words are one clear way to mark it as past.
Connects to
The regular -ar preterite was taught on Monday. Today’s words make the past time explicit, especially with hablamos / cantamos / compramos.
Word How to say it Meaning
ayer ah-YER yesterday
anoche ah-NOH-cheh last night
el lunes pasado el LOO-nes pah-SAH-doh last Monday
el martes pasado el MAR-tes pah-SAH-doh last Tuesday

A time word does not replace the verb ending; it sits with a preterite verb and tells you when the completed action happened. Ayer yo hablé. Yesterday I spoke. Anoche ella cantó. Last night she sang. El lunes pasado nosotros compramos pan. Last Monday we bought bread.

That last sentence is the useful one for nosotros: compramos could be present, but el lunes pasado marks it as preterite. The same is true of ayer hablamos and anoche cantamos.

Keep hoy (today) and ahora (now) for the present, as already used in Term 1. Do not write Ayer yo hablo.

Remember

ayer (yesterday), anoche (last night), and el lunes pasado (last Monday) place a completed action in the past and are used with the preterite. They do not replace the verb ending. With nosotros, they make hablamos / cantamos / compramos clearly past.

Economics · Credit, and a bank that holds deposits and makes loans

30 min · Method: labeled invented examples of paying later, and of a deposit and a loan

Learn

Key terms

credit (buying now, paying later)
Definition
An arrangement in which a person or a shop allows a purchase now, with payment later, in one later sum or in parts. The numbers in this lesson are labeled invented classroom examples, not a real official rate.
Origin
Borrowed from Middle French crédit, “belief, trust,” from Latin crēditum, “a loan, credit,” from crēdere, “to believe” (English Wiktionary, entry “credit”).
Example
An invented case: a $40 tool is taken home now; $10 is paid now and $10 at the end of each of the next three months, with no added fee in this first example, so the total paid is $40.
Not this
Not a gift, and not a claim about a real bank’s interest rate or a government figure this course has not sourced. A later fee, if one is named, will be labeled as invented as well. This is not the already-taught compound interest problem from Day 9, though a fee on credit is related to interest and will be kept distinct when it appears.
Connects to
Day 9 taught compound interest and principal on labeled invented sums. Today begins credit as buying now and paying later. A real official rate is not invented.
bank (deposits and loans)
Definition
In this first look, a bank is a place that holds deposits (money left there for safekeeping, still belonging to the depositor) and makes loans (money it lets a borrower use now and repay later). The figures in this lesson are invented.
Origin
From Middle English banke, from Middle French banque, from Italian banca, “counter, moneychanger’s table” (English Wiktionary, entry “bank”).
Example
An invented classroom bank holds a $100 deposit and makes an $80 loan, keeping $20 in reserve. These numbers are labeled invented; they are not a report of a named real bank.
Not this
Not a shop, and not a sourced official reserve requirement or central-bank rate. This course will not invent a real official rate and then treat it as a fact.
Connects to
Credit is the broader idea of buying now and paying later. A bank loan is one common form of that idea, beside a shop’s own “pay later” arrangement.

Credit, in this first look, is buying now and paying later. A shop may let you take a tool home and pay in parts; a bank may lend you a sum that you repay later. The idea is the same: the goods or the money are in your hands now, and the payment comes afterwards.

A labeled invented example, not a real shop: a $40 tool is taken home now. You pay $10 now and $10 at the end of each of the next three months, with no added fee in this example. The total paid is $40, the same as the price. A later lesson may add a fee; if it does, that fee will also be labeled invented. This course will not invent a real official interest rate and then treat it as a fact.

A bank, in this same first look, is a place that holds deposits and makes loans. An invented classroom bank takes in a $100 deposit and makes an $80 loan, keeping $20 in reserve. The deposit still belongs to the depositor; the loan is money the borrower must repay. Compound interest, already taught on Day 9, is a related tool for later work on a loan or a deposit; it is not retaught as new today, and it is not used here to invent an official rate.

Remember

Credit is buying now and paying later. A bank, in this first look, holds deposits and makes loans. All figures today are invented classroom examples. A real official rate is not invented. Compound interest, already taught, is not retaught as new.

Key terms: credit (buying now, paying later), bank (deposits and loans).

Watch me

In the invented $40 tool example, how much is paid now, how much later, and what is the total, if there is no added fee? Then, in the invented bank, what is the deposit, the loan, and the amount kept?

  1. $10 is paid now, and $10 at the end of each of the next three months, so $30 is paid later. The total is $40, equal to the price, because this example has no added fee.
  2. The invented bank holds a $100 deposit, makes an $80 loan, and keeps $20.

Together

A student writes, as a fact, “the official bank rate is 5%.” What is wrong with that sentence in this lesson, and how would you repair a classroom example that needs a fee?

Hint 1: The official rate

This course has not sourced a real official rate. Inventing one and stating it as a fact would be dishonest.

Hint 2: A repair

Label an invented fee as invented: for example, “in this classroom example, a $2 fee is added, so $42 is repaid.” That is a labeled example, not an official rate.

Use today's key terms (credit (buying now, paying later), bank (deposits and loans)). For each term, close the card and then: (1) explain it in your own words; (2) give a new example, not the one on the card; (3) name a near-miss — something it is easily confused with — and say how you would tell them apart.

Hint 1: Own words

Start from the definition, but do not copy it. If you can only repeat the card, close it and try again from the example you invent.

Hint 2: Near-miss

The "Not this" line on the card names a common mix-up. Explain why that mix-up would change an answer in this lesson.

Check

Keep invented numbers labeled. Do not invent an official rate.

  1. In your own words, what is credit as this lesson uses the word?
  2. In the invented $40 example with no fee, fill in a short table: paid now, paid later, total paid.
  3. What two jobs does a bank have in this first look, and what are the invented $100 / $80 / $20 figures?
  4. How should a classroom example that includes a fee be labeled, and what must it not pretend to be?
  5. For each of today's key terms (credit (buying now, paying later), bank (deposits and loans)), write one sentence in your own words and one near-miss you must not confuse it with. Then apply one of the terms to a fresh case of your own (not the example on the card).

Exercises

  1. An invented shop lets you pay $15 now and $15 later for a $30 book, with no fee. Is that credit? Why?
  2. Why is compound interest, already taught, not used today to name a real bank’s rate?
  3. If a $100 deposit is made and an $80 loan is made, who still owns the $100, and who must repay the $80?
