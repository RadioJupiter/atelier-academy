Latin · Capitulum X, part 2: the present infinitive, active

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. X · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

present infinitive (active)
Definition
The ‘to …’ form of the verb, not limited to a person in the way a finite form is. From Late Latin īnfīnītīvus (‘unlimited, indefinite’), from Latin īnfīnītus (‘unlimited, infinite’). In this lesson the present active infinitives include vocāre, habēre, vidēre, esse, and īre.
Example
Rēx canem vidēre potest. Homō in hortō ambulāre vult is a pattern you may meet later; today the companion verb taught with the infinitive is posse (‘to be able’), in the forms possum, potes, potest.
Not this
Not a new tense of the finite verb, and not an English ‘ing’ form. vidēre is ‘to see,’ not ‘sees’ and not ‘seeing’ as a main verb.
Connects to
īre was already named as ‘to go.’ Today the infinitive is used after potest / possum / potes: someone is able to do something.
posse + infinitive
Definition
posse means ‘to be able / can.’ In this first look we use possum (I am able), potes (you are able), and potest (he/she is able), followed by a present active infinitive.
Example
Possum vidēre. Potes vocāre. Rēx leōnem vidēre potest.
Not this
Not *potest videt (a finite form after potest), and not *possum videō. After posse, the second verb is an infinitive.
Connects to
The person is marked on possum / potes / potest, not on the infinitive.
Word How to say it Meaning
vidēre wi-DAY-reh to see
habēre ha-BAY-reh to have
vocāre wo-KAH-reh to call
esse ES-seh to be
īre EE-reh to go
possum, potes, potest POS-sum, PO-tes, PO-test I am able, you are able, he/she is able
Rēx vidēre potest rayks wi-DAY-reh PO-test The king is able to see / can see

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

A second verb, not a second finite form. Potest (he/she can) is followed by an infinitive: vidēre, habēre, vocāre, esse, īre. The person lives on possum / potes / potest.

Original sentences: Possum vidēre. Potes vocāre. Homō īre potest. Rēx leōnem vidēre potest. Puer canem habēre potest.

Remember

The present active infinitive is the ‘to …’ form (vidēre, habēre, vocāre, esse, īre). After possum / potes / potest, use an infinitive, not a second finite verb.

Mathematics · Geometry XVIII: regular polygons, and the interior angle

60 min · Method: divide a convex polygon into triangles from one vertex, then specialize to the regular case

Learn

Key terms

regular polygon
Definition
A polygon with all sides equal and all interior angles equal. The name polygon comes from Greek πολύγωνον (polugōnon), from πολύς (polus), “many,” and γωνία (gōnia), “angle.”
Origin
Polygon is from Greek polus, “many,” and gōnia, “angle.”
Example
A square is a regular quadrilateral. A regular hexagon has six equal sides and six equal interior angles, each 120°.
Not this
Not “any hexagon,” and not a circle (a circle is not a polygon). A rectangle that is not a square has equal angles but not all sides equal, so it is not regular.
Connects to
Day 6 proved that a triangle’s angles sum to 180°, and a stretch asked for the n-gon sum. Today that sum is taught as a main fact, and then used for a regular polygon’s one interior angle.

The sum, for any convex n-gon. From one vertex, draw diagonals to all non-adjacent vertices. These cut the polygon into n − 2 triangles. Each triangle has angle sum 180° (Day 6). So the interior angles of a convex n-gon sum to

(n − 2) × 180°.

The regular case. If the polygon is regular, the n interior angles are equal, so each is

((n − 2) / n) × 180°.

Checks you already know: an equilateral triangle (n = 3) gives ((1)/3)×180° = 60°. A square (n = 4) gives ((2)/4)×180° = 90°. A regular hexagon (n = 6) gives ((4)/6)×180° = 120°.

A first look at the exterior angle. If one side of a convex polygon is extended, the exterior angle and the interior angle on the same vertex add to 180°. For a regular n-gon, that exterior angle is 360° / n (because the n exterior angles, one at each vertex, taken all the same way around, sum to 360°). For a regular hexagon, 360°/6 = 60°, and 120° + 60° = 180°, which agrees.

This is a first, Term-1 look at regular polygons (the deeper treatment of polygons and the circle’s area is still planned later). Today’s job is the sum, the one interior angle, and the matching exterior angle.

Remember

A convex n-gon has interior-angle sum (n − 2)×180°. In a regular n-gon, each interior angle is ((n − 2)/n)×180°, and each exterior angle (side extended) is 360°/n.

Key terms: regular polygon.

Watch me

  1. Sum = (5 − 2)×180° = 540°.
  2. Each interior angle = 540°/5 = 108°, or ((3)/5)×180° = 108°.
  3. Each exterior = 360°/5 = 72°, and 108° + 72° = 180°.

45 min · Method: topic sentences first, for an essay that holds two claims side by side

Key terms

fair two-claim plan
Definition
A three-paragraph outline whose job is to state two opposing or contrasting claims fairly, without a verdict that depends on scenes not yet taught. Paragraph 1 names the conflict and the first claim; paragraph 2 names the second claim (or a concession); paragraph 3 closes without pretending to have finished a play or a case that is still open.
Example

Science · The lens changes shape: accommodation

50 min · Method: a first map of near and far focus, without invented focal lengths or diopter numbers

Learn

Key terms

accommodation (eye)
Definition
The change of shape of the eye’s lens that allows a near object, or a far object, to be focused on the retina. When the lens becomes more rounded, the eye is set for a nearer object; when it flattens, for a farther one (in this first look).
Example
A printed page held close asks the lens to accommodate for near work; the horizon asks for a flatter lens. This lesson does not give a measured focal length, or a count in diopters.
Not this
Not a change of the cornea’s place, and not a new retina. The cornea still does much of the bending of light (Day 32); the lens makes a further, adjustable change.
Connects to
The camera analogy is weaker here: a camera often focuses by moving a lens; the living lens changes shape. The analogy remains a map, not a mechanism we have measured in class.

Last week’s path still holds: cornea, pupil, lens, retina. Today the lens is not a fixed piece of glass.

Accommodation is the change of the lens’s shape so that a near or a far object can be focused on the retina. A more rounded lens is for near; a flatter lens is for far, in this first look.

This course will not invent a focal length for the eye, or a diopter number, or a measured time for the change. If those figures appear in other books, they are not today’s lesson unless a later, sourced lesson sets them.

The camera analogy, already limited, is weaker again: many cameras focus by moving a lens along a barrel; the eye’s lens changes shape.

Remember

Accommodation is the lens changing shape to focus near or far on the retina. More rounded for near, flatter for far, in this first look. No invented focal lengths or diopters.

Atelier · A likeness check of the features in context

50 min · Method: check the features already placed (eyes, nose, mouth, ear) as a set, against the plate or plaster, not as a new separate drawing

Learn

Key terms

likeness check in context
Definition
A slow look at whether the features, already placed, still sit in the right heights, distances, and tilts on this head, judged as a set against the plate or plaster.
Example
If the mouth has crept too far from the nose, or the ear has risen, the check should name that interval, then correct it without redrawing the whole face from a diagram.
Not this
Not a feature-by-feature “improvement” that ignores the plate, and not a new eye drawn from a remembered recipe beside a nose that was already right. The plate wins.
Connects to
Last week placed the ear and the neck, and laid in hair masses. Today those parts are in the same check as the eyes, nose, and mouth.

Squint, then measure. Check intervals: brow to nose, nose to mouth, the ear’s height and tilt, the width between the features. Correct one interval at a time if you can, and check each correction against the plate.

A likeness is often in the intervals, not in a more “finished” eyelid. Do not add lashes or strands to hide a placement that has drifted.

Remember

Check the features as a set, by intervals, against the plate. Correct a drift; do not hide it with detail. The plate wins.

Key terms: likeness check in context.

Modern language · Spanish: the weather, in the present

40 min · Method: three present-tense weather patterns, contrasted; English remains the language of instruction

Learn

Key terms

weather in the present
Definition
Present-tense patterns for the weather: hace + a weather noun or adjective in set phrases (hace frío, hace calor, hace viento, hace sol); está + an adjective (está nublado, está despejado); and hay + a noun (hay niebla, hay nubes). The subject is not a person in these set phrases.
Example
Hoy hace frío. Está nublado. Hay nubes. ¿Qué tiempo hace? Hace calor.
Not this
Not *es frío for the weather (that would describe a thing as ‘a cold thing’ in a different sentence), and not a past tense (*hizo frío is not today’s form). And not *hace nublado: nublado takes está.
Connects to
Hacer is already known (hago, hace). Today hace is used in set weather phrases, not as ‘he makes cold.’ Dates and the clock remain different patterns (Es el 5 de mayo; Son las dos).
Word How to say it Meaning
¿Qué tiempo hace? keh TYEM-poh AH-seh What’s the weather like?
Hace frío AH-seh FREE-oh It is cold
Hace calor AH-seh kah-LOR It is hot
Hace sol AH-seh sohl It is sunny
Hace viento AH-seh BYEN-toh It is windy
Está nublado es-TAH noo-BLAH-doh It is cloudy
Hay nubes eye NOO-bes There are clouds

Three present patterns, not one.

  • Hace frío / calor / sol / viento / buen tiempo. (set phrases with hace)
  • Está nublado / despejado. (estar + adjective)
  • Hay nubes / hay niebla. (hay + noun)

The question: ¿Qué tiempo hace? Original sentences: Hoy hace frío. No hace calor. Está nublado, y hay nubes. Do not write es frío or hace nublado for today’s weather meanings.

Remember

Hace frío/calor/sol/viento; está nublado; hay nubes. ¿Qué tiempo hace? Not *es frío, not *hace nublado, and still not a past tense.

Music theory · Interval inversion, with the octave as the frame

40 min · Method: two intervals that add to an octave, named from intervals already taught

Learn

Key terms

interval inversion
Definition
The partner of an interval when one of the two notes is moved by an octave, so that the old top note is now below, or the old bottom note is now above. In this first look, the interval numbers add to nine (a third inverts to a sixth, a fifth to a fourth), and a perfect interval inverts to a perfect interval, while a major interval inverts to a minor interval.
Example
C up to G is a perfect fifth; G up to the next C is a perfect fourth. C up to E is a major third; E up to the next C is a minor sixth. Each pair fills an octave.
Not this
Not a claim that the two intervals sound the same, and not a new meaning of “inverse” from yesterday’s mathematics or from the logic inverse. And not a full table of augmented and diminished inversions today.
Connects to
Day 12 already named the perfect fourth, perfect fifth, major third, and perfect octave (and Day 32 returned to the fifth and the major third). Today those intervals are paired by inversion, with the octave as the frame.
octave as a frame
Definition
The octave (from Latin octavus, “eighth”) is the interval of eight letter names, inclusive, for example C to the next C. In this lesson it is the frame that interval inversion fills: a fifth plus the fourth that inverts it make an octave.
Origin
From Latin octavus, “eighth.”
Example
C–C′ is a perfect octave. C–G and G–C′ together complete that octave.
Not this
Not a new scale, and not a claim that “eighth” is a count of steps beyond C. The count is inclusive, as already taught.
Connects to
The word’s origin is a reminder of the eight letter names, not a new counting rule.

The frame. An octave (C to the next C) has eight letter names, counted inclusively, as Day 12 taught. The word comes from Latin octavus, “eighth.”

Inversion, in this first look. Keep one note, and move the other by an octave so the pair is still the same two letter names, now in the other order:

  • A fifth inverts to a fourth (5 + 4 = 9). Perfect stays perfect: C–G (P5) inverts to G–C (P4).
  • A third inverts to a sixth (3 + 6 = 9). Major inverts to minor: C–E (M3) inverts to E–C (m6).

The numbers add to nine because the two counts share an endpoint and together fill the eight names of the octave. This lesson does not build a full table of every quality. It teaches these pairs, and the two quality rules above, on intervals you already can name.

A word caution. “Inversion” here is a music term. It is not f⁻¹, and not the logic inverse of a conditional.

Remember

An octave (from octavus, “eighth”) is the frame. A fifth inverts to a fourth, a third to a sixth (numbers add to nine). Perfect stays perfect; major inverts to minor. Not the math or logic inverse.
