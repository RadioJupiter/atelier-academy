Latin · Friday mix: purpose clauses with ut, and the futures

40 min · Method: Friday mix of this week’s purpose clauses with already-taught tenses; nē reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a purpose clause with ut, a first look — Day 156
  • a purpose clause with ut and ēgredior — Day 157

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes purpose clauses with ut and the already-taught tenses of sequor and ēgredior. A purpose clause uses ut and the present subjunctive: ut sequātur, ut ēgrediātur. The futures sequētur and ēgrediētur are not purpose clauses. The two verbs are not swapped. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg. A negative purpose with nē remains reserved.

Remember

Purpose: ut sequātur, ut ēgrediātur (ā). Future: sequētur, ēgrediētur (ē). Sequor is to follow; ēgredior is to go out. Nē remains reserved.

Watch me

  4. Secūtī erunt, or secūtae erunt.

The first is a purpose of going out. The second is the future of following.

Hint 2: The vowels

Purpose uses the present subjunctive, ā; the future has ē.

Mathematics · Algebra / analysis: Friday mix of this week’s limits

40 min · Method: Friday mix of approaching, a hole, and 1/n; invented figures; continuity reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a limit, a first look — Day 156
  • a limit where the function is not defined at the point — Day 157
  • a limit as a positive integer grows large — Day 158

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This Friday mixes this week’s limits. A limit is a value approached as an input approaches a chosen number, or as a positive integer grows large. lim x→3 2x = 6. lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4, though the expression is not defined at 2. lim n→∞ 1/n = 0, and ∞ is not a number to substitute. The figures are invented and labeled invented. Continuity, and a formal epsilon argument, remain reserved.

Remember

A limit is a value approached. Substitution may agree, but is not the definition. A limit may exist where the function is not defined. As n grows large, 1/n approaches 0, and ∞ is not a number to substitute.

Watch me

  1. lim x→3 2x = 6, from the invented table and from approaching.
  2. lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4, though the expression is not defined at 2.
  3. lim n→∞ 1/n = 0, as n grows large.

Together

Hint 2: The miss

The original expression is not defined at 3. The limit is 6; that is not the same as writing k(3) = 6.

Key terms

law of refraction (Snell’s law)
Definition
In OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 25.3, the law of refraction, also called Snell’s law, is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. The angles “are measured relative to a perpendicular to the surface at the point where the light ray crosses it,” the same normal already used for reflection. Official Example 25.2 takes medium 1 as air, n₁ = 1.00, θ₁ = 30.0°, θ₂ = 22.0°, and finds n₂ = (1.00 × 0.500) / 0.375 = 1.33, which the book identifies as the index of refraction for water.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 25.3, states the law of refraction, also called Snell’s law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. The angles are measured from the perpendicular to the surface at the point where the ray crosses it, the same normal already used last week for reflection. When the speed decreases, the ray moves closer to the normal; when the speed increases, it moves away from the normal. Official Example 25.2 takes medium 1 as air, with n₁ = 1.00, an incident angle θ₁ = 30.0°, and a refraction angle θ₂ = 22.0°. Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is a limit, in this week’s first look, and why is substituting x = 2 into (x² − 4)/(x − 2) a miss at the point itself?
  2. A limit is a value that a quantity approaches as an input approaches a chosen number, or as a positive integer grows large.
  3. The expression (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not defined at x = 2, because the denominator is zero there. The limit as x approaches 2 is 4, from the invented table and from the already-taught factorization, but that is not the same as substituting x = 2 into the unsimplified fraction.
  4. A second miss is to write “1/∞ = 0” as a substitution. ∞ is not a number to substitute; lim n→∞ 1/n = 0 is the approaching as n grows large.

Together

In your own words, what is an encomium in this course, and what invented subject was praised this week? How does an encomium differ from a commonplace?

Hint 1: The subject

An encomium praises a general type, or an already-taught work, with already-taught reasons. This week’s invented subject was “The Crow and the Pitcher” as a fable well made, not as history.

Hint 2: The commonplace

A commonplace amplifies a general, already-acknowledged fault of a type, as with last week’s treating a fable as history. An encomium is a different job, and it does not name a living person.
