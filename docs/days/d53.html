Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: is, ea, eum, and eam

40 min · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences, Capitula I–X only. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

is, ea, eum, and eam
Definition
Third-person pronouns already taught in Capitula I–X (Day 7): is and ea are nominative (he, she); eum and eam are accusative (him, her). Reviewed today, not as a new chapter.
Example
Puer est. Puella eum videt. The girl sees him.
Not this
Not the same as the interrogatives quis and quem, and not a new pronoun set from a later chapter.
Connects to
Day 7 introduced these forms. quis and quem were already used as questions; today is, ea, eum, and eam point back to someone already named.

eum EH-um him (accusative)
eam EH-am her (accusative)

Mathematics · Algebra XVIII: a downward-opening parabola, and a square

40 min · Method: the already-taught shape still decides the intervals; a square is a special graph that never goes below the axis

Learn

Key terms

a downward-opening quadratic inequality
Definition
When the x² coefficient is negative, the already-taught parabola opens downward, so the regions swap: y > 0 between the roots, and y < 0 outside them.
Example
y = −(x − 1)(x − 5) opens downward. y > 0 for 1 < x < 5.
Not this
Not a change of method: we still read the graph. We do not flip inequality signs by a memorized rule that forgets the shape.
Connects to
Monday’s upward-opening case is the companion. The graph still decides the intervals.
a perfect-square inequality
Definition
A quadratic that is a square, such as (x − 3)², is never negative. (x − 3)² ≥ 0 for all real x, and (x − 3)² = 0 only at x = 3. (x − 3)² < 0 has no real solution.
Example
(x − 3)² > 0 is true for every real x except 3.
Not this
Not a claim that every quadratic is a square, and not a claim about complex numbers, which this course has not begun.
Connects to
Perfect squares and quadratic graphs are already taught. Today they are read as an inequality.

Monday’s method still holds: write one side as zero, mark the roots, and read the graph. What changes today is the shape in two special cases.

A downward-opening parabola. If y = −(x − 1)(x − 5), the leading coefficient is negative, so the parabola opens downward. It is above the x-axis between the roots, and below the axis outside them. So y > 0 when 1 < x < 5, and y < 0 when x < 1 or x > 5.

We can also think of y = −(x − 1)(x − 5) as the opposite of Monday’s kind of product: the sign is flipped, so the inequality direction, once we have the shape, is flipped with it. The safe habit is still to sketch the already-taught shape, not to memorize a flip in isolation.

A perfect square. y = (x − 3)² is a parabola that opens upward and touches the x-axis only at 3 (a repeated root). The graph never goes below the axis.

  • (x − 3)² ≥ 0 for every real x; equality only at x = 3.
  • (x − 3)² > 0 for every real x except 3.
  • (x − 3)² < 0 has no real solution.
  • (x − 3)² ≤ 0 only at x = 3.

What we will not do. We will not invent a downward-opening shape for a positive x² coefficient, and we will not begin complex numbers in order to “solve” (x − 3)² < 0.

Remember

If the parabola opens downward, y > 0 between the roots. A square (x − a)² is never negative: > 0 except at x = a, where it is 0. (x − a)² < 0 has no real solution. Still read the graph.

Key terms: a downward-opening quadratic inequality, a perfect-square inequality.

Watch me

  2. (x − 3)² is positive everywhere except at the touch point x = 3.
  3. So (x − 3)² > 0 for all real x except 3.

The downward-opening parabola is below the axis outside the roots: x < 1 or x > 5.

Physics · Weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass

40 min · Method: W = m g, already taught; Mars g ≈ 3.7 m/s²; invented masses stay labeled

Learn

Key terms

weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass
Definition
Weight as the already-taught W = m g, using today’s Mars g ≈ 3.7 m/s² and an invented classroom mass that stays labeled invented. The mass is not a NASA figure.
Example
For an invented 50 kg mass, W on Mars is 50 × 3.7 = 185 N, beside 50 × 9.8 = 490 N on Earth (working g).
Not this
Not a change of mass with the planet, and not an invented person or identity. Mass stays the same; weight changes because g changes.
Connects to
Week 8 taught mass and weight. Today the g is Mars’s about 3.7 m/s².

Weight is the already-taught W = m g. Mass does not change when we imagine the same object on another body. g does change.

On Mars, use g ≈ 3.7 m/s², labeled as Monday’s computation and as the fact-sheet listed value. On Earth, keep the working 9.8 m/s².

An invented classroom mass. A 50 kg mass in a practice item is invented, and stays labeled invented. It is not a NASA measurement, and it is not a person’s identity.

  • Earth: W = 50 × 9.8 = 490 N.
  • Mars: W = 50 × 3.7 = 185 N.

The Mars weight is 185 / 490 = 37/98 ≈ 0.38 of the Earth weight, the same comparison as 3.7 / 9.8. We will not invent an orbital speed, and we will not turn the 50 kg into a named person.

Remember

W = m g. Mass stays the same; g changes. Mars g ≈ 3.7 m/s²; Earth working g = 9.8 m/s². Invented masses stay labeled invented. No invented orbital speed.

Key terms: weight on Mars, for a labeled invented mass.

History · The pine-tree cloud, and what the set extract does not yet give

40 min · Source: the same Firth (1900) letter; the set extract only; later scenes reserved

Learn

Key terms

the pine-tree cloud
Definition
In Firth’s translation of Pliny’s letter, the unusual cloud is at first not clearly tied to one mountain; it is afterwards discovered to have been Vesuvius, and in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, with a long, high trunk and a spread of branches.
Example
Firth: “Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.”
Not this
Not a drawing, not a later oil painting of the eruption, and not a licence to invent a measured height in meters for the cloud.
Connects to
Monday set the letter, the uncle at Misenum, and the conventional year. Today sets the cloud description. Later scenes remain reserved.
eruption, as a later name
Definition
A later English name for the event the letter describes. The set extract itself describes an unusual cloud, afterwards tied to Vesuvius; we may say “eruption” as a conventional event-name, labeled as such, without inventing a modern scientific log the letter does not give.
Origin
From Middle French éruption, from Latin eruptio (English Wiktionary, entry “eruption”).
Example
The conventional event is the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. The set extract’s own wording is a cloud of unusual size and shape, like a pine-tree, afterwards discovered to have been Vesuvius.
Not this
Not a measured column height, not a modern volcanic classification invented in this lesson, and not a death toll.
Connects to
The letter is the source for the cloud. The event-name “eruption” is a later conventional word, kept labeled.

The set extract. J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified):

“Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance could not make out from which mountain it was rising — it was afterwards discovered to have been Mount Vesuvius — but in likeness and form it more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else, for what corresponded to the trunk was of great length and height, and then spread out into a number of branches.”

What it gives. From some distance, the mountain was not at first made out. It was afterwards discovered to have been Vesuvius. The cloud more closely resembled a pine-tree than anything else: a long, high trunk, then a spread of branches.

A later event-name. We may call the conventional event an eruption, labeled as a later name. The set sentences themselves describe a cloud, and then name Vesuvius. We will not invent a column height in kilometers, a modern eruption class, or a death toll.

Still reserved. The uncle’s decision to take ship, Rectina, Stabiae, and the uncle’s death are in later parts of the same letter, not in this extract. Towns sometimes named in later accounts (for example in a much later encyclopedia) are not, for that reason, in today’s set sentences. We will not smuggle them in as if Pliny had set them here.

Remember

Firth’s set extract: delayed identification as Vesuvius; a pine-tree likeness, trunk and branches. “Eruption” is a later event-name, labeled as such. No invented height or death toll. Later scenes reserved.

Key terms: the pine-tree cloud, eruption, as a later name.

Atelier · Two cast shadows, on the same ground

40 min · Method: one lamp; two cast shadows that agree; still not a head

Learn

Key terms

two cast shadows, one lamp
Definition
Each form throws a cast shadow on the ground. Under one lamp the two shadows lean the same way, meet the forms at a consistent place, and are not as dark as an invented hole in the paper.
Example
The cube’s shadow is often clearer at the edge of a plane; the sphere’s shadow is softer at its edge. Both still agree in direction.
Not this
Not the form’s own dark plane, not a second lamp’s shadow, and not a traced outline left as the finished shadow.
Connects to
Week 10 separated the cube’s dark plane from its cast shadow. Today both forms have shadows on the same ground.

A cast shadow is thrown on another surface when the form blocks the lamp. It is not the form’s own dark plane.

Today’s job. Put both shadows on the same ground, under the same lamp. They lean the same way. The cube’s shadow often has a clearer edge where a plane cuts the light; the sphere’s shadow often softens as it leaves the form. Neither shadow needs to be the blackest thing you can make; a hole in the paper is usually a darker invention than the ground warrants.

Still not a head. Do not turn a shadow into a silhouette of a profile.

Remember

Two cast shadows, one lamp, one ground, same direction. A shadow is not the form’s dark plane. Do not invent a hole in the paper. Still not a head.

Key terms: two cast shadows, one lamp.

Modern language · Spanish: antes de and después de + infinitive

40 min · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only; infinitive after de

Learn

Key terms

antes de and después de, with an infinitive
Definition
Two present-tense time phrases: antes de + infinitive (before doing something) and después de + infinitive (after doing something). Instruction stays in English. They do not, today, begin a past-tense clause with a conjugated verb after de.
Example
Antes de leer, escribe. Después de leer, escribe.
Not this
Not a past-tense lesson, and not antes de que (a later pattern this course has not taught, and one that would pull in a new mood).
Connects to
ir a + infinitive and acabar de + infinitive were already taught. Today is a different job: before and after an action, still with an infinitive.
Word How to say it Meaning
antes de AHN-tes deh before (with an infinitive today)
después de des-PWEHS deh after (with an infinitive today)
antes de leer AHN-tes deh leh-EHR before reading
después de leer des-PWEHS deh leh-EHR after reading

antes de + infinitive means before doing something. después de + infinitive means after doing something. The verb after de stays in the infinitive today. Instruction stays in English.

Original sentences: Antes de leer, escribe. Después de leer, escribe. Antes de escribir, lee. Después de escribir, lee.

We do not begin antes de que or a new mood today.

For the guide: mime reading then writing. Keep the verb after de in the infinitive. Present time; no past-tense lesson.

Remember

antes de + infinitive: before doing. después de + infinitive: after doing. No antes de que today. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words.

Key terms: antes de and después de, with an infinitive.

30 min · Method: Platner and Ashby, Amphitheatrum Flavium (1929); a later building, not a photograph of 79

Learn

Key terms

the Flavian amphitheater (the Colosseum)
Definition
The amphitheater ordinarily known as the Colosseum, in Platner and Ashby’s entry Amphitheatrum Flavium (1929): built by Vespasian on the site of the stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea; carried to the second arcade and dedicated before Vespasian’s death in 79; Titus added the third and fourth stories and celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with games that lasted one hundred days. A later building, not a photograph of 79.
Example
Platner: Vespasian “carried the structure to the top of the second arcade of the outer wall” and “dedicated it before his death in 79 A.D.” Titus “added the third and fourth stories” and “celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with magnificent games that lasted one hundred days.”
Not this
Not a finished monument of 79 in the form later seen, and not a claim that this course has independently read Suetonius. The hundred-day games are taught as Platner, citing Suetonius, Titus 7 (and other authorities Platner lists). Not a death toll, and not a picture of the pine-tree cloud.

What Platner says (1929, as presented on LacusCurtius). The amphitheater was built by Vespasian, in the depression between the Velia, the Esquiline, and the Caelian, “a site previously occupied by the stagnum of Nero’s domus Aurea.” Vespasian “carried the structure to the top of the second arcade of the outer wall” and of the maenianum secundum of the cavea, “and dedicated it before his death in 79 A.D.” Titus “added the third and fourth stories,” and “celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with magnificent games that lasted one hundred days.” For those games Platner cites Suetonius, Titus 7, among other authorities. This course cites Platner, and notes that citation; it does not pretend to have read Suetonius independently this sitting.

What that changes. A building dedicated, in part, before 79 is not the same as the enlarged building whose dedication games Platner places in 80. A modern view of the Colosseum is not a photograph of 79, and not a photograph of the pine-tree cloud in this week’s history letter.

We will not invent a seating capacity Platner has not been quoted for here, and we will not treat the entry as a tourist caption.
