Latin · Capitulum IV, part 2 and two-week check

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. IV

Learn

This is a two-week check of the Latin taught so far, not a new chapter point. You should be able to: read a short sentence aloud; say whether a word is the subject or the object; make a simple adjective agree; use est / sunt and videt / vident; greet someone with salvē or salvēte.

Read aloud the next third of Capitulum IV (15 min). Then do the check without the book.

Remember

Nominative and accusative. Agreement of adjectives. Verb endings for number. Salvē / salvēte. Read first, then say what the sentence tells you.

Watch me

A review sentence: Puellae oppidum magnum vident. The girls see a large town. Puellae is the subject (plural), vident matches it, oppidum magnum is the object (neuter, so the adjective is magnum).

Together

Make 'the boy sees a large island' in Latin. Īnsula is feminine.

Hint 1: Object

īnsulam magnam — feminine accusative.

Hint 2: Sentence

Puer īnsulam magnam videt.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. In puellae oppidum magnum vident, why is the verb vident?
  2. What is the difference between salvē and salvēte?

Two-week check (15 min)

  1. Give nominative plural, genitive singular and accusative singular: fluvius, insula, oppidum, puer.
  2. Translate into Latin: (a) The islands of Greece are not large. (b) Why does the slave call the master? (c) Slave, listen! (d) The girl sees the boy, but the boy does not see her.
  3. Correct the error in each: (a) Dominus servus vocat. (b) Ancilla, venit! (c) Oppida magnī sunt.

Mathematics · Two-week problem set

60 min · In the tradition of the Russian problem books: many short problems in graded difficulty, mixing every topic so far. Work without notes for 50 minutes.

Learn

This is a two-week problem set, not a new technique. Use what was taught: bar models to equations, definitions and short proofs, the three standard identities, congruence tests, quadratic solving, parallel lines and a transversal, inequalities (reverse the sign when multiplying by a negative), the triangle inequality, and simple functions and graphs. Draw a diagram or a bar when the relationship is the point.

  1. A sculptor used 1/4 of a block of clay for a bust and 2/3 of the rest for small figures. 150 g were left. How much clay was there at first? (Draw the model.)
  2. Factor x⁴ + 4. (Hint: add and subtract 4x².)
  3. Solve x² − 2x − 15 = 0 and check with Vieta.
  4. In triangle ABC, ∠A = 80°. The bisectors of angles B and C meet at I. Find ∠BIC.
  5. Solve |2x − 3| < 5.
  6. Find the minimum of 2x + 8/x for x > 0, and where it occurs.
  7. Can a triangle have sides 3, 5 and 9? Prove your answer.
  8. Prove that n³ − n is divisible by 6 for every integer n.
  9. Sketch y = |x − 1| − 2 and state where it crosses the x-axis.
  10. (Stretch.) Prove that in any triangle, a median is shorter than half the sum of the two sides that meet at its vertex. (Hint: extend the median to twice its length.)

Remember

Bars before equations. Reasons in proofs. Identities, congruence tests, F = the facts on parallels, quadratic checks, the sign-reverse rule, the triangle inequality, f(x) and its graph. No new method is required today.

Watch me

A reminder at the level of the set, with different numbers: a tailor used 1/3 of a length of linen, then 3/4 of the remainder. 50 cm was left. How long was the piece at the start?

  1. After the first cut, 2/3 remains. Then 3/4 of that remainder is used, so 1/4 of the remainder is left.
  2. 1/4 of 2/3 is 1/6 of the whole. 1/6 of the whole = 50 cm, so the whole is 300 cm.
  3. Check: 300 → 100 used, 200 left → 150 used, 50 left. ✓ This is the same remainder structure as item 1 of the set, not that item itself.

Together

Before the set: solve x² − 2x − 8 = 0, and check the roots with Vieta (sum and product). This is the same method as item 3 of the set, with different numbers.

Hint 1: Factor or complete the square

(x − 4)(x + 2) = 0, so x = 4 or x = −2. (Or (x − 1)² = 9.)

Hint 2: Vieta

Sum 4 + (−2) = 2, matching −(−2); product 4 · (−2) = −8. ✓

Check

These questions use skills from the two weeks, at the level of the set, but they are not items from the set.

  1. Solve x² + 4x − 12 = 0 and check with Vieta.
  2. Can 4, 7 and 12 be the sides of a triangle? Why or why not?

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium III, rhetoric: the three appeals

45 min · Method: Aristotle, Rhetoric (the three "proofs"), taught as the final stage of the Trivium

Learn

Rhetoric, in the trivium, is the craft of persuasion. Aristotle names three appeals the speaker provides: ethos (character and credibility), pathos (the audience's emotional state), and logos (the argument itself). Honest rhetoric uses all three; manipulation uses pathos or ethos to hide a weak logos. Today's check is to name the appeals in a short passage and to write a brief argument that uses them cleanly.

The appeals. Aristotle names three means of persuasion that the speaker provides: ethos, the speaker's character and credibility; pathos, putting the audience in the right emotional state; logos, the argument itself, the proof or apparent proof. Good rhetoric uses all three honestly; manipulation uses pathos or ethos to replace logos.

In the sources you have read. Pericles uses pathos (the dead, the city's glory) and ethos (the leader speaking for the city). Socrates' gadfly passage uses logos and a vivid figure, and refuses the pathos usual in Athenian courts. (In the Apology he notes that he will not bring his weeping children before the jury.)

Remember

Ethos: why we should trust the speaker. Pathos: the state of the audience. Logos: the proof. Name them; do not let feeling stand in for a missing reason.

Watch me

A clean three-part sketch: I have measured this plate twice (ethos: care). If the height is wrong, the head will not sit (logos). You already know how a wrong proportion spoils a likeness (pathos: a shared frustration). So we should correct the height before we shade.

Together

A speech that only flatters the listener and never offers a reason: which appeal is it leaning on, and what is missing?

Hint 1: Lean

Pathos (and perhaps a cheap ethos).

Hint 2: Missing

Logos — a real argument.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Name the three appeals.
  2. Why is a strong feeling not enough, on its own, to make a good argument?

Exercises

  1. Write a persuasive paragraph (150–200 words) on: Every serious art student should study Latin. Plan it as an SPO first. Use all three appeals and label each in the margin.
  2. Revise it with this checklist: topic sentence arguable? Each claim supported? One sentence with although? One appositive? No sentence that could be cut without loss?
  3. Identify the main appeal in each: (a) As a surgeon for twenty years, I can tell you that this treatment is safe. (b) Imagine the faces of the children who will have no school. (c) The data from all eight trials show the same effect.

Physics · Newton's third law and the two-week check

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Newton's third law: if A exerts a force on B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different bodies, so they do not cancel each other. A book pushes down on a table; the table pushes up on the book. Today's work also reviews two weeks of motion and laws: take the check slowly, and use a small free-body sketch when forces appear.

Pre-reading. When object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. A rocket pushes exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward.

ConcepTests

  1. A heavy truck and a small car collide head-on. During the collision: (A) the truck exerts a larger force on the car than the car exerts on the truck (B) the forces are equal in size (C) the car exerts the larger force.
  2. In the same collision, which experiences the larger acceleration? (A) the truck (B) the car (C) the same.
  3. A horse pulls a cart. By the third law the cart pulls back on the horse equally. How can they move forward? (A) They can't (B) The horse pushes backward on the ground, and the ground pushes the horse forward with a force larger than the cart's pull (C) The horse's force is slightly larger.

Remember

Forces come in pairs, equal and opposite, on two bodies. They never cancel as if they acted on the same body. Fnet = ma* still holds for each body separately.

Watch me

A 600 N person stands on the floor. The person pushes the floor down with 600 N; the floor pushes the person up with 600 N. Those two forces are a third-law pair. They do not cancel, because one acts on the floor and one on the person. The net force on the person can still be zero, because a different pair (the person's weight and the floor's upward push on the person) act on the same body.

Together

A rocket pushes exhaust backward. What is the pair of that force, and which body speeds up forward?

Hint 1: Pair

The exhaust pushes the rocket forward, equally hard.

Hint 2: Which body

The rocket (and the exhaust goes backward). The pair acts on two bodies.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Why do a third-law pair of forces not cancel each other?
  2. A box sits at rest on a table. Is the table's upward force on the box the third-law pair of the box's weight, or of the box's push on the table?

Two-week check (problems)

  1. A ball is thrown up at 20 m/s. How high does it rise? How long until it returns?
  2. A 1,500 kg car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 8.0 s. Find the net force.
  3. A 70 kg skater pushes off a wall with an average force of 140 N. What is the skater's acceleration, and what force does the skater exert on the wall?

Atelier · Critique and plan

90 min

Learn

Critique is a method, not a mood. Set the cast drawing, the egg, and a Bargue plate together, at the same distance, and photograph them if you wish. Then answer a fixed list: are the large proportions right? Do the value families still separate? Have halftones stayed in the light? Only after that do you name one or two goals for the next fortnight. Finish the cast first, then critique.

Finish the cast drawing (60 min): resolve halftones, check edges, and put in the few darkest accents (the occlusion shadows where the cast touches its base).

Critique (30 min), the atelier way: set the cast drawing and the egg beside your Bargue plate, all at the same distance, and photograph them. Then answer in writing: 1. Proportion: where is each drawing most accurate, and least? 2. Value: does the shadow family read as one mass in each? 3. Edges: where are they too hard? 4. Which single habit, if corrected, would most improve all three?

Remember

Finish the work, then compare at a distance. Ask about proportion, then value families, then a few edges and accents. Write one or two goals, not a dozen.

Watch me

Demonstration of a critique order:

  1. Stand at the viewing point (or look at the photograph) and check height against width, then a plumb alignment.
  2. Squint: does the shadow still read as one family, lighter or darker than the cast?
  3. Check that no halftone has fallen into the shadow, and that a few dark accents are reserved for real contacts and recesses.
  4. Write one proportion goal and one value goal for the next period of work. Stop there.

Together

If the likeness is already pleasing but the height is still short, what goes on the goal list?

Hint 1: Priority

The height (a large proportion) comes before a pleasing finish.

Hint 2: Goal

A sentence such as: 'Next time, establish height and width before any modeling.'

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What should you check first in a critique: accents or large proportions?
  2. How many goals should you write down at the end, and why not a long list?

Modern language · Spanish: review and composition

25 min

Learn

This is a two-week check of the Spanish taught so far: sounds; articles and gender; ser and estar; present of regular -ar, -er, -ir verbs; tener and ir; numbers 0–30; and question words. Study the forms once more, then do the check without turning it into a guess.

Word How to say it Meaning
ser sehr to be (identity, quality)
estar es-TAR to be (place, condition)
tener teh-NEHR to have
ir eer to go
tengo que TENG-goh keh I have to
voy a voy ah I am going to
¿dónde? DOHN-deh where?
¿cuándo? KWAHN-doh when?

Two-week check

  1. Conjugate ser, estar, tener, ir in the present.
  2. Translate: (a) The museum is in the center and it is very old. (b) We have to study, but we are tired. (c) Are you (tú) going to draw on Saturday? (d) They live in a small city and they write a lot.
  3. Write 8–10 sentences about your ideal school day, using at least one -ar, one -er and one -ir verb, and ir a + infinitive.

Remember

Ser for what something is; estar for where and how it is. Tener que + infinitive; ir a + infinitive. Articles match gender.

Watch me

El museo está en el centro y es muy antiguo. The museum is in the center (estar, place) and it is very old (ser, quality). Tenemos que estudiar, pero estamos cansados. We have to study, but we are tired (estar, condition).

Together

Translate: 'Are you (tú) going to draw on Saturday?'

Hint 1: Pattern

ir a + dibujar, with vas, and el sábado.

Hint 2: Sentence

¿Vas a dibujar el sábado?

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Ser or estar: 'We are tired.'
  2. How do you say 'I have to study'?

Elective · Poetry: meter and the iambic pentameter

30 min · Seminar rotation: Elective

Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line. The unit is the foot: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), spondee (DUM-DUM). Iambic pentameter is five iambs per line, the most common meter of English verse from Chaucer onward, and the line of Shakespeare's sonnets and blank verse.

Model (Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, lines 1–4, public domain):

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate: Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, And summer's lease hath all too short a date:

Exercises

  1. Scan all four lines: mark each syllable as stressed (/) or unstressed (×), and divide the feet.
  2. Where does the rhythm vary from strict iambs? What effect does the variation have?
  3. Write four lines of your own in iambic pentameter, rhyming ABAB, describing a drawing in progress.
