Latin · Capitulum IV, part 2 and two-week check

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. IV

Learn

Key terms

declension
Definition
The pattern of endings a noun (and its adjectives) follows as the case and number change.
Origin
From Latin dēclīnātiō, from dēclīnāre (‘to lean away, to inflect a word’).
Example
First-declension nouns such as vīlla have one pattern; second-declension nouns such as puer and oppidum have another, with a neuter branch.
Not this
A declension is not a tense, and it is not ‘whatever ending looks familiar’.
Connects to
Case (Day 8) is the role; declension is the pattern that shows the role. The two-week check uses both.
conjugation
Definition
The pattern of endings a verb follows as the person, number, and (later) tense change.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin coniugātiō, from coniugāre (‘to join together’).
Example
Est and sunt belong to the irregular conjugation of ‘to be’; regular -āre verbs will have a different, predictable set of endings.
Not this
A conjugation is not a declension. Verbs conjugate; nouns decline.
Connects to
Verb endings (Day 7) are pieces of a conjugation. This check asks you to keep noun and verb patterns distinct.

This is a two-week check of the Latin taught so far, not a new chapter point. You should be able to: read a short sentence aloud; say whether a word is the subject or the object; make a simple adjective agree; use est / sunt and videt / vident; greet someone with salvē or salvēte.

Read aloud the next third of Capitulum IV (15 min). Then do the check without the book.

Remember

Nominative and accusative. Agreement of adjectives. Verb endings for number. Salvē / salvēte. Read first, then say what the sentence tells you.

Key terms: declension, conjugation.

Mathematics · Two-week problem set

60 min · In the tradition of the Russian problem books: many short problems in graded difficulty, mixing every topic so far. Work without notes for 50 minutes.

Learn

Key terms

heuristic
Definition
A deliberate method of attack, not a guess: for example, draw a bar, try a smaller case, or write a short proof plan before computing.
Origin
An irregular formation from Ancient Greek εὑρίσκω (‘to find’) (compare the regular Greek term εὑρετικός).
Example
For a fraction-of-a-remainder word problem, the heuristic is: draw the whole, mark the first fraction, then cut only the remainder.
Not this
A heuristic is not ‘hoping the first idea works’ and not a substitute for a reason when a proof is asked for.
Connects to
This set uses the methods of Days 1–9. The word names the habit of choosing a method on purpose.
unit (in a bar or ratio)
Definition
One equal part in a bar model or a ratio. A stated change (an amount spent, a number added) is often a whole number of units, which is how the unit is found.
Example
If 3 units = $90, then 1 unit = $30, and 7 units = $210.
Not this
A unit here is not the same as a unit of measurement such as a meter, and it is not ‘whatever number looks convenient’ if it does not match both sides of the ratio.
Connects to
This is the same unit used in Day 1’s unchanged-quantity ratio problems.

This is a two-week problem set, not a new technique. Use what was taught: bar models to equations, definitions and short proofs, the three standard identities, congruence tests, quadratic solving, parallel lines and a transversal, inequalities (reverse the sign when multiplying by a negative), the triangle inequality, and simple functions and graphs. Draw a diagram or a bar when the relationship is the point.

  1. A sculptor used 1/4 of a block of clay for a bust and 2/3 of the rest for small figures. 150 g were left. How much clay was there at first? (Draw the model.)
  2. Factor x⁴ + 4. (Hint: add and subtract 4x².)
  3. Solve x² − 2x − 15 = 0 and check with Vieta.
  4. In triangle ABC, ∠A = 80°. The bisectors of angles B and C meet at I. Find ∠BIC.
  5. Solve |2x − 3| < 5.
  6. Find the minimum of 2x + 8/x for x > 0, and where it occurs.
  7. Can a triangle have sides 3, 5 and 9? Prove your answer.
  8. Prove that n³ − n is divisible by 6 for every integer n.
  9. Sketch y = |x − 1| − 2 and state where it crosses the x-axis.
  10. (Stretch.) Prove that in any triangle, a median is shorter than half the sum of the two sides that meet at its vertex. (Hint: extend the median to twice its length.)

Remember

Bars before equations. Reasons in proofs. Identities, congruence tests, F = the facts on parallels, quadratic checks, the sign-reverse rule, the triangle inequality, f(x) and its graph. No new method is required today.

Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium III, rhetoric: the three appeals

45 min · Method: Aristotle, Rhetoric (the three "proofs"), taught as the final stage of the Trivium

Learn

Key terms

ethos
Definition
The character the speaker or writer seems to have: credible, careful, fair. One of the three appeals in classical rhetoric.
Origin
From Ancient Greek ἦθος (‘character, custom’).
Example
A sourcing sentence that admits Plato’s loyalty to Socrates builds ethos, because it shows the writer is not hiding a problem in the evidence.
Not this
Ethos is not ‘being famous’, and it is not a substitute for evidence.
Connects to
The trivium’s third part, rhetoric, uses the claims and warrants you already write, plus these three appeals.
pathos
Definition
The appeal to feeling: the reader’s pity, anger, or loyalty, used on purpose and still tied to a claim.
Origin
From Ancient Greek πάθος (‘suffering, feeling, experience’).
Example
Hector’s infant shrinking from the helmet is pathos, and it supports a claim about what the war costs a family.
Not this
Pathos is not ‘being emotional instead of arguing’, and it is not automatically dishonest. It becomes dishonest when it replaces evidence.
Connects to
Use it alongside ethos and logos, not instead of them.
logos
Definition
The appeal to reason: the structure of claim, evidence, and warrant, and the consistency of the argument.
Origin
From Ancient Greek λόγος (‘word, account, reason’).
Example
A paragraph that states a claim, gives a quotation with a line number, and explains the warrant is working by logos.
Not this
Logos is not ‘using Greek terms’, and it is not a list of facts without a claim.
Connects to
Day 8’s claim, evidence, and warrant are logos, now named as one of the three appeals.

Rhetoric, in the trivium, is the craft of persuasion. Aristotle names three appeals the speaker provides: ethos (character and credibility), pathos (the audience's emotional state), and logos (the argument itself). Honest rhetoric uses all three; manipulation uses pathos or ethos to hide a weak logos. Today's check is to name the appeals in a short passage and to write a brief argument that uses them cleanly.

The appeals. Aristotle names three means of persuasion that the speaker provides: ethos, the speaker's character and credibility; pathos, putting the audience in the right emotional state; logos, the argument itself, the proof or apparent proof. Good rhetoric uses all three honestly; manipulation uses pathos or ethos to replace logos.

In the sources you have read. Pericles uses pathos (the dead, the city's glory) and ethos (the leader speaking for the city). Socrates' gadfly passage uses logos and a vivid figure, and refuses the pathos usual in Athenian courts. (In the Apology he notes that he will not bring his weeping children before the jury.)

Remember

Ethos: why we should trust the speaker. Pathos: the state of the audience. Logos: the proof. Name them; do not let feeling stand in for a missing reason.

Key terms: ethos, pathos, logos.

Watch me

A clean three-part sketch: I have measured this plate twice (ethos: care). If the height is wrong, the head will not sit (logos). You already know how a wrong proportion spoils a likeness (pathos: a shared frustration). So we should correct the height before we shade.

Together

A speech that only flatters the listener and never offers a reason: which appeal is it leaning on, and what is missing?

Hint 1: Lean

Pathos (and perhaps a cheap ethos).

Hint 2: Missing

Logos — a real argument.

Physics · Newton's third law and the two-week check

45 min · Peer Instruction protocol

Learn

Key terms

Newton's third law
Definition
If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size, opposite in direction, and of the same type. The two forces act on different bodies.
Example
A pushes B to the right; B pushes A to the left with a force of the same size.
Not this
The two forces do not cancel, because they do not act on the same body. The third law is not ‘every action has an equal reaction’ if that is taken to mean the forces are on one object and so nothing can accelerate.
Connects to
The second law still governs each body separately: Fnet on that body equals m a of that body.
action–reaction pair
Definition
The two forces named by the third law: they are equal, opposite, simultaneous, of the same type, and they act on two different bodies.
Example
The downward force of a book on a table, and the upward force of the table on the book.
Not this
The weight of the book and the normal force on the book are not an action–reaction pair: both act on the book. (They may cancel, but that is the first and second laws on one body.)
Connects to
This is the third law, applied carefully so it is not confused with balanced forces on a single object.

Newton's third law: if A exerts a force on B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different bodies, so they do not cancel each other. A book pushes down on a table; the table pushes up on the book. Today's work also reviews two weeks of motion and laws: take the check slowly, and use a small free-body sketch when forces appear.

Pre-reading. When object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. A rocket pushes exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward.

ConcepTests

  1. A heavy truck and a small car collide head-on. During the collision: (A) the truck exerts a larger force on the car than the car exerts on the truck (B) the forces are equal in size (C) the car exerts the larger force.
  2. In the same collision, which experiences the larger acceleration? (A) the truck (B) the car (C) the same.
  3. A horse pulls a cart. By the third law the cart pulls back on the horse equally. How can they move forward? (A) They can't (B) The horse pushes backward on the ground, and the ground pushes the horse forward with a force larger than the cart's pull (C) The horse's force is slightly larger.

Remember

Forces come in pairs, equal and opposite, on two bodies. They never cancel as if they acted on the same body. Fnet = ma* still holds for each body separately.

Key terms: Newton's third law, action–reaction pair.

Watch me

A 600 N person stands on the floor. The person pushes the floor down with 600 N; the floor pushes the person up with 600 N. Those two forces are a third-law pair. They do not cancel, because one acts on the floor and one on the person. The net force on the person can still be zero, because a different pair (the person's weight and the floor's upward push on the person) act on the same body.

Together

A rocket pushes exhaust backward. What is the pair of that force, and which body speeds up forward?

Hint 1: Pair

The exhaust pushes the rocket forward, equally hard.

Hint 2: Which body

The rocket (and the exhaust goes backward). The pair acts on two bodies.

History · Synthesis: law, citizenship and judgment

45 min · Document-based essay using Days 3, 5, 6 and 8

Learn

Key terms

synthesis
Definition
A reasoned putting-together of several sources around an idea, not a tour of one document after another.
Origin
From Latin synthesis, from Ancient Greek σύνθεσις (‘a putting together; composition’), from συν- (‘together’) + τίθημι (‘set, place’).
Example
An essay organized by the idea of ‘who may judge whom’, using Hammurabi, Herodotus, Pericles, and the Apology in the same paragraph when they speak to that idea.
Not this
Synthesis is not a summary of each source in turn, and it is not a list of quotations without a thesis.
Connects to
Every document still needs a sourcing sentence (Days 1–8). Comparison lives inside the paragraphs.
judgment
Definition
The act of deciding a case, or the standard by which people are judged. In this course the word also means your own reasoned decision about a source.
Origin
From Middle English juggement, from Old French jugement, from Late Latin iūdicāmentum, from Latin iūdicō; in English, judge + -ment.
Example
Hammurabi presents judgment as a king’s decree applied by rank; the Apology asks whether the citizens’ judgment must itself answer to reason.
Not this
Judgment is not ‘having an opinion’. In a document-based essay it must be argued from sourced evidence.
Connects to
The essay question is about who should be judged, by whom, and by what standard, in the sources you already know.

Question. What did the ancient sources studied so far believe about who should be judged, by whom, and by what standard?

Use all four documents: Hammurabi's laws (sections 196–199), Herodotus's opening, Pericles' Funeral Oration (2.37) and Plato's Apology. Write 500 words: a thesis, three body paragraphs organized by idea rather than by document, and a conclusion. For every document you use, include one sentence of sourcing (who, when, why, how reliable).

Remember

A document-based essay is organized by idea, not by a tour of one source after another. Every document still needs a sourcing sentence (who, when, why, how reliable). The four documents: Hammurabi 196–199, Herodotus's opening, Pericles 2.37, and Plato's Apology.

Key terms: synthesis, judgment.

Herodotus supplies the method: inquire into causes, not only list events. Each will need a sourcing sentence in the essay.

Atelier · Critique and plan

90 min

Learn

Key terms

critique
Definition
A structured look at a finished or nearly finished drawing, naming what is true (shape, value, family) and what must be corrected, with a reason for each point.
Origin
From French critique, from the Greek-derived critic; used here as a careful judgment of work, not as an insult.
Example
‘The block-in height is true, but the terminator on the cheek is too high, so the face looks turned away from the light.’
Not this
A critique is not a list of praises, and it is not ‘I like it’ or ‘I don’t like it’ with no reason.
Connects to
Every term from sight-size through halftone is a possible point of critique. The plan for the next plate starts from what this one taught.
plan (for the next work)
Definition
A short, practical list of what to keep and what to change in the next set-up or the next drawing, written after the critique.
Example
‘Keep this viewing distance. Next time, stand the cast a little more toward the light so the core shadow reads, and check the plumb of the ear to the ankle early.’
Not this
A plan is not a hope, and it is not a new method invented for one day. It uses the same sequence, with one or two disciplined changes.
Connects to
The critique names the facts; the plan turns them into the next session’s set-up.

Critique is a method, not a mood. Set the cast drawing, the egg, and a Bargue plate together, at the same distance, and photograph them if you wish. Then answer a fixed list: are the large proportions right? Do the value families still separate? Have halftones stayed in the light? Only after that do you name one or two goals for the next fortnight. Finish the cast first, then critique.

Finish the cast drawing (60 min): resolve halftones, check edges, and put in the few darkest accents (the occlusion shadows where the cast touches its base).

Critique (30 min), the atelier way: set the cast drawing and the egg beside your Bargue plate, all at the same distance, and photograph them. Then answer in writing: 1. Proportion: where is each drawing most accurate, and least? 2. Value: does the shadow family read as one mass in each? 3. Edges: where are they too hard? 4. Which single habit, if corrected, would most improve all three?

Remember

Finish the work, then compare at a distance. Ask about proportion, then value families, then a few edges and accents. Write one or two goals, not a dozen.

Key terms: critique, plan (for the next work).

Watch me

Demonstration of a critique order:

  1. Stand at the viewing point (or look at the photograph) and check height against width, then a plumb alignment.
  2. Squint: does the shadow still read as one family, lighter or darker than the cast?
  3. Check that no halftone has fallen into the shadow, and that a few dark accents are reserved for real contacts and recesses.
  4. Write one proportion goal and one value goal for the next period of work. Stop there.

Hint 1: Priority

The height (a large proportion) comes before a pleasing finish.

Hint 2: Goal

A sentence such as: 'Next time, establish height and width before any modeling.'

Modern language · Spanish: review and composition

25 min

Learn

Key terms

composition (Spanish)
Definition
A short piece of connected Spanish, written from words and patterns already taught, not from a translator.
Example
A short paragraph that uses a greeting, an article and a noun, ser or estar correctly, one regular present, and one question.
Not this
Composition is not a list of isolated words, and it is not English with Spanish nouns dropped in.
Connects to
This is a two-week check of Days 1–9: vowels, gender, ser and estar, -ar/-er/-ir, tener, ir, and questions.
contrast of ser and estar
Definition
The choice, in every ‘to be’ sentence, between what something is (ser) and where or how it is for now (estar).
Example
La ciudad es antigua, but el libro está en la mesa, and nosotros estamos cansados.
Not this
There is no single English ‘is’ that covers both, and guessing at random is not a contrast.
Connects to
Day 3 and Day 4 taught the two verbs separately. The review asks you to choose, and to say why.

This is a two-week check of the Spanish taught so far: sounds; articles and gender; ser and estar; present of regular -ar, -er, -ir verbs; tener and ir; numbers 0–30; and question words. Study the forms once more, then do the check without turning it into a guess.

Elective · Poetry: meter and the iambic pentameter

30 min · Seminar rotation: Elective

Learn

Key terms

meter (verse)
Definition
The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of verse. (US spelling: meter.)
Origin
From Middle English meter, metre, from Latin metrum, from Ancient Greek μέτρον (‘measure’).
Example
Iambic pentameter is five iambs, a common English line: da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM.
Not this
Meter in this lesson is not the SI unit of length, which is a different English word of the same spelling, also from μέτρον.
Connects to
A foot is the unit of the pattern. Scanning a line marks the feet and shows where the rhythm varies on purpose.
iambic pentameter
Definition
A line of five iambs (five da-DUM feet), the most common meter of English verse from Chaucer onward, and the line of Shakespeare’s sonnets and blank verse.
Origin
Iambic is from Latin, from Ancient Greek ἴαμβος (the iambic foot). Pentameter is from Greek πέντε (‘five’) + μέτρον (‘measure’).
Example
‘Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?’ is scanned as five feet, with the first foot sometimes read with stress on I or on Shall.
Not this
It is not ‘any ten-syllable line’, and a spondee (DUM-DUM) such as ‘Rough winds’ is a variation, not a mistake.
Connects to
Meter is the general word; iambic pentameter is the specific pattern you write and scan today.
foot (verse)
Definition
The repeating unit of meter: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), spondee (DUM-DUM).
Origin
A use of the ordinary English word foot (from Old English fōt) as a unit of a line, on the model of the Latin and Greek metrical ‘foot’.
Example
In ‘Shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer’s DAY’ each bar is a foot.
Not this
A foot is not a syllable, and it is not a line. A pentameter line has five feet.
Connects to
To scan a line is to mark its feet and to notice a variation such as the spondee in ‘Rough winds’.

Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line. The unit is the foot: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), spondee (DUM-DUM). Iambic pentameter is five iambs per line, the most common meter of English verse from Chaucer onward, and the line of Shakespeare's sonnets and blank verse.

Model (Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, lines 1–4, public domain):

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate: Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, And summer's lease hath all too short a date:

Remember

Meter is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. A foot is the unit: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest, dactyl, spondee. Iambic pentameter is five iambs, the common English line (Shakespeare's sonnets and blank verse). Variation (a spondee, a shifted first foot) is often deliberate. US spelling: meter, not metre, for verse in this course. (The SI unit is a different word of the same spelling.)

Key terms: meter (verse), iambic pentameter, foot (verse).

Watch me

Scanning line 3 of Sonnet 18, as a model: 'Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May'.

  1. A strict iambic line would be × / | × / | × / | × / | × /. 'Rough winds' is heavy-heavy: a spondee (/ /), then do SHAKE | the DAR | ling BUDS | of MAY.
  2. The variation is the point: the winds feel forceful because the first foot is heavier than an iamb.
  3. When you write four lines of your own, aim at ten syllables in a mostly da-DUM pattern, rhyming ABAB, and allow a deliberate variation if you can name it.

Watch me

A model for a returning week-1 term, now used in a week-2 setting.

  1. Question: What is a warrant, and how is it different from evidence?
  2. Evidence is the quotation or fact you offer. A warrant is the reason that fact supports the claim — the rule that joins them.
  3. A near-miss is to pile up quotations and call them an argument. Without a warrant, the reader is asked to guess the link. This is the same habit as sourcing a document in history: say what it is, then say what it can support.
