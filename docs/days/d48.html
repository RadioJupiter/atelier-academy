Latin · Review of Capitula I–X: quis, quid, and ubi

question words (quis, quid, ubi)
Definition
Three question words already used in Capitula I–X: quis (who), quid (what), and ubi (where). They ask; they do not invent a new verb tense.
Example

Mathematics · Algebra XVI: a line and a parabola, as a system

60 min · Method: substitution, as with a line and a circle, now used on a parabola. Invented equations stay labeled.

Learn

Key terms

a line and a parabola, as a system
Definition
Two equations together, one the already-taught parabola y = ax² + bx + c (often y = x² in a first look) and one a line, solved by substitution. The resulting quadratic may give two meetings, one meeting, or none.
Example
y = x² and y = x + 2, invented classroom graphs, meet where x² = x + 2, so x² − x − 2 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0. After checking y in both equations, the points are (2, 4) and (−1, 1).
Not this
Not a line-and-circle system (that was Day 44), and not a claim that every line meets a parabola twice. The algebra decides.
Connects to
Quadratic graphs (week 7), quadratic equations, and Day 44’s substitution method, now used on a parabola instead of a circle.

Day 44 solved a line and a circle as a system, by substitution. Today the second curve is a parabola you already know how to graph, often y = x² in a first look. The method is the same: substitute, then solve the quadratic. Invented classroom equations stay labeled as invented.

A worked system. y = x² and y = x + 2.

  • Substitute: x² = x + 2.
  • Rearrange: x² − x − 2 = 0.
  • Factor: (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 2 or x = −1.
  • The points are (2, 2 + 2) = (2, 6) and (−1, −1 + 2) = (−1, 1).
  • Check in y = x²: 2² = 4, but the line said 6 — wait: 2² = 4, and x + 2 = 4, not 6.

That check fails, so we stop and redo the y-value from the line and the parabola with care: at x = 2, y = x² = 4, and y = x + 2 = 4. The point is (2, 4), not (2, 6). At x = −1, y = 1, and y = −1 + 2 = 1. The points are (2, 4) and (−1, 1).

The diagram uses those checked points. A first arithmetic slip is a reason to check, not a reason to keep a wrong pair.

A quadratic may also have a repeated root (the line just touches) or no real root (the line misses). Today’s worked case meets twice.

Remember

Substitute a line into a parabola (often y = x²). A quadratic may give two meetings, one, or none. Check each y in both equations. Invented figures stay labeled.

Key terms: a line and a parabola, as a system.

Key terms

Franklin's method, with hints
Definition
A reconstruction, from brief notes, of a short set passage already attributed, followed by a comparison with the original words. The notes are hints, not a license to invent later scenes.
Example
Hints from the set opening: attentive hearers; a man begins from a couch; a queen's command; telling renews a sad remembrance of a fate. The reconstruction must be checked against Dryden's four lines, not against a later book.
Not this
Not a new translation of Virgil, and not a summary of a later Book II scene before that extract is set.
Connects to

Physics · g on the Moon, from the same formula and sourced figures

50 min · Method: g = G M / R² with sourced Moon figures, compared with a table value and a qualitative NASA Science comparison. No invented speed.

Learn

Key terms

g on the Moon, a first sourced look
Definition
The same formula g = G M / R², now used with sourced Moon figures, compared with a NASA table value and with a NASA Science qualitative comparison.
Example
Using the NASA Planetary Fact Sheet Moon mass 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg (two significant figures) and diameter 3,475 km, the computation gives about 1.6 m/s², matching the same sheet's gravity 1.6 m/s² for the Moon. NASA Science also says the gravity on the Moon's surface is one-sixth of Earth's.
Not this
Not a new value of G, and not a claim of extra digits the mass cannot support. 0.073 has two significant figures, so the result is about 1.6 m/s², not 1.613.
Connects to
Monday's g = G M / R², and the same NASA fact-sheet family already used for Earth.

Monday's formula does not belong only to the Earth. The same g = G M / R² can be used at another body's surface, if M and R are sourced, and if the significant figures are respected.

Sourced Moon figures, from the same NASA Planetary Fact Sheet that lists Earth mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg and Earth diameter 12,756 km: the Moon's mass is 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg, and its diameter is 3,475 km. The same table lists gravity 9.8 m/s² for Earth and 1.6 m/s² for the Moon. A NASA Science page, "Moon Facts," also says that the gravity on the surface of the Moon is one-sixth of Earth's.

The arithmetic, with G still 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²:

  • M = 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg = 7.3 × 10²² kg (two significant figures).
  • R = 3,475 / 2 = 1,737.5 km = 1.7375 × 10⁶ m.
  • G M = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(7.3 × 10²²) = 4.8691 × 10¹².
  • R² = 3.0189 × 10¹².
  • g ≈ 1.61 m/s² before rounding, which must be written about 1.6 m/s², because the mass has two significant figures.

That computed 1.6 m/s² matches the table's 1.6 m/s². Compared with this course's working 9.8 m/s², 9.8 / 1.6 = 6.125, about six times, which agrees at that rounding with NASA Science's "one-sixth."

We do not invent a third digit for the Moon's mass in order to write 1.62, and we do not invent an orbital speed.

Remember

The same g = G M / R², with NASA Moon mass 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg and diameter 3,475 km, gives about 1.6 m/s² (two significant figures). The table lists 1.6; NASA Science says one-sixth of Earth's. No invented speed.

History · The Arch of Titus: a later commemoration, not a photograph of 70 CE

40 min · Method: Platner and Ashby (1929) on date, reliefs, and a nineteenth-century rebuild. Not an invented portrait.

Learn

Key terms

the Arch of Titus, as a later monument
Definition
A single-bay arch on the Sacra Via in Rome, erected in honour of Titus and in commemoration of the siege of Jerusalem, but, in Platner and Ashby's account, not finished and dedicated until after Titus's death (CIL VI.945). It is a later commemoration, not a photograph of 70 CE.
Example
Platner and Ashby (1929) describe the south inner relief as the spoils from the temple at Jerusalem (the table of shewbread, the seven-branched candlestick, and the silver trumpets) carried in a triumph, and the north inner relief as Titus in a quadriga.
Not this
Not a building completed in 70 CE, and not the original unaltered fabric: the same entry says the arch was taken down in 1822 and rebuilt by Valadier.
Connects to
Monday's 70 CE and Josephus caution. Wednesday uses a 1929 public-domain topographical entry, not an invented portrait of Titus.
spoils, in a triumphal relief
Definition
Goods carried in a triumph, as later carved. On the Arch of Titus, in Platner and Ashby's description, the south inner relief shows spoils from the temple at Jerusalem: the table of shewbread, the seven-branched candlestick, and the silver trumpets.
Origin
The noun spoils is from the verb spoil, from Middle English spoilen, from Old French espoillier, from Latin spoliare (English Wiktionary, entry “spoil”).
Example
The candlestick and trumpets in that description are part of a later Roman monument's imagery, not a second Josephus paragraph, and not a photograph from 70 CE.
Not this
Not a claim that we can date the carving to the month of the event, and not a classroom invention of other objects “surely also” on the relief.
Connects to
Monday's narrative, and today's Platner and Ashby entry.

Monday reserved this monument. Today uses a public-domain topographical entry, not an invented portrait and not a measured survey. The diagram above is a classroom outline of an arch and an attic, labeled as such.

Platner and Ashby, 1929. Samuel Ball Platner, as completed and revised by Thomas Ashby, A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome (London: Oxford University Press, 1929), entry Arcus Titi, as presented on LacusCurtius (Bill Thayer). Public domain. The entry “Arcus Titi” says the arch was “erected in honour of Titus and in commemoration of the siege of Jerusalem in summa Sacra via, but not finished and dedicated until after his death (CIL VI.945).” So even as a commemoration of the siege of Jerusalem, it is later than Titus's death, not a building finished in 70 CE.

The reliefs, in the same entry: on the south, “the spoils from the temple at Jerusalem, the table of shewbread, the seven-branched candlestick, and the silver trumpets, which are being carried in triumph into the city”; on the north, “Titus standing in a quadriga.” We use those named objects and that description. We do not invent further objects, and we do not treat the carving as a photograph of 70 CE.

A later restoration. The same entry says the injury to the structure was so great that it was taken down in 1822 and rebuilt by Valadier, who restored a large part of the attic and the outer half of both piers in travertine, and that the frieze and inscription are preserved only on the side towards the Colosseum. A modern view of the arch is not a simple view of the Flavian fabric.

Josephus remains Monday's narrative source; this arch is a later commemorative monument.

The Arch of Titus commemorates the siege of Jerusalem but, in Platner and Ashby, was finished and dedicated after Titus's death. The south relief shows named spoils; the north shows Titus in a quadriga. 1822 Valadier rebuild: a modern view is not simply the Flavian fabric. Not a photograph of 70 CE.

Atelier · The cube's dark plane, and a cast shadow

90 min · Method: one lamp; dark plane on the form; cast shadow on the table; still not a head.

Learn

Key terms

cast shadow of a cube
Definition
The shadow the cube throws onto the table or the wall, explained by the same single lamp. It is not a stain invented on the dark plane, and it is not the dark plane itself.
Example
If the lamp is high and to the left, the cast shadow falls on the table to the right of the cube, beginning from the cube's contact with the table.
Not this
Not the dark plane (that is on the form), and not a second shadow from a second, invented lamp.
Connects to
Last week's cast shadow on the sphere, now used with a cube whose edges can give the shadow a clearer start.

The dark plane is on the cube, where a face turns away from the lamp. The cast shadow is on the table or the wall, thrown by the cube. That distinction was already taught on the sphere; today it is used on a form with edges.

The same single lamp must explain both. The cast shadow begins where the cube meets the table, on the side away from the lamp. It should not float, and it should not be copied onto the dark plane as a smear.

Do not invent a second lamp to "fill in" the shadow, and do not make the shadow a careful portrait of something the lamp cannot cause.

Remember

The dark plane is on the cube. The cast shadow is on the table or the wall, explained by the same lamp, beginning at the contact with the table. Not a second lamp, and not a smear on the dark plane.

Modern language · Spanish: desde and hasta

40 min · Method: present tense; two prepositions of start and end. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

desde and hasta
Definition
Two prepositions already useful in the present: *desde* marks a starting point (“from,” “since”); *hasta* marks an end point (“until,” “as far as”). They often work as a pair, but each can be used alone.
Example
Estudio desde aquí. Camino hasta allí. Estudio desde las dos hasta las cuatro. (*Aquí* and *allí* are already taught; the clock times use the telling-the-time pattern already taught.)
Not this
Not a past tense, and not a swap (*hasta aquí* as if it meant “from here”). *Desde* is the start; *hasta* is the end.
Connects to
Week 9's *aquí, ahí, allí*, and the already-taught time-of-day pattern, now used with two prepositions. Instruction stays in English.
Word How to say it Meaning
desde DES-deh from, since (a starting point)
hasta AHS-tah until, as far as (an end point)
desde aquí DES-deh ah-KEE from here
hasta allí AHS-tah ah-YEE as far as there

Instruction stays in English. Today is two prepositions: desde (from, since) and hasta (until, as far as).

Model sentences: Camino desde aquí. Camino hasta allí. Camino desde aquí hasta allí. Estudio desde las dos hasta las cuatro.

Aquí and allí are already taught. Clock times, if you use them, must use the telling-the-time pattern already taught; this lesson does not invent a new clock system. No year-dates in the Spanish.

Remember

Desde = from / since (a start). Hasta = until / as far as (an end). Camino desde aquí hasta allí. Not a past tense, and not a swap of the two.

Art history · The Arch of Titus: a later relief, not a photograph

50 min · Method: the same Platner and Ashby entry as today's history lesson, used for an art-historical caution. No invented portrait.

Learn

Key terms

a commemorative relief, later than the event
Definition
A carved scene on a later monument that commemorates an event, without being a photograph of that event. On the Arch of Titus, in Platner and Ashby's 1929 account, the south inner relief shows spoils from the temple at Jerusalem, and the monument was finished and dedicated after Titus's death.
Origin
Commemorate: borrowed from Latin commemoratus, from commemorare (English Wiktionary). Today's term uses that verb as a later public remembering, in stone.
Example
The seven-branched candlestick on that south relief, as Platner and Ashby name it, is part of a later commemorative carving, not a studio portrait of 70 CE, and not a measured copy of Josephus's narrative.
Not this
Not a claim that the carving is contemporary with the event, and not an invented likeness of a face the entry does not give us as a portrait source.
Connects to
Today's history lesson on the Arch of Titus, and week 9's refusal of an invented Flavian portrait.

Today's history lesson, using Platner and Ashby (1929), places the Arch of Titus as a later commemoration, finished and dedicated after Titus's death, with a south relief of named spoils and a north relief of Titus in a quadriga, and with a large 1822 restoration by Valadier.

This seminar's job is the art-historical caution: a commemorative relief is a later public image, not a photograph, and not a license to invent a portrait. We may name the objects Platner and Ashby name (the table of shewbread, the seven-branched candlestick, the silver trumpets). We may not invent a facial likeness, and we may not treat a modern photograph of the arch as if it were simply the Flavian fabric.

The classroom arch diagram in today's history lesson is labeled as a diagram, not a survey. This seminar does not add a second, invented drawing of the spoils.

Remember

A commemorative relief is later than the event it remembers. The Arch of Titus, in Platner and Ashby, was dedicated after Titus's death; the south relief shows named spoils. Not a photograph of 70 CE, and not an invented face. The 1822 rebuild also means a modern view is not simply the Flavian fabric.
