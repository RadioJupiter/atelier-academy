Latin · Capitulum IV, part 1: calling and commanding

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. IV "Dominus et servī"

Learn

The **vocative** is the form used for calling or addressing someone (*Salvē, puella!*). In many words it looks like the nominative; a familiar exception in the early books is masculine names and nouns in *-us*, which often take *-e* (*Mārce*, *serve*). Commands (the imperative) are used when you tell one person to do something.
WordHow to say itMeaning
salvēSAL-wayhello (to one)
salvētesal-WAY-tehhello (to more than one)
valēWAH-laygoodbye (to one)
MārceMAR-kehMarcus! (vocative of Mārcus)
videWID-ehsee! / look!

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Learn

**Read aloud twice** the first third of Capitulum IV. **Notice: the vocative**, the form used to address someone. For nouns in *-us* it ends in **-e**: *serve!, domine!, Marce!* Nouns in *-ius* end in *-ī*: *Iūlī!* Other nouns use the nominative form: *ancilla!, puer!* **Notice: the imperative**, a command to one person: *vocā!* (call!), *tacē!* (be quiet!), *audī!* (listen!), *pōne!* (put!). It is the verb's stem: *voca-t → vocā!*, *tace-t → tacē!*, *audi-t → audī!*; verbs in *-it* with a short *i* take *-e*: *pōnit → pōne!*

Remember

When you speak *to* someone, use the vocative, not just the nominative. *Salvē, puella! Salvē, Mārce!*

Watch me

Salvē, Mārce! Hello, Marcus! The name is Mārcus when he is the subject, but Mārce when you call him. Vide oppidum! Look at the town!

Together

How do you say hello to one girl, and hello to several people?

Hint 1: One *Salvē, puella!*
Hint 2: Several *Salvēte!*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is the vocative of *Mārcus*? 2. What does *valē* mean?

Exercises

  1. Give the vocative: servus, dominus, ancilla, puer, Iūlius.
  2. Give the imperative: vocat, videt, audit, venit, respondet.
  3. Write four short commands addressed to a named person, e.g. Serve, venī!

Mathematics · Functions and their graphs

60 min · Method: Gelfand, Glagoleva & Shnol, Functions and Graphs (graphs built by transformation, not by plotting tables)

Learn

A **function** assigns to each allowed input exactly one output. We write *y* = *f*(*x*), or just *y* in terms of *x*. The **graph** is the set of points (*x*, *f*(*x*)) in the plane. A vertical line should meet the graph at most once (the vertical-line test). Linear functions have graphs that are straight lines; *y* = *mx* + *c* has slope *m* and intercept *c*.

Learn

**Function.** A rule assigning to each allowed input x (the **domain**) exactly one output f(x). The set of outputs is the **range**. **Transformations of y = f(x).** - y = f(x) + c: shift up by c. - y = f(x − c): shift right by c. - y = a·f(x): stretch vertically by a (and reflect if a < 0). - y = f(−x): reflect in the y-axis. - y = |f(x)|: reflect the parts below the x-axis upward. **Parabolas by completing the square.** y = x² − 4x + 1 = (x − 2)² − 3: the graph of y = x², shifted 2 right and 3 down; vertex (2, −3).

Remember

One input, one output. Graph = points (*x*, *f*(*x*)). Vertical-line test. For *y* = *mx* + *c*, *m* is the slope (rise/run) and *c* is where the line meets the *y*-axis.

Watch me

Let *f*(*x*) = 2*x* + 1. Then *f*(3) = 7, so the point (3, 7) is on the graph. The slope is 2: from any point, run 1 and rise 2. The line meets the *y*-axis at (0, 1).

Together

For *g*(*x*) = −*x* + 4, find *g*(0) and *g*(4), and say whether the line is rising or falling as *x* increases.

Hint 1: Values *g*(0) = 4, *g*(4) = 0.
Hint 2: Slope The slope is −1, so the line is falling.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is *f*(3) if *f*(*x*) = 2*x* + 1? 2. Can a function send one input to two different outputs?

Exercises

  1. Sketch on one set of axes, without plotting points: y = |x|, y = |x − 2|, y = |x| − 2, y = |x − 2| − 1.
  2. Find the vertex of y = 2x² + 8x + 5 and sketch it.
  3. Domain of: (a) y = √(x − 3) (b) y = 1/(x² − 4) (c) y = √(4 − x²).
  4. f(x) = 2x + 3 and g(x) = x². Find f(g(2)) and g(f(2)). Are they equal in general?
  5. Sketch y = |x² − 4|.
  6. How many solutions has |x − 1| + |x + 1| = 2? And |x − 1| + |x + 1| = 3? (Think of distance on the number line.)
  7. (Stretch.) For which values of a does x² − 4|x| + 3 = a have exactly four solutions?

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 3: verse into prose

45 min · Franklin's second exercise: "I took some of the tales and turned them into verse; and, after a time, when I had pretty well forgotten the prose, turned them back again."

Learn

Franklin's method, third pass: after hints and order, **compare** your rewrite with the original and make a short list of what you missed — a word choice, a clause, a turn of thought. Then write once more, aiming at those misses only. This is not copying with extra steps; it is training your ear on a real passage.

Learn

**Why it works.** Verse forces you to search for words of the same meaning but different length and sound; prose forces you to make the logic explicit. Moving between them builds what Franklin called "a stock of words". **Model** (Shakespeare, Sonnet 73, first quatrain, public domain): > That time of year thou mayst in me behold > When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang > Upon those boughs which shake against the cold, > Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.

Remember

Compare, list the misses, rewrite once more. Do not inflate the list with taste ('I just prefer this word') unless the original's word does a job yours does not.

Watch me

Original: Because grain was scarce, the council opened the stores. Your first rewrite: The council opened the stores. Miss: the cause is gone. Second rewrite: The council opened the stores, because grain was scarce. The thought is now complete, even if the word order differs.

Together

Your rewrite dropped a contrast that the original marked with *but*. What do you put on the miss-list?

Hint 1: Miss The contrast (*but* …).
Hint 2: Next draft Put the contrast back, in your own accurate words.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What two things do you do after the first rewrite? 2. Is a different word order automatically a miss?

Exercises

  1. Copy the quatrain exactly. Mark the stressed syllables in the first line.
  2. Turn it into plain, exact prose of one or two sentences, leaving out nothing the poem says.
  3. Identify the metaphor in line 4: what are the "choirs", and why are they "ruin'd"?
  4. Turn this prose into four lines of rhymed verse (any metre, rhyme ABAB): In the morning, before work, I draw for an hour; the light is clear, the house is quiet, and the hand is steady.

Science · Light: polarisation and the quantitative model

45 min · Phenomenon extended; olympiad problems

Another clue in the sky. Light scattered by air molecules is partly polarised: its vibrations are concentrated in one direction. The effect is strongest in the part of the sky about 90° away from the Sun. With polarising sunglasses (or the polarising filter of a camera), look at the sky 90° from the Sun and rotate the lens: the sky darkens and brightens. Some insects, such as bees, use this pattern of polarisation to navigate. Never look at the Sun itself.

Useful relations. Speed of light in vacuum c = 299,792,458 m/s (exact, by definition of the metre). For any wave, c = fλ.

Problems

  1. Find the frequency of 500 nm green light.
  2. The mean Earth–Sun distance is about 1.496 × 10¹¹ m. How long does sunlight take to reach us?
  3. Do the polarisation observation. Where in the sky did the darkening occur most strongly? Draw a sketch with the Sun's direction.
  4. Light of 400 nm and 700 nm: by what factor do their scattering intensities differ?
  5. (Olympiad-style estimation.) Seen from space, the Sun looks white, not yellow. Explain in two sentences.

Literature · Writing about the Iliad: the analytical essay

45 min · Write an essay of 400–600 words using the Writing & Rhetoric methods

Prompt (choose one). (a) Is Hector or Achilles the true hero of Books 1–6? (b) How do the gods' actions shape human responsibility in Books 1–6? Are the human characters free? (c) What does Book 6 show about the cost of war that the battle scenes do not?

Process. (1) Write a thesis (one sentence that could be argued against). (2) Make a single-paragraph outline for each of three body paragraphs. (3) Gather two quotations per paragraph, with book and line numbers. (4) Draft. (5) Revise with this checklist: every paragraph begins with a claim; every quotation is introduced and explained; at least one sentence uses although to acknowledge the other side.

Atelier · Cast drawing 3: halftones

90 min

Learn

**Halftones** are the values in the light family as the form turns toward the terminator — still light, but no longer the brightest plane. They must stay lighter than the shadow family, or the form flattens. Lay them after the two families are in, and keep them simple: a few planes, not a polish.

Learn

**Model the light side only.** The shadow family stays as one flat tone (darken it slightly near the terminator if the cast shows it). Find the halftones on the light side. Work from the terminator outward toward the light; each step should be one value step on your scale. Leave the brightest planes untouched paper. **Edges.** Look along the terminator: where the form turns sharply (a nose's side plane), the edge is sharp; where it turns slowly (a cheek), it is soft. Edges carry as much information about form as values do.

Remember

Halftones belong to the light family. They stay lighter than the terminator's shadow side. Add them late, in a few planes, and check by squinting.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Confirm that the shadow family and the terminator still match the cast.
  2. On the light side, find one or two planes that are darker than the highlight but clearly not shadow. Match each to a step on your scale (for example, steps 2 or 3, not step 6).
  3. Lay those planes lightly, then stand back and squint: the light should still read as one family, turning slowly.
  4. If a halftone has slipped into the shadow's darkness, lift it.

Together

A 'halftone' you have just laid looks as dark as the shadow family. What has gone wrong?

Hint 1: Family It has crossed the terminator and is no longer a halftone.
Hint 2: Fix Lighten it until it belongs to the light family again.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Which family do halftones belong to? 2. Why must a halftone stay lighter than the shadow?

Exercises

  1. (70 min) Model the halftones. Before each area, name its value number aloud.
  2. (20 min) Compare the drawing and the cast at the same time from the marked spot, squinting. List any area whose value is off by more than one step.

Modern language · Spanish: ir and the near future

25 min

Learn

*Ir* (to go) is irregular: *voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van*. *Ir a* + infinitive talks about the near future: *voy a dibujar* — I am going to draw.
WordHow to say itMeaning

vasvahsyou go

vamosBAH-mohswe go

ir aeer ahto be going to
sábadoSAH-bah-dohSaturday

Learn

**ir** (to go): voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van. *Voy al museo* (a + el = al). **Near future:** *ir a* + infinitive: *Voy a dibujar* (I am going to draw). **Days of the week** (not capitalised): lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo. *El lunes* = on Monday; *los lunes* = on Mondays.

Remember

*Voy al museo. Vamos a estudiar.* *A* + *el* becomes *al*. Use *ir a* + infinitive for a plan.

Watch me

¿Vas a dibujar el sábado? Are you going to draw on Saturday? Answer: Sí, voy a dibujar. or No, voy a estudiar.

Together

Say in Spanish: 'They are going to live in the city.'

Hint 1: Forms *van a* + *vivir*, and *en la ciudad*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Van a vivir en la ciudad.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *voy* mean? 2. How do you say 'we are going to study'?

Exercises

  1. Translate: (a) We are going to the library. (b) On Fridays I go to the museum. (c) What are you (tú) going to study tomorrow (mañana)? (d) They are going to paint on Saturday.
  2. Write five sentences about what you are going to do next week, one for each school day.

Economics & personal finance · Compound interest

30 min · Seminar rotation: Economics

Simple interest pays only on the original principal; compound interest pays on the interest already earned. With principal P, annual rate r and n years, compounded yearly: A = P(1 + r)ⁿ.

Rule of 72. Money at r% a year doubles in roughly 72/r years (a good approximation for rates between about 2% and 15%).

APR and APY. A credit card with 24% APR charges about 2% a month; compounded monthly, that is (1.02)¹² − 1 ≈ 26.8% a year. The same mathematics that makes savings grow makes debt grow.

Exercises

  1. $1,000 is invested at 7% a year, compounded yearly. How much after 10 years? Compare with simple interest.
  2. Use the rule of 72: about how long does money take to double at 6%? at 9%?
  3. A loan has 12% APR compounded monthly. What is the effective annual rate?
  4. Two people save $100 a month until the same date; one starts ten years earlier. Explain in two sentences, using the formula, why the early starter ends with far more than just the extra deposits.
