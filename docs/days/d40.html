Learn

Key terms

Capitulum X check
Definition
A Friday check of this week’s Latin: third-declension nouns homō, leō, and pēs (stem from the genitive; accusative in -em); posse + a present infinitive, active or passive (vidēre / vidērī, vocāre / vocārī, habēre / habērī; also esse and īre in the active). Original sentences only; not a copied textbook paragraph.
Example
Homō leōnem vidēre potest. Rēx vocārī potest. Pēs canis parvus est.
Not this
Not a new chapter, and not a finite passive paradigm. Do not mix an accusative object with vidērī as if it were vidēre.

Key terms

précis of a draft
Definition
A compact restatement, in your own words, of what your three-paragraph essay did — its two claims, its grant or contrast, and its close — not a new essay and not a string of quotations.
Example
A fair précis: “This essay stated Antigone’s claim of burial and Creon’s claim of a public command, granted the civic need for a command, and closed on the cost of stating both before a verdict.” (Your own close may differ, if that is what you actually wrote.)
Not this
Not a retelling of the play, and not a second hidden quotation of Jebb’s last chorus. If you comment on the end of the play, attribute any short line and keep the comment shorter than a new essay.

Physics · Friday mix: gravitation, and a first look at orbits

50 min · Friday mix of F = G Mm / r², mass and weight, and a qualitative picture of an orbit · no invented speed

Learn

Key terms

orbit (first look)
Definition
A path in which a body keeps falling toward a larger body and yet does not arrive, because it also has motion to the side. In Newton’s thought-picture (a throw from a high mountain, often illustrated from his *System of the World*), a faster sideways throw curves with the Earth instead of hitting the ground nearby. This lesson gives no invented orbital speed, and it does not treat every orbit as a circle.
Example
The Moon is in orbit around the Earth: it is falling toward the Earth and moving aside, in a closed path. The path need not be a perfect circle.
Not this
Not a claim that a circular orbit is the only possible path, and not a measured speed from this classroom. A picture of a throw is a thought-picture, not a classroom experiment from a real mountain.
Connects to
Monday’s inverse-square force is the pull; today’s job is to name the path without inventing a number for how fast the Moon, or a spacecraft, moves.

A mix, and one new picture. You may use F = G Mm / r², the inverse-square pattern, and W = mg.

Newton’s thought-picture. From a high mountain, a sideways throw falls to the ground. A faster throw goes farther. In the thought-picture, a throw that is fast enough curves with the Earth and does not land: an orbit. The picture is a help, not a measured experiment, and this lesson will not invent the speed.

A caution. Orbits are not all circles. A circle is a special case. Do not draw every orbit as a perfect ring and call that a fact of nature.

Remember

Retrieve F = G Mm / r², inverse square, and W = mg. An orbit is a sideways-falling path in Newton’s thought-picture; not every orbit is a circle; no invented speed.

Key terms: orbit (first look).

Atelier · A two-week critique, and a plan for the next sitting

50 min · Method: a structured critique of the two weeks’ head, then a short written plan — not a score, and not a new head in the last ten minutes

Learn

Key terms

two-week critique and plan
Definition
A short, honest review of the study against the plate or plaster after two weeks of work on the same head: what is right, what still fails, and what the first move of a later sitting will be.
Example
A useful plan names one placement or interval, one value or edge, and one thing you will not rush (strands, a new head, a diagram-ear).
Not this
Not a general ‘do better,’ not a score, and not a new drawing begun in the last ten minutes. Last Friday’s critique was of one week; today looks at the two weeks (ear, hair, neck, intervals, edges, accents).
Connects to
If time remains, make only the first move of the new plan, not a second new start.

Set the study beside the plate. Write three notes: (1) a placement or a mass that is about right; (2) a value, an edge, or an interval that still fails; (3) the first move of the next sitting. If time remains, make only that first move.

A critique without a plan is a complaint. A plan without the plate is a wish. Keep the study.

Remember

Critique the two weeks’ study against the plate: one success, one failure, one first move. No score. At most the first move if time remains. Keep the study.

Elective · Poetry 4: Shelley, “Ozymandias” (1818), a short public-domain model

40 min · Method: a public-domain sonnet, short attributed quotation, and a look at what the inscription in the poem claims

Learn

Key terms

Ozymandias (Shelley, 1818)
Definition
A sonnet by Percy Bysshe Shelley, first published in 1818, in the public domain. A traveler reports a broken statue in a desert, and an inscription that still boasts. This lesson uses a short attributed extract, not the whole poem copied onto the page.
Example
The inscription, in Shelley’s wording, includes the line “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!” The boast and the broken statue sit in the same poem; the lesson’s job is to notice that contrast, not to preach a ready-made moral as if it were the only possible comment.
Not this
Not a Roman inscription from the Pantheon, and not a historical source for a named pharaoh in this course. The poem is a made thing, dated 1818, attributed to Shelley.
Connects to
Week 6 used Blake, “The Tyger” (1794). Today a different public-domain poem. Meter and imagery have already been taught; today the job is the inscription’s claim against what the traveler sees.

Percy Bysshe Shelley’s sonnet “Ozymandias” was published in 1818 and is in the public domain. We use a short attributed extract, not the whole poem on this page. You should read the poem from a public-domain text in class.

Two short quotations, attributed:

  • The opening: “I met a traveller from an antique land…”
  • The inscription the traveler reports: “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”

(Shelley, “Ozymandias,” 1818.)

The job. The inscription boasts. The traveler sees a wreck in a desert. Hold those two facts together. You may connect this, carefully, to this week’s habit about public claims (an inscription, a first-person record, a monument) — but the poem is not a source for the Pantheon, and it is not a history of 27 BCE.

Do not treat a single moral sentence (“pride falls”) as if it were the whole of the poem, unless you can show that sentence from the words.

Remember

Shelley, “Ozymandias,” 1818, public domain. Short attributed extracts only. The inscription boasts; the traveler sees a wreck. The poem is not a source for the Pantheon or for 27 BCE.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is the inverse of a linear function different from the logic inverse of a conditional, and what is f⁻¹(x) for f(x) = mx + b (m ≠ 0)?
  2. The inverse function undoes f: f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m. The logic inverse of “If P, then Q” is “If not P, then not Q.” They share an English word, not a definition.
  3. A check is composition: f(f⁻¹(x)) = x. The notation f⁻¹ is not a power, and is not 1/f(x).
  4. A near-miss is to treat f⁻¹ as a reciprocal, or to give the logic inverse when the lesson asked for a function.
