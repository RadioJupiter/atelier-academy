Friday mix, not a new angle system. The unit circle has radius 1. Cos θ = x, sin θ = y. Axis angles, 30°, 45°, and 60°, and the signs in the four quadrants, are all in play. Radians remain reserved. Exact values are kept; a decimal is not a replacement.

Remember

Cos θ = x, sin θ = y, on the circle of radius 1. At 30°, P is (√3/2, 1/2). Signs follow the quadrant.

Watch me

  1. 0°: (1, 0), sin 0, cos 1. 90°: (0, 1), sin 1, cos 0.
  2. 30°: (√3/2, 1/2), sin 1/2, cos √3/2.
  3. 150°: (−√3/2, 1/2), sin 1/2, cos −√3/2.

Together

Give sin 45°, cos 60°, sin 270°, and cos 330°.

Hint 1: First quadrant

Sin 45° = √2/2; cos 60° = 1/2.

Hint 2: Axis and fourth

Sin 270° = −1; cos 330° = √3/2.

Check

Friday mix, not a new exercise. Write or tighten a short narrative, and then, separately, say how a fable differs from it. Do not mix a moral into the narrative. Last week’s Townsend moral, if quoted, stays on the fable side. Chreia and maxim remain reserved.

Remember

A narrative: a short complete action, in order, with when and where, and no moral. A fable: the same kind of short action, with a stated moral at the end.

Watch me

Take the invented ink-jar event and show it once as a narrative and once as a fable.

  1. Narrative: At noon, a jar of ink stands open on a desk. A draft slides a sheet toward the rim. The sheet is pulled back, and the jar is closed.
  2. Fable: the same action, then a stated moral, for example “A lid is cheaper than a spoiled page.”
  3. The moral belongs only to the fable. It is not added to the narrative.

Together

Write a short original narrative, then, in a separate short paragraph, state how a fable would differ, without turning your narrative into one.

Hint 1: The narrative

Complete action, in order, when and where, no moral.

Hint 2: The contrast

A fable would add a stated moral at the end, and must not be folded back into the narrative.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Write a short narrative, with when and where, in order, and with no moral.
  2. In a separate paragraph, say how a fable differs from that narrative.
  3. What would break either piece, and what remains reserved?

Exercises

  1. Show the invented ink-jar event once as a narrative and once as a fable.

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Friday mix, not a new section. I = P/A, E = IAt, and intensity proportional to amplitude squared. Example 16.9: E = 5.04 × 10^6 J, I′ = 1.40 × 10^5 W/m². Example 16.10: 4.00 W/m², not 2.00. Decibels and Chapter 17 sound remain reserved.

Remember

I = P/A. Energy follows amplitude squared, so a doubled amplitude gives four times the intensity. Official book figures stay official; invented figures stay invented.

Watch me

Hint 1: Energy

Hint 2: Constructive

I′ = 4 × 2.00 = 8.00 W/m².

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Write I = P/A, and say how energy is related to amplitude. Recompute E and I′ in Example 16.9.
  2. In Example 16.10, why is the result 4.00 W/m², not 2.00, and why is energy not created?
  3. For invented 400 W/m², 0.250 m², and 2.00 h, find E, and for invented 2.00 W/m² waves, perfectly constructive, find I′.

Exercises

Seminar · Artistic anatomy: Gray 1918, the atlas, a first look

40 min · Method: Gray’s atlas as ring-like, with no body and no spinous process; axis and C7 reserved

Learn

Key terms

the atlas (first cervical vertebra)
Definition
In Gray, 1918, as posted at Theodora, the first cervical vertebra is named the atlas “because it supports the globe of the head.” Its chief peculiarity is that it has no body, “due to the fact that the body of the atlas has fused with that of the next vertebra.” Its other peculiarities are that it has no spinous process, “is ring-like, and consists of an anterior and a posterior arch and two lateral masses.” Official lengths remain reserved. The axis and the seventh cervical vertebra remain reserved.
Example
Gray: the anterior arch “forms about one-fifth of the ring”; the posterior arch “forms about two-fifths of the circumference of the ring” and “ends behind in the posterior tubercle, which is the rudiment of a spinous process.” “The lateral masses are the most bulky and solid parts of the atlas, in order to support the weight of the head.”
Not this
Not last week’s typical cervical vertebra as if it were the atlas. Not the axis, or the vertebra prominens, which Gray still says must be separately described and which remain reserved. Not an official measured length invented for the ring or for the arches.
Connects to
Last week’s foramen transversarium, Gray’s distinguishing mark of a cervical vertebra. The atlas is the first of the three Gray set apart.

Last week’s anatomy lesson, from Gray, 1918, “The Cervical Vertebrae,” as posted at Theodora, distinguished a cervical vertebra by the foramen transversarium, and reserved the first, second, and seventh, which Gray says “must be separately described.” Today the first: the atlas. Gray: “The first cervical vertebra is named the atlas because it supports the globe of the head.” “Its chief peculiarity is that it has no body, and this is due to the fact that the body of the atlas has fused with that of the next vertebra.” “Its other peculiarities are that it has no spinous process, is ring-like, and consists of an anterior and a posterior arch and two lateral masses.” The anterior arch “forms about one-fifth of the ring.” The posterior arch “forms about two-fifths of the circumference of the ring: it ends behind in the posterior tubercle, which is the rudiment of a spinous process.” “The lateral masses are the most bulky and solid parts of the atlas, in order to support the weight of the head.” Those fractions, “about one-fifth” and “about two-fifths,” are Gray’s wording, not a classroom measurement of a bone. Invent no official length of the ring, and no official diameter of a foramen. The axis and the seventh cervical vertebra remain reserved. This is artistic anatomy from a public-domain text, not a medical diagnosis, and no body is being described from life.

Remember

Gray names the first cervical vertebra the atlas because it supports the globe of the head. It has no body and no spinous process; it is ring-like, with an anterior arch, a posterior arch, and two lateral masses. Official lengths remain reserved.

Watch me

  2. Its chief peculiarity is that it has no body, because, Gray says, the body has fused with that of the next vertebra.
  3. It has no spinous process, is ring-like, and consists of an anterior and a posterior arch and two lateral masses.

Together

Hint 1: The arches

Hint 2: The masses, and a limit

Remember

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: What is the unit circle, and why is a circle of official radius 2, with sine read as the raw y-coordinate, a miss?
  2. The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. On it, cos θ is the x-coordinate of the point at angle θ, and sin θ is the y-coordinate.

Hint 2: The official example
