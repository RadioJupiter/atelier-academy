Latin · Capitulum III, part 2: verbs and pronouns

30 min · Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. III

Learn

Key terms

verb ending
Definition
The last part of a Latin verb, which shows who is acting (I, you, he or she, we, you plural, they) and, later, tense.
Example
A change from a third-person form such as est to a first-person form changes who is being talked about, even if the stem is related.
Not this
The ending is not decoration, and English ‘you’ will not tell you whether a Latin verb is singular or plural.
Connects to
Est and sunt (Day 1) were already two endings of the same verb ‘to be’. Other verbs now follow the same idea.
pronoun
Definition
A short word that stands in for a noun (I, you, he, she, it, we, they), often already marked by the verb ending in Latin.
Origin
From Latin prōnōmen, from prō (‘for, in place of’) + nōmen (‘name, noun’).
Example
A Latin sentence may use only a verb ending and no separate word for ‘he’, because the ending already says it.
Not this
A pronoun is not a noun, and dropping the Latin pronoun is not the same as dropping the ending.
Connects to
The accusative (Day 6) will also have pronoun forms when the object is ‘me’ or ‘you’, not only a noun.

Verbs in the early chapters change ending for person and number, just as Spanish hablo / habla / hablan do. Est is 'is' (he, she, it); sunt is 'are'. Videt is 'sees' (he, she, it); a plural subject will take a plural verb (vident). Read the ending before you guess the subject.

Word How to say it Meaning
est est is
sunt soont are
videt WID-et sees (singular)
vident WID-ent see (plural)
habet HAH-bet has (singular)
habent HAH-bent have (plural)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Read aloud twice the middle third of Capitulum III.

Notice: three verb types in the third person singular: -at (vocat, pulsat, cantat), -et (videt, rīdet, respondet), -it (audit, venit, dormit). Pronouns: is, ea (he, she) become eum, eam as objects; questions ask quis? (who?) and quem? (whom?). Cūr? (why?) is answered with quia (because).

Remember

The verb ending must match the subject in number. Puella videt; puellae vident. Do not leave a plural subject with est or videt.

Key terms: verb ending, pronoun.

Mathematics · Algebra III: inequalities and the mean

60 min · Gelfand & Shen, Algebra; the classical inequality between means

Learn

Key terms

inequality
Definition
A statement that one quantity is greater or less than another. Adding the same number, or multiplying by a positive number, preserves the inequality; multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the sign.
Origin
From inequality as in- + equality, from Latin aequālitās, from aequālis (‘equal’).
Example
2x + 3 > 11 becomes x > 4. From −3x ≥ 12, dividing by −3 gives x ≤ −4.
Not this
You must not keep the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative. A quadratic inequality is also not solved by ‘just doing the same to both sides’ without using the roots and the shape of the graph.
Connects to
A linear inequality is like an equation with one extra rule. Quadratic inequalities in this lesson use the graph between or outside the roots.
AM–GM inequality
Definition
For a, b ≥ 0, (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b. It is a tool for maxima and minima.
Example
Among rectangles of area 36, a + b ≥ 12, so the smallest perimeter is 24, when the rectangle is a 6 × 6 square.
Not this
AM–GM does not apply to a negative number, and equality is not ‘almost when a is near b’ — it holds only when a = b.
Connects to
The proof is (√a − √b)² ≥ 0, an inequality that comes from a square never being negative.

An inequality such as 2x + 3 > 11 is solved much like an equation, with one extra rule: if you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, you must reverse the inequality sign. You can also reason on a number line, or with bars when the problem is a comparison of quantities.

Rules. You may add the same number to both sides of an inequality, or multiply both sides by a positive number. Multiplying by a negative number reverses the sign.

Quadratic inequalities are solved from the roots and the shape of the graph: x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) < 0 exactly when x lies between the roots.

The arithmetic mean–geometric mean inequality (two numbers). For a, b ≥ 0: (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b. Proof. (√a − √b)² ≥ 0, since a square is never negative. Expand: a − 2√(ab) + b ≥ 0. ∎

Use it to optimise. Among rectangles of perimeter 40, which has the largest area? Sides a and b with a + b = 20: √(ab) ≤ 10, so ab ≤ 100, with equality when a = b = 10. The square wins.

Remember

You may add or subtract the same number on both sides. Multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the sign. Check with a value from the solution set.

Key terms: inequality, AM–GM inequality.

Watch me

Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 2: reconstruction

45 min · Continue from Day 4

Learn

Key terms

revision
Definition
A second (or third) pass that changes structure, order, or wording after a draft exists, using the original as a standard of comparison.
Origin
From Latin revīsiō, from revidēre (‘to look at again’), from re- + vidēre (‘to see’).
Example
In Franklin’s second stage you compare your rewrite with the original and mend what is weaker, not only what is misspelled.
Not this
Revision is not proofreading alone, and it is not copying the original back in when your version is thin.
Connects to
Franklin’s method, part 2, is revision against a model. Later you will revise your own essay the same way.
diction
Definition
The choice of words: precise, ordinary, or borrowed from the model, each chosen on purpose.
Origin
From Latin dictiō (‘a saying, expression’), from dīcere (‘to say’).
Example
Quiet, still, and silent are not always interchangeable; the right one depends on the sentence you are writing.
Not this
Diction is not handwriting, and it is not ‘using the longest word’.
Connects to
Copia (Day 2) trained many versions; diction is how you choose among them in revision.

Franklin's method, second pass: this time scramble the hints and practice putting them into a clear order before you write. Order is part of the thinking, not a decoration at the end. Cause usually comes before effect, unless you have a reason to hold the cause back.

Science · Phenomenon: why is the sky blue and the sunset red?

45 min · Phenomenon-based investigation

Learn

Key terms

Rayleigh scattering intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴
Definition
The scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength. Short wavelengths are scattered much more strongly than long ones (the 1/λ⁴ law). Named after John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh.
Example
Blue light at 450 nm is scattered about 5.9 times as strongly as red light at 700 nm. That is why a clear sky looks blue.
Not this
It is not the reason clouds are white: cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength and scatter all colors about equally.
Connects to
The same law, applied to a long path through air at sunset, takes blue out of the direct beam and leaves orange and red.
wavelength λ
Definition
The distance from one crest of a wave to the next. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to about 700 nm (red).
Origin
A transparent English compound of wave + length; no classical root is claimed here.
Example
Violet light has a shorter wavelength than red, so in Rayleigh scattering it is scattered more strongly.
Not this
Wavelength is not the same as the speed of light, and it is not ‘how strong the light is’.
Connects to
The 1/λ⁴ law in Rayleigh scattering is a statement about wavelength. Day 9 will also use λ in c = fλ.

The phenomenon. On a clear day the sky is blue overhead; near sunset the Sun turns orange-red. Clouds are white. On the Moon the daytime sky is black.

Model. Sunlight contains all visible wavelengths (about 400 nm violet to 700 nm red). Air molecules are much smaller than these wavelengths, and they scatter light with an intensity proportional to 1/λ⁴ (Rayleigh scattering): short wavelengths are scattered far more than long ones. Light scattered toward you from all over the sky is therefore rich in short wavelengths. At sunset, sunlight crosses far more atmosphere to reach you, so most of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving orange and red. Cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength and scatter all colors about equally, so clouds look white.

Experiment (15 min). Fill a clear glass or jar with water and stir in a few drops of milk. In a dark room, shine a flashlight through the side. Look at the glass from the side, then look through it toward the flashlight. Add milk drop by drop and repeat.

Remember

Sunlight contains all visible wavelengths (about 400–700 nm). Air molecules are much smaller than those wavelengths and Rayleigh-scatter with intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴, so short wavelengths are scattered much more. The sky looks blue; at sunset the direct beam is drained of blue and looks orange-red. Cloud droplets are larger and scatter all colors about equally (clouds look white). The Moon's daytime sky is black because it has almost no atmosphere. Never look at the Sun itself.

Key terms: Rayleigh scattering, wavelength.

Watch me

Computing the 1/λ⁴ ratio, as a model.

  1. Blue 450 nm, red 700 nm: the ratio of scattering is (700/450)⁴ = (14/9)⁴ ≈ 1.556⁴ ≈ 5.9. Blue is scattered about 5.9 times as strongly.
  2. That is why, looking sideways at the sky, we see a blue-rich scatter. Looking toward the setting Sun, we see the beam after much of that blue has been removed.
  3. Violet is even shorter, but the sky is not violet: sunlight has less violet, some is absorbed higher up, and our eyes are less sensitive to violet than to blue.

Together

Hint 1: From the side

A faint bluish tint: short wavelengths scattered sideways, like the daytime sky.

Hint 2: Toward the light

The direct beam looks yellow-orange: blue has been removed, like sunset. More milk deepens the effect, like a longer path through air.

Literature · Iliad, Books 4–6

45 min · Read Books 4–6 before this lesson

Learn

Key terms

aristeia
Definition
A hero’s ‘best moment’: a stretch of the poem in which one warrior dominates the battlefield.
Origin
Ancient Greek ἀριστεία (‘excellence, a hero’s prowess’), from ἄριστος (‘best’).
Example
Book 5 is Diomedes’ aristeia: with Athena’s help he dominates the fighting and even wounds Aphrodite and Ares.
Not this
It is not ‘the whole war’, and it is not a guarantee that the hero will survive the poem.
Connects to
The aristeia of Diomedes is placed just before Hector’s return to Troy, so glory and what the war costs a family are set side by side.
aidōs
Definition
Shame before others; a sense of honor that answers to the community’s gaze. Hector names it when he refuses to stay on the wall.
Origin
Ancient Greek αἰδώς (‘shame, respect, honor before others’).
Example
Hector tells Andromache he would feel shame before the Trojans if he shirked battle, even though he foresees Troy’s fall.
Not this
It is not the same as private guilt, and it is not ‘cowardice’. It can drive a person toward danger.
Connects to
Compare Achilles in Book 1, whose honor (timē) is personal prize and recognition. Hector’s aidōs is tied to the city and the family of Book 6.

Book 4. The gods decide to break the truce; Athena prompts the archer Pandarus to wound Menelaus, and the battle resumes. Book 5. Diomedes, inspired by Athena, dominates the battlefield (his aristeia, a hero's "best moment") and even wounds Aphrodite and Ares. Book 6. Hector returns to Troy. He asks his mother to pray to Athena, rebukes Paris, refuses to rest with Helen, and meets his wife Andromache and their infant son at the Scaean Gate. The child is frightened by Hector's helmet with its horsehair crest; Hector laughs, takes it off, and prays that his son may one day be greater than his father.

Remember

Book 4: the truce is broken (Pandarus wounds Menelaus). Book 5: Diomedes' aristeia. Book 6: Hector in Troy, and the meeting with Andromache and the child at the Scaean Gate. Hector's aidōs (shame before the Trojans) outweighs his own safety, even though he foresees the fall of the city. Copywork: the helmet and the child.

Key terms: aristeia, aidōs.

Watch me

Why the poem places Hector and Andromache in the middle of the killing, as a model.

  1. The scene shows what the war destroys: family, household, the next generation. It makes Hector, and Troy, human.
  2. Every later fight is read in its light. The reader knows, as Hector half-knows, that Troy will fall.
  3. The child's fear of the helmet is a small, exact detail that does that work without a speech about 'the cost of war'.

Hint 2: The cost

Honor in the eyes of the community outweighs his own safety, even though he foresees Troy's fall and Andromache's slavery.

Atelier · Cast drawing 1: set-up and block-in

90 min

Learn

Key terms

cast (sculpture)
Definition
A plaster (or similar) copy of a sculpture, used as a still, pale subject for training the eye in shape and value.
Origin
From Old Norse and Old English in the sense ‘to throw’; the studio sense ‘an object formed in a mold’ is later English from the same verb.
Example
A plaster cast of a feature or a bust is set up in steady light for several sessions of sight-size drawing.
Not this
A cast is not a Bargue plate (a plate is a printed drawing). You now draw from a solid form, still using sight-size.
Connects to
Block-in (Day 2) and contour (Day 3) are the first stages again, now from a three-dimensional subject.
set-up
Definition
The fixed arrangement of viewing point, easel, subject, and light, which must not wander from session to session.
Example
Mark the viewing point, the easel feet, and the cast’s position, and keep the same lamp or window.
Not this
A set-up is not ‘wherever you happen to stand today’. If the viewpoint moves, yesterday’s proportions will not match.
Connects to
Sight-size (Day 1) depends on a set-up that stays put. A plaster cast makes that discipline obvious.

A plaster cast is a still, white form. Set it at a height you can see without tilting your head, with one clear light, and a viewing point marked on the floor. The first session is set-up and block-in only: height and width, a plumb line, the big shapes, all in light line, the same sequence as the Bargue plate, now from a three-dimensional object.

Cast drawing is the bridge between copying plates and drawing from life: a white plaster cast holds still, shows form without the distraction of color, and can be lit consistently for days. Traditionally the casts are of classical sculpture (heads, hands, feet, ornament).

What to use. A plaster cast of a simple subject (a plaster ear, nose, hand or ornament, which art suppliers sell) is ideal. If you do not have one, use a white matte object with clear planes and curves: a white ceramic jug, or a white-painted wooden block and sphere together.

Set-up. Single light from above and to one side; dark backdrop behind the cast; your board beside the cast for sight-size; your viewing spot marked on the floor. Light and position must not change for the whole study, so photograph the set-up.

Remember

One light, a marked viewing point, sight-size if you can arrange it. Block height, width, and large shapes. No modeling yet.

Key terms: cast (sculpture), set-up.

Watch me

Demonstration:

  1. Place the cast so its mid-height is near eye level when you stand at the viewing point.
  2. Set one lamp; walk to the viewing point and confirm that the shadow pattern is simple.
  3. On the paper, mark the greatest height and width as you did for the plate, and drop a light plumb line through a useful landmark (a nose, a fold, a corner of the base).
  4. Block the large masses only, then stop. Do not shade on the first set-up day if the proportions are still moving.

Hint 1: Lights

Choose one main light and reduce the other if you can.

Hint 2: Why

Two competing lights make two terminators and confuse the form.

Modern language · Spanish: tener

25 min

Learn

Key terms

tener
Definition
The Spanish verb ‘to have’, irregular in the present: tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen.
Origin
Spanish tener, from Latin tenēre (‘to hold’).
Example
Tengo un lápiz. ¿Tienes un libro?
Not this
Tener is not ser or estar, and tengo is not hablo. ‘I have’ and ‘I am’ are different verbs.
Connects to
Like ser, tener is an important irregular present you memorize as a set, then use in short sentences.
irregular present
Definition
A present-tense pattern that does not simply drop -ar, -er, or -ir and add the regular endings; the stem or the ending (or both) must be learned as they are.
Example
Tengo does not come from a regular *tenero. Soy does not come from a regular *sero.
Not this
Irregular does not mean ‘no pattern at all’: tengo, tienes, tiene share a family, which you learn together.
Connects to
Regular -ar verbs (Day 5) are the contrast. Ser, estar, and tener are the irregular set so far.

Tener (to have) is an important irregular present: tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen. It is also used in tener que + infinitive, 'to have to'.

Word How to say it Meaning
tener teh-NEHR to have
tengo TENG-goh I have
tienes TYEH-nehs you have (tú)
tiene TYEH-neh he/she has
tenemos teh-NEH-mohs we have
tienen TYEH-nen they have
tener que teh-NEHR keh to have to

tener (to have): tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen. Spanish uses tener for many states where English uses "to be": tener hambre (be hungry), tener sed (thirsty), tener frío / calor (cold / hot), tener sueño (sleepy), tener razón (be right), tener miedo (be afraid). Tener que + infinitive = to have to: Tengo que estudiar.

Remember

Tengo un lápiz. Tiene una casa. Tenemos que estudiar — we have to study. Learn the six forms; do not apply the regular -er pattern here (teno is not a word).

Key terms: tener, irregular present.

Music theory · The major scale and key signatures

30 min · Seminar rotation: Music theory

Learn

Key terms

major scale
Definition
A scale whose steps follow the pattern whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half (W W H W W W H).
Example
C major on the white keys needs no sharps or flats: C D E F G A B C, with half steps at E–F and B–C.
Not this
A major scale is not ‘any eight notes’, and starting on a note other than C will need sharps or flats to keep the pattern.
Connects to
Whole steps and half steps, and the letter names of Day 2, are the pieces. The pattern is new.
key signature
Definition
The sharps or flats placed at the start of the staff to keep a scale’s pattern, added in a fixed order (sharps: F C G D A E B; flats: B E A D G C F).
Example
G major has one sharp, F♯. A major has three: F♯, C♯, G♯. A signature of three flats is E♭ major (the second-to-last flat names the major key).
Not this
A key signature is not a time signature, and the sharps are not added in random order.
Connects to
The major scale’s pattern forces the signature. Each letter name appears exactly once in a major scale.

The major scale follows the step pattern W W H W W W H (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half). From C on the white keys it needs no sharps or flats: C D E F G A B C. Starting on any other note, sharps (♯, raise a half step) or flats (♭, lower a half step) are needed to keep the pattern.

Key signatures. Sharps are added in the order F C G D A E B; flats in the order B E A D G C F. G major has one sharp (F♯); D major two (F♯, C♯). F major has one flat (B♭); B♭ major two (B♭, E♭). Each letter name appears exactly once in a major scale.

Remember

A major scale follows W W H W W W H. Sharps are added in the order F C G D A E B; flats in the order B E A D G C F. G major: one sharp (F♯). D major: F♯, C♯. F major: one flat (B♭). In flat keys, the second-to-last flat names the major key. Each letter name appears exactly once in a major scale.

Key terms: major scale, key signature.

Watch me

Writing G major, as a model, then reading a signature.

  1. Start on G and keep W W H W W W H: G A B C D E F♯ G. The half steps are B–C and F♯–G. One sharp is needed, and it is F♯ (the first sharp in the order F C G D A E B).
  2. A signature of three flats is B♭, E♭, A♭. The second-to-last is E♭, so the major key is E♭ major.
  3. If you flatten the wrong letter, a letter will be repeated or missed, and the pattern will break.
