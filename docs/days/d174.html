Latin · Purpose and result, a tighter Thursday mix

40 min · Method: already-taught purpose and result; original sentences; no new construction

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • a result clause with ut, a first look — Day 166
  • a negative result with ut nōn, a first look — Day 167

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting is a short mix of already-taught purpose and result. No new construction. Keep the four jobs distinct, and keep the present subjunctive distinct from the future. Original classroom sentences only.

Remember

Purpose: ut or nē. Result: ut or ut nōn, often with tam, ita, or sic. The verb is the present subjunctive, not the future.

Watch me

Watch one short mixed set.

  1. Puella venit ut eum sequātur is purpose.
  2. Puella tam celeriter venit ut eum sequātur is result.
  3. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur is a negative result, not a nē-clause.

Together

Translate and label Puella manet nē eum sequātur and Puer tam celeriter surgit ut ēgrediātur.

Hint 1: The first

Nē marks a negative purpose.

Hint 2: The second

Tam and ut mark a positive result.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Translate and label four sentences, one for each already-taught job, using sequor or ēgredior.
  2. Why is nē the wrong negative in a result clause?
  3. Give the present subjunctive and the future, third person singular, of sequor and of ēgredior.

Exercises

  1. Write four original sentences, one for each job, and translate them.
  2. In one sentence, replace the present subjunctive with the future and say why that form is a miss.

Mathematics · The derivative of invented f(x) = x², a tighter mix

40 min · Method: a tighter mix of the difference quotient, the derivative at a point, and f'(x) = 2x, on invented f(x) = x²; no new rule; a formal epsilon argument reserved

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the difference quotient, a first look — Day 171
  • the derivative at a point, a first look — Day 172
  • the derivative of invented f(x) = x² — Day 173

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

This sitting mixes Monday’s difference quotient, Tuesday’s derivative at a point, and Wednesday’s rule f'(x) = 2x, still on invented f(x) = x². No new rule. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. Derivatives of other functions remain reserved. The function is invented.

Remember

The difference quotient of invented f(x) = x² is 2x + h, for h ≠ 0. The derivative is the limit as h approaches 0, so f'(x) = 2x.

Watch me

Watch a short mixed set.

  1. At 3, the difference quotient is 6 + h, and f'(3) = 6.
  2. At a general x, the difference quotient is 2x + h, and f'(x) = 2x.
  3. f is also continuous at 3, from last week, because f(3) = 9 equals the limit of x² as x approaches 3. Continuity and a derivative are still different jobs.

Together

Find f'(7) from the rule, and also from the difference quotient at 7.

Hint 1: The rule

f'(7) = 2 × 7 = 14.

Hint 2: The quotient

[ (7 + h)² − 49 ] / h = 14 + h, and the limit is 14.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key. The function is invented.

  1. Write the difference quotient of invented f(x) = x² at a general x, and the derivative f'(x).
  2. Find f'(3), f'(2), and f'(0), and say how each can be seen both from the rule and from a limit.
  3. Why is a derivative of a different function still reserved?

Exercises

  1. Find f'(8) and f'(−1).
  2. On invented f(x) = x², is f continuous at 2? Give f(2) and the limit. Is that the same job as f'(2) = 4?

Science · Official Example 20.8, the book’s example cost of a compact fluorescent light

40 min · Method: OpenStax official Example 20.8, part (b); 15 W, $7.20/4 = $1.80, 0.1 × $1.50 = $0.15, total $1.95 for 1000 hours; 12 cents remains the book’s example figure

Learn

Key terms

official Example 20.8, the book’s example cost of a compact fluorescent light
Definition
OpenStax official Example 20.8, part (b), replaces the 60-W bulb with a compact fluorescent light that provides the same light output at one-quarter the wattage, so 15 W, costs $1.50, and lasts 10 times longer. For 1000 hours, the official electricity cost is $7.20/4 = $1.80. The official share of the bulb cost for that time is 0.1 × $1.50 = $0.15. The official total is $1.95 for 1000 hours. The 12 cents per kWh remains the book’s example figure, not a named utility rate.
Example
Official book arithmetic for 1000 hours: CFL electricity $1.80, bulb share $0.15, total $1.95, beside part (a)’s official $7.45. The rate is still the book’s example 12 cents per kWh.
Not this
Not Tuesday’s $7.45 as if it were already the CFL total. Not a named utility rate. Not invented official rates. Not last week’s official 170 V used as a cost figure.
Connects to
Tuesday’s official Example 20.8, part (a), $7.20 and $7.45, at the book’s example 12 cents per kWh.

Tuesday’s official Example 20.8, part (a), found $7.20 for the electricity and $7.45 in all, for a 60-W incandescent bulb used 1000 hours, at the book’s example 12 cents per kWh, not a named utility rate. Part (b) now replaces that bulb with a compact fluorescent light that provides the same light output, but at one-quarter the wattage, and which costs $1.50 but lasts 10 times longer, 10,000 hours. One-quarter of 60 W is 15 W, as the book states. For 1000 hours, the official electricity cost is $7.20/4 = $1.80. The official share of the bulb cost for that time is one tenth of $1.50, or 0.1 × $1.50 = $0.15. The official total is $1.95 for 1000 hours. The 12 cents per kWh remains the book’s example figure, not a named utility rate. Those figures are official book example figures.

Remember

Official Example 20.8, part (b): for 1000 hours, electricity $1.80, bulb share $0.15, total $1.95, at the book’s example 12 cents per kWh, beside part (a)’s official $7.45.

  2. The bulb lasts 10 times longer, so the share of $1.50 for 1000 hours is 0.1 × $1.50 = $0.15, official.
  3. The official total is $1.95 for 1000 hours, beside official $7.45 for the incandescent bulb.

Literature · Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 11: a ball at Netherfield, and vanity and pride

40 min · Method: 1813 wording; a ball at Netherfield; “chuses”; vanity and pride; Chapter 12 reserved

Learn

Key terms

Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 11: a ball at Netherfield, and vanity and pride
Definition
Later in Volume I, Chapter 11, in the 1813 wording, Miss Bingley asks, “By the bye, Charles, are you really serious in meditating a dance at Netherfield?” Bingley answers that Darcy “may go to bed, if he chuses, before it begins,” but “as for the ball, it is quite a settled thing.” “Chuses” stays inside the quotation. Later, Elizabeth says she never ridicules what is wise or good, and names “vanity and pride.” Darcy answers, “Yes, vanity is a weakness indeed. But pride—where there is a real superiority of mind, pride will be always under good regulation.” “Elizabeth turned away to hide a smile.” 1813 forms such as “chuses,” “chusing,” “honour,” “any thing,” and “Teaze” stay inside quotations. Chapter 12 remains reserved.
Example
The chapter itself settles a ball at Netherfield, with Bingley’s “if he chuses,” and then the exchange in which Darcy distinguishes vanity from pride. Keep the 1813 wording inside quotations.
Not this
Not Tuesday’s opening of Chapter 11 as if it were new. Not a modernization of “chuses” or of “Teaze.” Not Chapter 12, which remains reserved.
Connects to
Tuesday’s opening of Chapter 11, after dinner and “no enjoyment like reading.” Chapter 12 remains reserved.

Tuesday’s extract opened Chapter 11 with the ladies after dinner and Miss Bingley’s praise of reading. Later in the same chapter, in the 1813 wording, she turns to the mention of a ball and asks, “By the bye, Charles, are you really serious in meditating a dance at Netherfield?—I would advise you, before you determine on it, to consult the wishes of the present party; I am much mistaken if there are not some among us to whom a ball would be rather a punishment than a pleasure.” Bingley answers, “If you mean Darcy, he may go to bed, if he chuses, before it begins—but as for the ball, it is quite a settled thing; and as soon as Nicholls has made white soup enough I shall send round my cards.” “Chuses” stays inside the quotation. In a later exchange, Elizabeth hopes she never ridicules what is wise or good, and names “Such as vanity and pride.” Darcy answers, “Yes, vanity is a weakness indeed. But pride—where there is a real superiority of mind, pride will be always under good regulation.” “Elizabeth turned away to hide a smile.” 1813 forms such as “chuses,” “chusing,” “honour,” “any thing,” and “Teaze” stay inside quotations. Chapter 12 remains reserved.

A ball at Netherfield is “quite a settled thing.” Bingley says Darcy may go to bed “if he chuses.” Darcy calls vanity a weakness, and says that pride, “where there is a real superiority of mind,” will be “under good regulation.” “Chuses” stays inside the quotation.
