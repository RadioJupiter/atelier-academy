Latin · Cases as jobs (Capitula I–X)

40 min · Review of case jobs you already know · original sentences · Capitula I–X only

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • nominative — Day 8
  • accusative — Day 6
  • dative — Day 21
  • genitive — Day 4
  • in + ablative — Day 24

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Word How to say it Meaning
puella rosam habet PWEH-lah ROH-sahm HAH-bet The girl has a rose
puer puellae rosam dat POO-ehr PWEH-lye ROH-sahm daht The boy gives the girl a rose
rosa in hortō est ROH-sah in HOR-toh est The rose is in the garden

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Each case has a job. Nominative: the subject (puella habet). Accusative: the object (rosam habet). Genitive: “of” (rosa puellae, the girl’s rose). Dative: the person to or for whom (puellae dat). Ablative: place phrases you already know, such as in hortō.

A model. Puer puellae rosam dat. The boy gives the girl a rose. Puer is the giver (nominative). rosam is the thing given (accusative). puellae is the person to whom (dative).

We stay in Capitula I–X. Instruction is in English. The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

Nominative: subject. Accusative: object. Dative: to or for whom. Ablative: phrases such as in hortō. They are different jobs.

Watch me

  1. puer is nominative: the one who gives.
  2. rosam is accusative: the thing given.
  3. puellae is dative: the person to whom.

Mathematics · A quadratic inequality, from the graph

40 min · Review: read the inequality from the graph · keep this picture separate from III.36

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • quadratic inequality — Day 51
  • a downward-opening quadratic inequality — Day 53
  • a perfect-square inequality — Day 53

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

You already know how to read a quadratic inequality from the graph. Today you use that method again.

Write one side as zero. The roots split the number line. If the parabola opens upward (a > 0), y < 0 between the roots and y > 0 outside them. If it opens downward, those regions swap. A square (x − a)² is never negative, so (x − a)² < 0 has no real solution — we will not bring in complex numbers to force one.

The same worked shape. y = (x − 2)(x − 6) opens upward. y < 0 when 2 < x < 6. The shading in the figure is that region.

Keep this picture separate from Euclid III.36. Roots are not automatically a tangent and a secant.

Remember

Read a quadratic inequality from the graph. Upward: y < 0 between the roots. Downward: the regions swap. A square is never negative. This is not III.36.

Watch me

Solve (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0 from the graph, and say where y > 0.

  1. The parabola opens upward, roots 2 and 6.
  2. y < 0 between the roots: 2 < x < 6.
  3. y > 0 outside: x < 2 or x > 6.

Together

Solve −(x − 1)(x − 5) > 0, and say why the region is not the same as for (x − 1)(x − 5) > 0.

Hint 1: The shape

The minus makes it open downward.

Hint 2: Downward and greater than zero

A downward-opening parabola is above the axis between the roots: 1 < x < 5. The upward product is positive outside the roots.

Check

  1. Solve (x − 4)(x − 10) < 0.

Exercises

Writing & Rhetoric · Because, but, so: a later letter

40 min · Review: because / but / so on the set Firth wording · no new extract

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • because / but / so, on a later letter — Day 51
  • 79 CE, as a conventional event-year — Day 51

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

You already know the stems because, but, and so. Today you use them again on a source caution.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified) renders a letter from Pliny the Younger to Tacitus. Firth writes: “On the 24th of August, about the seventh hour, my mother drew his attention to the fact that a cloud of unusual size and shape had made its appearance.”

The habit. Because names what the letter can honestly do. But names a limit: it is a later letter, in translation, and 79 CE is the conventional event-year, not a year the set sentence states. So states what you will do: don’t invent a town or a death toll the extract does not give, and don’t treat Firth’s wording as a stopwatch reading.

A model: Firth’s Pliny can describe a cloud of unusual size and shape because the letter says the mother pointed it out at about the seventh hour, but we are reading a later request in a 1900 translation, so I will not treat “the seventh hour” as a stopwatch reading, and I will not name Pompeii or a death toll here.

Remember

Because names what the source can do. But names a limit. So states the habit. Stay inside the set wording. Don’t invent names or numbers the extract does not give.

Watch me

  1. Because: the letter can mention about the seventh hour, because Firth’s wording says the mother pointed the cloud out then.
  2. But: it is a later letter, in a 1900 translation, not a measured log.
  3. So: I will not treat the phrase as a stopwatch reading.

Hint 1: Because

  2. Write one fresh because / but / so sentence about “a later letter, in translation.”

Physics · g on the Earth, the Moon, and Mars

40 min · Review: g = G M / R² with the NASA figures you already used · no invented orbital speed

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • g on Mars, from the same formula — Day 51
  • g on the Moon, a first sourced look — Day 48

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

The formula is one you already know: g = G M / R², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² (three significant figures). Today you gather the three fields you have already computed.

Earth, from the NASA Earth Fact Sheet mass and equatorial radius used in week 10: about 9.79 m/s², beside this course’s working 9.8 m/s². The computation is limited by G’s three significant figures.

The Moon, from the Planetary Fact Sheet family: about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth of 9.8, limited by the two significant figures in the sheet’s mass, and sitting beside NASA Science’s “one-sixth.” Those wordings are not blended into a homemade official third number.

Mars, from last week: mass 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 6,792 km so R = 3,396 km. g comes out about 3.71 m/s², reported as about 3.7 m/s², the same two-figure value the fact sheet lists. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5.

We will not invent an orbital speed for any of the three, and we will not claim more significant figures than G and M support.

Remember

g = G M / R². Earth about 9.79, working 9.8. Moon about 1.6, about one-sixth. Mars about 3.7, about 0.38 of 9.8. No invented orbital speed.

Watch me

Show, in outline, why Mars’s g is reported as about 3.7 m/s², and how it compares with 9.8.

  1. M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 3,396 km, with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹, give about 3.71 m/s².
  2. That is reported as about 3.7, matching the fact sheet and the significant figures.
  3. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5.

Together

Why is the Moon’s g given as about 1.6 m/s², and not as a long string of extra figures?

Hint 1: The sheet

The Planetary Fact Sheet gravity is 1.6 m/s², two significant figures in the mass 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg.

Hint 2: The habit

This course does not invent a tighter official figure than the sources and G support.

Check

Use the formula and the figures from this lesson.

  1. What is this course’s working near-Earth g, and what sourced computation sits beside it?
  2. How does Mars’s g compare with the Moon’s, still using the figures already used?

Exercises

  1. Why will this lesson not invent an orbital speed for Mars?
  2. G has how many significant figures in this course, and why does that limit Earth’s computed g?
  3. 3.7 / 9.8 is about what fraction, and why is “exactly 2/5” a miss?

Logic · Valid forms: ponens, tollens, and disjunctive syllogism

30 min · Review of three valid forms · inclusive “or” remains the default

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • modus ponens — Day 46
  • modus tollens — Day 46
  • disjunctive syllogism — Day 51
  • inclusive “or,” in a first look — Day 51

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Three valid forms have already been taught. Today you gather them. Necessary and sufficient conditions were taught on Day 16; they are not today’s job.

Modus ponens. If P, then Q. P. Therefore Q.

Modus tollens. If P, then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P.

Disjunctive syllogism. P or Q. Not P. Therefore Q. This course’s default “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one: from P you may not infer not-Q under that default.

Invalid look-alikes. Affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P). Denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q). A non-square rectangle remains the counterexample to those two.

Remember

Ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q. Tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. Default “or” is inclusive. Not affirming the consequent.

Watch me

A labeled invented claim: “The tool is a pencil or a brush.” It is not a pencil. What follows, and which form is that?

  1. The tool is a brush.
  2. That is disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q.
  3. The inclusive default is enough for this direction.

Together

Same invented claim: it is a pencil. May you infer that it is not a brush? Why or why not?

Hint 1: The default

This course’s “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one.

Hint 2: The limit

From P you may not infer not-Q under that default.

Check

  1. State modus ponens, and give last week’s square-and-rectangle instance.
  2. State modus tollens, and say how it differs from denying the antecedent.

Exercises

  1. Why is “it is a rectangle; therefore it is a square” not modus ponens?
  2. Give a fresh “or” pair and apply disjunctive syllogism in one direction.
