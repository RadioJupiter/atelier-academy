Latin · Cases as jobs: a Term 1 close (Capitula I–X)

40 min · Method: already-taught case jobs, gathered; original sentences only; not a new chapter

Learn

Key terms

cases as jobs, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the case jobs already taught in Capitula I–X: nominative for the subject, accusative for the object, genitive for ‘of,’ dative for the person to or for whom, and ablative in already-taught phrases such as in with the ablative. They are different jobs, not five names for the same ending.
Example
In puer puellae rosam dat, puer is nominative (the one who gives), rosam is accusative (the thing given), and puellae is dative (the person to whom).
Not this
Not a new chapter, not Capitulum XI, and not a licence to treat every ending as nominative because it ‘comes first.’

Mathematics · A quadratic inequality, from the graph: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught graph reading, gathered; keep the picture separate from III.36

Learn

Key terms

a quadratic inequality, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the already-taught method: read a quadratic inequality from the graph of the already-taught parabola. For an upward-opening product such as (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0, the solution is between the roots: 2 < x < 6. The method is not new today.
Example
y = (x − 2)(x − 6) opens upward. y < 0 when 2 < x < 6. y > 0 when x < 2 or x > 6.
Not this
Not a linear inequality, not Euclid III.36, and not a licence to invent a complex solution for a square that is never negative.
Connects to
Week 11 taught this method as Algebra XVII and XVIII. Today gathers it for the term, from the same graph.

Week 11 taught how to read a quadratic inequality from the graph. Today is a close of that method, not a first look and not a new kind of number.

The method, already taught. Write one side as zero. The roots split the number line. If the parabola opens upward (a > 0), y < 0 between the roots, and y > 0 outside them. If it opens downward, those regions swap. A square (x − a)² is never negative: (x − a)² < 0 has no real solution, and this course does not begin complex numbers to “solve” it.

The same worked shape. y = (x − 2)(x − 6) opens upward. y < 0 when 2 < x < 6. The shading in the figure is that region.

Keep this picture separate from Euclid III.36. Roots are not automatically a tangent and a secant.

Remember

Read a quadratic inequality from the graph. Upward: y < 0 between the roots. Downward: the regions swap. A square is never negative. Not III.36.

Key terms: a quadratic inequality, a Term 1 close.

Writing & Rhetoric · Because, but, so: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught because / but / so, used again on the set Firth wording; no new extract

Learn

Key terms

because / but / so, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the already-taught because / but / so habit, used on a sourced caution already set: the because names what the source can do, the but names a limit, and the so states the habit. It is not a first look at the stems, and not a licence to invent a name or a number the set extract does not give.
Example
Firth’s Pliny can describe a cloud at about the seventh hour because the letter says the mother pointed it out, but it is a later letter in a 1900 translation, so I will not treat the wording as a stopwatch reading.
Not this
Not a claim that the letter is useless, and not a new extract (the voyage, Rectina, Stabiae, and the uncle’s death remain reserved).
Connects to
Weeks 10–11 already used because / but / so on a Josephus caution and on this later letter. Today gathers the habit for the term, on the same set wording.

Physics · g on the Earth, the Moon, and Mars: a Term 1 close

40 min · Method: already-taught g = G M / R², with sourced NASA figures already used; no invented orbital speed

Learn

Key terms

g on the Earth, the Moon, and Mars, a Term 1 close
Definition
A close of the already-taught formula g = G M / R², applied to sourced NASA fact-sheet figures already used: Earth about 9.79 m/s² beside the working 9.8; the Moon about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth; Mars about 3.7 m/s², matching the sheet, about 0.38 of 9.8. G remains 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². No invented orbital speed.
Example
Mars: M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 3,396 km, g about 3.71 m/s², reported as about 3.7 m/s².
Not this
Not a new formula, not an average of two NASA pages into a homemade official number, and not an invented orbital speed.
Connects to
Weeks 10–11 computed these three values from the same formula and the same fact-sheet family. Today gathers them for the term.

The formula is the one already taught: g = G M / R², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² (three significant figures). Today is a close of the three already-computed fields, not a first look and not a new formula.

Earth, from the NASA Earth Fact Sheet mass and equatorial radius used in week 10: about 9.79 m/s², beside this course’s working 9.8 m/s². The computation is limited by G’s three significant figures.

The Moon, from the Planetary Fact Sheet family: about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth of 9.8, limited by the two significant figures in the sheet’s mass, and sitting beside NASA Science’s “one-sixth.” Those wordings are not blended into a homemade official third number.

Mars, from last week: mass 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 6,792 km so R = 3,396 km. g comes out about 3.71 m/s², reported as about 3.7 m/s², the same two-figure value the fact sheet lists. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5.

We will not invent an orbital speed for any of the three, and we will not claim more significant figures than G and M support.

Remember

g = G M / R². Earth about 9.79, working 9.8. Moon about 1.6, about one-sixth. Mars about 3.7, about 0.38 of 9.8. No invented orbital speed.

Definition
A close of three already-taught valid forms: modus ponens (If P then Q; P; therefore Q), modus tollens (If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P), and disjunctive syllogism (P or Q; not P; therefore Q). This course’s default “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one. They are different forms, not three names for the same move.
Example
Modus ponens: If it is a square, then it is a rectangle; it is a square; therefore it is a rectangle. Disjunctive syllogism: The tool is a pencil or a brush; it is not a pencil; therefore it is a brush.
Not this
Not affirming the consequent, not denying the antecedent, and not inferring not-Q from P under an inclusive “or.” Necessary and sufficient conditions remain Day 16’s lesson and are not reteached as new.

Watch me

A labeled invented claim: “The tool is a pencil or a brush.” It is not a pencil. What follows, and which form is that?

  1. The tool is a brush.
  2. That is disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q.
  3. The inclusive default is enough for this direction.

Together

Same invented claim: it is a pencil. May you infer that it is not a brush? Why or why not?
