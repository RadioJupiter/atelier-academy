Latin · Capitulum XV: nōs and vōs, and licet

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV continues. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

nōs and vōs
Definition
Nōs means we or us; vōs means you (plural). The dative and ablative forms are nōbīs and vōbīs. Like ego and tū, these pronouns are written for contrast or clarity; the verb ending already names the person.
Example
Vōs recitātis. — You are the ones reciting. Nōbīs tacēre difficile est. — It is hard for us to be silent.
Not this
Not a first look at ego, tū, mē, tē, mihi, or tibi, or at nōbīscum from last week. The new work is the plural pronouns and nōbīs / vōbīs.
Connects to
Yesterday’s endings -mus and -tis already said we and you (plural). Today those persons can be named with a pronoun when a contrast is wanted.
licet
Definition
Licet is an impersonal verb: it is allowed. It has no personal subject. The person who is allowed stands in the dative, and what is allowed is an infinitive: Nōbīs recitāre licet.
Example
Vōbīs exīre nōn licet. — You (plural) are not allowed to go out.
Not this
Not the same as posse. Posse says what someone is able to do; licet says what is permitted. Recitāre possum is I can recite; Mihi recitāre licet is I am allowed to recite.
Connects to
The dative here names the person concerned, in a use close to last week’s dative of interest, now with this impersonal verb.

The plural pronouns complete a table that began with ego and tū, and with mē, tē, mihi, and tibi:

Word How to say it Meaning
nōs nohs we, us
vōs wohs you (plural)
nōbīs NOH-beess to/for us; by/with us
vōbīs WOH-beess to/for you (plural)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Recitāmus already means “we recite.” Nōs recitāmus adds contrast: we, as opposed to someone else, are reciting.

Licet means it is allowed. Nothing in the sentence is the subject of licet. Write a dative for the person who is permitted, and an infinitive for the act: Mihi recitāre licet. — I am allowed to recite. Nōbīs tacēre licet. — We are allowed to be silent.

Keep licet and posse apart. Ability and permission are not the same fact.

Remember

Nōs and vōs are the plural pronouns; nōbīs and vōbīs are the dative and ablative forms. Licet, it is allowed, takes a dative and an infinitive, and has no personal subject. It is not posse.

Key terms: nōs and vōs, licet.

Mathematics · Geometry XL: a sector of a circle

45 min · Method: a sector as a fraction of the circle already measured yesterday

Learn

Key terms

sector of a circle
Definition
A sector of a circle is the region bounded by two radii and the included arc. Its area is the same fraction of the circle’s area as the central angle is of 360°, or as the arc is of the circumference: A = (θ/360) × πr², when θ is measured in degrees.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin sector (Wiktionary). The Latin word is from secō, to cut.
Example
A 90° sector of a classroom circle of radius 6 has area (90/360) × 36π = 9π.
Not this
Not a triangle with two radii and a chord, and not the area of the whole circle. Not a first look at a central angle, which was taught on Day 21.
Connects to
The central angle, already taught, now names the fraction of the circle that the sector occupies.

A sector is a “slice” of a circle: two radii and the arc between them. The angle at the center is a central angle, already taught on Day 21. What is new is the area of that slice.

The slice is the same fraction of the whole circle as its angle is of a full turn. A full turn is 360° in the degree measure this course already uses. So

Asector = (θ / 360) × πr².

The same fraction of the circumference is the arc length: ℓ = (θ / 360) × 2πr. That is yesterday’s circumference, taken in part, not a new constant.

A 90° sector is a quarter of the circle. For r = 6, the whole area is 36π, and the sector is 9π.

Remember

A sector is bounded by two radii and an arc. Its area is (θ/360) × πr², in degrees. The matching arc length is (θ/360) × 2πr. A central angle is not a first look.

Key terms: sector of a circle.

Watch me

Watch a 90° sector of radius 6.

  1. The whole area is π × 6² = 36π.
  2. The angle is 90° out of 360°, which is 1/4.
  3. The sector area is (1/4) × 36π = 9π. The arc length would be (1/4) × 12π = 3π.

Hint 1: The fraction

60/360 = 1/6. The sector is one sixth of the circle.

Hint 2: The whole first

The whole area is 36π. One sixth of that is 6π.

Writing & Rhetoric · The opening and the close

40 min · Method: a short opening that states the thesis, and a close that does not merely repeat it, for the same analytical essay

Learn

Key terms

essay opening
Definition
The opening of this short analytical essay names the set text or question and states the thesis, without a long warm-up and without announcing the outline (“In this essay I will…”).
Example
A tight opening might name the set Longfellow passage and state, in one or two sentences, the claim the body paragraphs will defend.
Not this
Not a first look at a thesis, and not the first body paragraph. Not a biography of the author, unless the set task has made that the question, which this week’s essay has not.
Connects to
The thesis has been on the outline since Day 78. Today it is placed at the head of the essay, in prose, not in a slogan.
essay close
Definition
The close of this short analytical essay returns to the thesis in new wording, after the body and the other side have been heard, and stops. It does not tack on a new reason, and it does not merely copy the opening sentence.
Example
A close may, in a sentence, show what the other-side paragraph has left standing. It should not open a new case.
Not this
Not a first look at a conclusion as a school word in general, and not a moral for the reader’s life unless the set text itself has made that the question.
Connects to
The close is written after you know what the body and the other side actually said, so that it can return to the thesis honestly.

The body paragraphs and the other-side paragraph now exist, or exist as drafts. Today you write the two ends of the same short essay: a brief opening and a close.

The opening names the set text or question and states the thesis. It does not preview every heading (“first I will… then I will…”). It does not begin with a vast claim about all poetry or all history.

The close returns to the thesis in wording that has earned the right to be said, because the body and the other side have been heard. It does not repeat the opening sentence with “therefore” in front. It does not add a new reason that should have been a body paragraph. It stops.

Write both today, as paragraphs or as tight paragraph-openers, and keep them with the same outline. Tomorrow you will assemble the whole essay.

Remember

The opening names the question or set text and states the thesis, without a tour of the outline. The close returns to the thesis in new wording and stops, without a new reason.

Key terms: essay opening, essay close.

Watch me

  2. Repaired opening: it names the set extract and states the claim (fear stops the speaker; a report of help moves him), in one or two sentences.
  3. Weak close: a new point about a later canto, or a copied opening. Repaired close: a short return to the claim, now after the other side has been heard, and a stop.

Physics · The period of a mass on a spring

40 min · Method: T = 2π√(m/k), from OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.3, contrasted with last week’s pendulum

Learn

Key terms

period of a mass-spring
Definition
For a mass m on a spring that obeys Hooke’s law, and with no damping, the period is T = 2π√(m/k). T is larger for a larger mass and smaller for a stiffer spring. It does not depend on the amplitude.
Example
An invented classroom mass of 0.20 kg on a spring with k = 80 N/m has T = 2π√(0.20/80) = 2π√(0.0025) = 2π × 0.050 = 0.10π seconds, about 0.31 s.
Not this
Not last week’s simple-pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g). A pendulum’s period, for a small swing, does not depend on mass; a mass-spring period does.
Connects to
OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.3, “Simple Harmonic Motion: A Special Periodic Motion” (CC BY), gives T = 2π√(m/k) and f = 1/(2π)√(k/m), independent of amplitude.
simple harmonic motion
Definition
Simple harmonic motion, in OpenStax’s wording, is oscillatory motion for a system whose net force can be described by Hooke’s law. A mass on a spring, without damping, is the standard example.
Example
A block sliding on a frictionless surface, attached to a spring and released from a displacement, performs simple harmonic motion of amplitude equal to that first displacement, as in OpenStax Figure 16.9.
Not this
Not every oscillation. Last week’s pendulum is close to this for a small swing (less than about 15°), which is why a similar square-root formula appeared, but the pendulum formula is not F = −kx written in the same direct way, and mass drops out of it.
Connects to
The name is from OpenStax 16.3. This lesson uses it for the mass-spring system that obeys today’s formula.

OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.3 (CC BY), gives the period of a simple harmonic oscillator — a system whose net force follows Hooke’s law — as

T = 2π√(m/k),

and the frequency as f = 1/T = (1/(2π))√(k/m). Neither T nor f depends on the amplitude. Both depend on the mass m and on the force constant k only.

That is a real contrast with last week. For a simple pendulum and a small swing, T = 2π√(L/g) depends on length and on g, not on mass. For a mass on a spring, a larger mass makes a longer period.

Invented classroom figures: m = 0.20 kg, k = 80 N/m.

T = 2π√(0.20/80) = 2π√(0.0025) = 2π × 0.050 = 0.10π s, about 0.31 s if a decimal is wanted.

OpenStax’s later car example in 16.3 uses m = 900 kg and k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m (the same k as Example 16.1) and finds a frequency of about 1.36 Hz. Those figures stay with their source and are not mixed into the classroom 0.20 kg spring.

Remember

T = 2π√(m/k), from OpenStax 16.3. T grows with √m and shrinks as k grows. T does not depend on amplitude. A simple pendulum’s small-swing period does not depend on mass; this one does.

Key terms: period of a mass-spring, simple harmonic motion.

Watch me

  1. m/k = 0.20/80 = 0.0025. The square root is 0.050.
  2. T = 2π × 0.050 = 0.10π s.
  3. If the same mass were on a simple pendulum, last week’s formula would not use k, and would not change if a different bob mass were chosen, for a small swing. That is the contrast.

Hint 1: The ratio

Hint 2: Exact enough

T = 2π√(0.016) = 2π√(2/125). A decimal is about 0.80 s, which is 2π × 0.1265, rounded. Prefer 2π√(0.016) if you are not asked for a decimal.

History · The four triangles and the dome that seems suspended (Dewing I.1.42–49)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.42–49; no invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

four triangles under the circle (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.43–45, because the arches form a four-cornered plan, “the stonework between the arches produces four triangles (trigôna).” Each triangle is contracted to a point below, “by the coming together of each pair of arches,” and “ends in the segment of a circle which it supports.” Upon that circle rests the dome.
Example
The four triangles are the transition from the square of the arches to the circle that carries the dome. A later architectural name for such triangles is reserved for today’s art-history seminar and is not required in this history reading.
Not this
Not a first look at Monday’s four piers and four arches. Not a measured modern plan of the triangles.
Connects to
Dewing I.1.43–45, as posted at LacusCurtius.
dome that seems suspended (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.45–46, “upon this circle rests the huge spherical dome (sphairoeidês tholos) which makes the structure exceptionally beautiful. Yet it seems not to rest upon solid masonry, but to cover the space with its golden dome (sphaira) suspended from Heaven.”
Example
The seeming is like last week’s crescent that seemed to float and was actually braced. Here Dewing again states a seeming (suspended from Heaven) after stating a support (the circle).
Not this
Not a first look at last week’s crescent, and not an official height of the dome. Not a claim that the dome has no support.
Connects to
Dewing I.1.45–46. I.1.47–49 then describe a harmony in mid-air and a vision that “constantly shifts,” so that observers “depart… overwhelmed by the bewildering sight.”

Procopius, The Buildings, Book I, chapter 1, translated by H. B. Dewing, Loeb Classical Library, 1940, as posted at LacusCurtius: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html The 1940 copyright was not renewed and expired in 1968; the translation is in the public domain in the United States. British spellings in Dewing are kept inside quotations. No official measurement of the church is invented.

Monday ended with the cylindrical structure on the crowns of the arches, through which daylight first smiles (I.1.41). I.1.42 continues that it “towers above the whole earth, as I believe,” and is interrupted at short intervals with openings “to be channels for the admission of light in sufficient measure.”

Then the triangles and the dome, in Dewing’s wording:

I.1.43–45: the arches form a four-cornered plan; the stonework between them produces four triangles; each is an acute point below and “ends in the segment of a circle which it supports.”

I.1.46: “Yet it seems not to rest upon solid masonry, but to cover the space with its golden dome (sphaira) suspended from Heaven.”

I.1.47–49: the details, “fitted together with incredible skill in mid-air,” produce “a single and most extraordinary harmony,” and yet the eye cannot linger; “the vision constantly shifts suddenly”; observers “are still unable to understand the skilful craftsmanship, but they always depart from there overwhelmed by the bewildering sight. So much, then, for this.”

Keep “centre,” “skilful,” and other British forms inside quotations. No official height is invented. I.1.50 and after remain reserved.

Remember

Four triangles stand between the arches and end in the circle that carries the huge spherical dome. The dome seems suspended from Heaven, yet it is said to rest upon that circle. The eye cannot linger, and observers leave overwhelmed. Dewing I.1.42–49; I.1.50 remains reserved.

Atelier · Modeling inside the shadow family, still monochrome

60 min · Method: modest modeling inside the already-stated shadow family, without breaking the family or finishing the light

Learn

Key terms

modeling in the shadow family
Definition
Modeling in the shadow family, at this stage, means small, monochrome changes of value inside the shadow, to show a turn of the form, while the whole family remains darker than the light family. The light family is left alone, or nearly so.
Example
A slightly deeper core, already taught in earlier weeks, may be restated inside the shadow. It must still belong to the shadow family, not become a new black outline.
Not this
Not a first look at core shadow, and not a first look at the families. Not full modeling of the light, and not full color. Not a finish of the features.
Connects to
Monday’s lay-in and Tuesday’s check are still in force. Today adds work only inside the shadow family.

The families are stated and have been checked. Today you may model a little, and only in the shadow family.

Look for one turn inside the shadow: a slightly deeper core, a softer recession, a place where the form goes back. Make the change small, in the same monochrome. Then stand back. The whole shadow family must still be darker than the light family, and must still read as one family at a distance.

Leave the light family almost untouched. Light is easy to fill with early halftones that flatten the design. That work is not today’s job. Features still wait. Full color still waits until Term 3.

If you are in a drawing rehearsal, the same rule holds: model inside the shadow with the pencil or charcoal, and do not start a tight finish in the light.

Remember

Model only inside the shadow family, in small monochrome steps, without breaking the family. Leave the light family alone, or nearly so. Features and full color still wait.

Key terms: modeling in the shadow family.

Watch me

Watch one small turn made in the shadow, then checked at a distance.

  1. A slightly deeper value is placed where the core of the shadow turns away from the light, already known as a core shadow.
  2. The mark stays inside the family. It is not a new outline at the terminator.
  3. At a distance, the shadow is still one family, darker than the light. If it has broken into patches, the modeling is too strong and must be softened.

Hint 1: One turn

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of saber

40 min · Method: the irregular preterite of saber (sup-), with a careful completed-event sense

Learn

Key terms

supe
Definition
Supe is the first-person singular preterite of saber. The stem is sup-: supe, supiste, supo, supimos, supisteis, supieron. In the preterite, the usual school sense is a completed event of finding out, or coming to know, on that occasion, not a general state of knowing.
Example
Ayer supe la verdad. — Yesterday I found out the truth. (Invented classroom sentence.)
Not this
Not a first look at the present sé, and not a first look at pude or puse. Not the imperfect, which is not today’s job. Not last week’s supe invented as a uv-stem: this is a different verb and a different stem.
Connects to
English “I knew” can name a state. Spanish supe, in this school sense, names the occasion of finding out. This lesson does not treat that as a full map of every use, only as the sense to learn with the preterite now.

Saber, to know (a fact) in the present, has an irregular preterite stem sup-:

Word How to say it Meaning
supe SOO-peh I found out / I came to know
supiste soo-PEES-teh you (singular, informal) found out
supo SOO-poh he, she, or you (formal) found out
supimos soo-PEE-mohs we found out
supisteis soo-PEES-tayss you (plural, informal, Spain) found out
supieron soo-PYEH-rohn they, or you (plural) found out

The school sense to learn now is a completed event: I found out, I came to know, on that occasion. Invented: Ayer supe la verdad. — Yesterday I found out the truth.

English “I knew” is often a state. Do not treat supe as a simple copy of that English state. This is the sense taught with the preterite here; it is not a claim to have mapped every later use.

Elective · Art history: the pendentive, as a later name for Dewing’s four triangles

40 min · Method: pendentive as a later architectural term for the four triangles already read in Dewing I.1.43–45, contrasted with the Pantheon already taught

Learn

Key terms

pendentive
Definition
A pendentive is a concave triangular section of vaulting that makes the transition from a square base to the circle of a dome and helps carry the dome’s weight. In this lesson it is taught as the later architectural name for the four triangles Dewing describes in Buildings I.1.43–45, “the stonework between the arches” that “produces four triangles” and “ends in the segment of a circle which it supports.”
Origin
Borrowed from French pendentif (Wiktionary).
Example
The huge spherical dome in I.1.45–46 rests upon that circle. The pendentives are the four triangular transitions, not the dome itself, and not Monday’s four piers alone.
Not this
Not a first look at Dewing’s four arches or four piers (Monday), or at the dome’s seeming (today’s history lesson, if you have already read it, or the same extract used here). Not a first look at the Pantheon, already taught on Day 38 as a circular temple with a concrete dome and an oculus, generally treated as a Hadrianic rebuild.
Connects to
The word is a later school term, borrowed from French pendentif (Wiktionary). Dewing himself writes “triangles (trigôna),” not “pendentives.” This course uses both, with that caution.

Today’s history extract, Dewing I.1.43–45, describes four triangles of stonework between the arches, ending in a circle that supports the dome. A later architectural name for those triangular transitions is pendentive.

Dewing does not use that later word. He writes “four triangles (trigôna).” This seminar uses the later name honestly, as a school term for what he describes, not as if it had appeared in the 1940 translation.

A pendentive is not the dome, and not a pier. It is the concave triangle that lets a dome sit on a square of arches.

A useful contrast is already in this course. The Pantheon in Rome, taught on Day 38, is a circular temple with a concrete dome and a central oculus, generally treated as a rebuild under Hadrian. That dome sits on a circular plan, not on four triangles rising from a square of arches. The two buildings are not to be merged. No official height or span is invented for either one.

The seeming in I.1.46 — a golden dome “suspended from Heaven” that is also said to rest on the circle — belongs with this form: the support is real, and the seeming is Dewing’s.

Remember

A pendentive is the later name for a triangular transition from a square of arches to the circle of a dome. Dewing calls them triangles. The Pantheon, already taught, is a dome on a circular plan and is not the same arrangement. No invented measurements.

Key terms: pendentive.
