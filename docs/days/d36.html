Latin · Capitulum X, part 1: more nouns of the third family

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. X · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

third-declension types (homō, leō, pēs)
Definition
More nouns of the third family, still with genitive singular in -is and accusative singular in -em. The stem is seen in the genitive: homō, hominis (stem homin-); leō, leōnis (stem leōn-); pēs, pedis (stem ped-). vōx, vōcis is already known on the same plan.
Example
Homō in viā est. Leōnem rēx videt. Pēs puerī magnus est.
Not this
Not a first-declension -a noun, and not a claim that the nominative singular already shows the whole stem (homō does not; look at hominis). Do not invent *homōnem or *pēsem.
Connects to

Mathematics · Geometry XVII: the tangent-chord angle (Euclid III.32)

60 min · Method: a picture, then a proof from facts already taught (radius to a tangent; inscribed and central angles)

Learn

Key terms

alternate segment theorem (III.32)
Definition
The angle between a tangent and a chord, at the point of contact, equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment of the circle (the segment on the other side of the chord). This is Euclid, Elements, Book III, Proposition 32.
Example
If a tangent at A meets a chord AB, and C is a point on the circle in the segment of the chord that does not lie against that tangent-chord angle, then the tangent-chord angle equals ∠ACB.
Not this
Not ‘any angle next to a tangent equals any inscribed angle,’ and not a new definition of a tangent. A tangent still meets the circle at exactly one point, and the radius to that point is still perpendicular to the tangent (Day 19).
Connects to
Day 21’s fact (an inscribed angle is half the central angle on the same arc, Euclid III.20) and Day 19’s perpendicular are the engine of the proof, not decorations.
tangent (origin recalled)
Definition
A line that meets a circle at exactly one point. The word is borrowed from Latin tangēns, ‘touching’ (in the phrase līnea tangēns, a touching line), the present participle of tangō, ‘touch.’
Origin
From Latin tangēns, “touching,” in līnea tangēns, “a touching line.”
Example
The blue line in today’s figure is a tangent at A. It touches the circle only at A.
Not this
Not a chord (a chord meets the circle twice), and not a line that merely looks close to the circle on the page.
Connects to
The new theorem is about an angle this touching line makes with a chord, not a new meaning of tangent.

The fact. A tangent at A and a chord AB form an angle (marked 1 in the figure). That angle equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment (marked 2: ∠ACB). “Alternate” means the segment of the circle on the other side of the chord from angle 1.

A proof, from what you already have.

  1. The radius OA is perpendicular to the tangent at A (Day 19).
  2. OA = OB (both radii), so in triangle OAB the base angles are equal. Let ∠OAB = α. Then ∠OBA = α, and the central angle ∠AOB = 180° − 2α (Day 6, angle sum).
  3. The tangent-chord angle is 90° − α, because it sits next to α in the right angle between tangent and radius.
  4. The inscribed angle in the alternate segment, standing on the same arc AB, is half the central angle (Day 21, III.20): ½(180° − 2α) = 90° − α.

The two angles are equal. That is III.32. The figure’s numbers 1 and 2 are those two angles.

A near-miss. The inscribed angle on the same side as the tangent-chord angle is a different angle; the theorem names the alternate segment, not “any angle in the figure.”

Remember

III.32: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment. The proof uses OA ⊥ tangent, OA = OB, the triangle sum, and the inscribed-angle theorem (III.20).

Key terms: alternate segment theorem (III.32), tangent (origin recalled).

Watch me

  2. Angle 2 is the inscribed angle in the alternate segment, ∠ACB.
  3. They are equal by Euclid III.32 (or by the proof: both equal 90° − α).

Writing & Rhetoric · Integrating a short quotation

45 min · Method: a short attributed quotation inside a paragraph that still keeps a topic-sentence promise

Learn

Key terms

integrated quotation
Definition
A short, attributed quotation placed inside a sentence or paragraph of your own, so that the quotation supports the paragraph’s job and does not replace the topic sentence. Attribution and quotation marks (or a clearly marked short extract) are required.
Example
Bacon writes that studies “serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability” (“Of Studies,” 1625). The sentence uses only that short opening, and then the paragraph must say what the quotation is doing there.
Not this
Not a paragraph that is only a quotation, and not a long unattributed extract. Day 18 already required introduction and explanation; today that habit is used inside this week’s three-paragraph frame.
Connects to
Literature this week will offer a short public-domain line from Sophocles, *Antigone*, trans. R. C. Jebb (1917). You may use Bacon, or a short Jebb line once it has been set in class, but not a longer passage, and not the end of the play before Thursday’s literature lesson has taught it.

A quotation is a guest in your paragraph. The topic sentence still names the job. You then introduce a short extract, attribute it, and explain it in your own words.

Physics · A first look at gravitation: an inverse-square force

50 min · Method: the qualitative inverse-square pattern, then Newton’s formula with G named as a standard constant, not a classroom measurement

Learn

Key terms

Newton’s law of gravitation (first look)
Definition
Every two masses attract each other with a force along the line joining them. The size of that force is F = G Mm / r², where M and m are the two masses, r is the distance between their centers (in this first look, treated as particles or as spheres), and G is the universal gravitational constant.
Example
If the distance r doubles, and the masses stay the same, the force becomes one fourth as large, because of the r² in the denominator. If one mass doubles, the force doubles.
Not this
Not a classroom measurement of G, and not a new value of g as if it were G. g (already used as 9.8 N/kg near the Earth) is the gravitational field, or the acceleration, near the Earth; G is the constant in the general formula. This lesson does not invent an orbital speed.
Connects to
Power and torque last week were mechanical rates and turning measures. Today’s force is a different idea: an attraction that grows weaker with distance as 1/r².
inverse-square (gravitation)
Definition
A pattern in which a quantity is proportional to 1/r²: if r is multiplied by 2, the quantity is multiplied by 1/4; if r is multiplied by 3, the quantity is multiplied by 1/9.
Example
Three times as far from a mass, with nothing else changed, the gravitational force is one ninth as large.
Not this
Not “half as far, twice the force” (that would be a simple inverse, 1/r, not 1/r²), and not a claim about sound or light in this lesson.
Connects to
The formula F = G Mm / r² is the inverse-square pattern, with the two masses in the numerator.

The formula, as a first look.

F = G Mm / r²

  • M and m are the two masses.
  • r is the distance between centers (in this first model).
  • G is the gravitational constant. This course uses the standard value G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², labeled as a standard constant, not as a number you measured in class.

The pattern to practice. Keep the masses fixed and change r:

  • r → 2r, then F → F/4
  • r → 3r, then F → F/9
  • r → (1/2)r, then F → 4F

Two cautions. g = 9.8 N/kg near the Earth is not the same symbol as G. And this lesson does not invent an orbital speed, or treat every path as a circle (Friday will look at orbits qualitatively).

Remember

F = G Mm / r², with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² as a standard constant, not a classroom measurement. Inverse square: double the distance, one fourth the force. g and G are not the same. No invented orbital speed.

Key terms: Newton’s law of gravitation (first look), inverse-square (gravitation).

Watch me

  3. The masses and G are unchanged; only r² in the denominator changes.

History · After Augustus: 14 CE, and the succession of Tiberius

50 min · Method: a short conventional timeline and a caution that succession is not a new founding day

Learn

Key terms

succession of Tiberius
Definition
Augustus died in 14 CE. Tiberius, already a grown member of the household and a long-serving commander, became princeps after him. The year 14 CE is a conventional date for that succession, not a new constitution and not a second “start of the principate” to replace 27 BCE.
Example
A careful sentence: “In 14 CE, after the death of Augustus, Tiberius succeeded as princeps.” The offices and the habit of one man’s pre-eminence continued; this lesson does not treat 14 CE as the day the Republic returned, or as the day it finally “fell.”
Not this
Not a claim that the succession was simple, uncontested in every contemporary mind, or fully described by later writers as a modern election. Tacitus and Suetonius remain later (early second century CE).
Connects to
Last week’s conventional pair (Actium 31 BCE; 27 BCE as the start of the principate) still stands. 14 CE is a later date on the same timeline, for a death and a succession.

A short continuation of last week’s timeline (dates labeled conventional where this course has used that caution):

  • 14 CE: death of Augustus, and the succession of Tiberius as prīnceps.
  • The principate continues. This is not a new founding day, and not a return of the Republic.

How we know, still. Tacitus and Suetonius write in the early second century CE, about a century after 14 CE as well as after 27 BCE. The Res Gestae remains Augustus’s first-person record; it does not, by itself, narrate the years after his death.

Do not invent a scene of the succession that no source in this lesson has set. The fact taught today is the date, the names, and the caution.

Remember

Augustus died in 14 CE; Tiberius succeeded as princeps. 14 CE is a succession date, not a new founding day and not a “fall” or a return of the Republic. Later writers are still later.

Modern language · Spanish: the superlative

40 min · Method: el/la más (or menos) + adjective + de, built on Day 19’s comparatives; English remains the language of instruction

Learn

Key terms

superlative (el más / el menos)
Definition
A present-tense pattern that names the top or bottom of a group: el/la/los/las + más (or menos) + adjective + de. The article agrees with the noun being described; the adjective agrees too.
Example
Ana es la más alta de la clase. Este libro es el menos difícil de los tres. Estos museos son los más grandes de la ciudad.
Not this
Not a two-thing comparison with más…que (Day 19), and not *el más de alta. The de introduces the group, not a second person being compared with que. Still not a past tense.
Connects to
Day 19 taught más…que, menos…que, and tan…como, and the irregulars mejor, peor, mayor, menor. Today those irregulars also have a superlative use: el mejor, la peor, with de for the group.
Word How to say it Meaning
el más alto de el mahs AHL-toh deh the tallest of
la más alta de lah mahs AHL-tah deh the tallest (feminine) of
el menos difícil de el MEH-nohs dee-fee-SEEL deh the least difficult of
el mejor de el meh-HOR deh the best of
Ana es la más alta de la clase AH-nah ess lah mahs AHL-tah deh lah KLAH-seh Ana is the tallest in the class

The pattern. el / la / los / las + más (or menos) + adjective + de + the group. The article and the adjective agree: la más alta, los más grandes, las menos largas.

Irregulars, as a superlative. el mejor, la peor, el mayor, el menor — still not el más mejor. De names the group: el mejor de los libros.

This is not más…que (a comparison of two). Original sentences: Este libro es el más largo de la mesa. Luis es el menor de la familia. Estas lecciones son las más importantes del día. (You may use del as de + el.)

Remember

el/la/los/las + más or menos + adjective + de + the group. Agreement required. Irregulars: el mejor, la peor, el mayor, el menor — not más mejor. This is not más…que.

Logic · A first look at the undistributed middle

40 min · Method: contrast Barbara with one invalid form that looks like a syllogism

Learn

Key terms

undistributed middle
Definition
An invalid syllogistic form in which the middle term never refers to the whole of its class in either premise, so it does not actually link the other two terms. A stock example: All dogs are mammals; all cats are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs. The middle term “mammals” is not distributed in either premise.
Example
“All squares are rectangles; all rhombi are rectangles; therefore all rhombi are squares.” That is the same invalid form: “rectangles” is not used in its whole-class sense in either premise.
Not this
Not Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), in which the middle term is distributed in the first premise (“All M…”). And not a claim that every argument with a repeated word is invalid.
Connects to
Day 6 taught validity and two propositional fallacies (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent). Today’s fallacy belongs to the syllogism, after last week’s Barbara. “Distributed” here means, in this first look, that the term is used of the whole class (as in “All M…”).

Barbara, recalled. All M are P. All S are M. Therefore all S are P. The middle term M is used as a whole class in “All M are P.” That is what lets it link S to P.

An invalid look-alike. All S are M. All P are M. Therefore all S are P. Here M appears in both premises, but always as the predicate of an A-sentence: “All … are M.” In this first look, that use does not speak of all M, so M is undistributed. The two classes S and P may both sit inside M without being the same.

Geometry you already know can host the same form, wrongly: All squares are rectangles; all rhombi are rectangles; therefore all rhombi are squares. Both premises can be true (in this course’s geometry, with a rhombus that is not a square), and the conclusion is false. So the form is invalid.

This lesson does not teach a full theory of distribution for E, I, and O. It teaches one named failure, in contrast to Barbara.

Remember

The undistributed middle is an invalid form: the middle term never covers its whole class, so it does not link S to P. Barbara is different: “All M are P” distributes M. Day 6’s propositional fallacies are a different family.

Key terms: undistributed middle.

Watch me

Write the stock mammals example, mark the middle term, and say why it is not Barbara.

  1. All dogs are mammals. All cats are mammals. Therefore all cats are dogs.
  2. The middle term is mammals.
  3. It is not Barbara, because neither premise is “All mammals are …”; mammals is never the whole-class subject of an A-sentence here.

Together

Why can both premises be true and the conclusion false in the rectangles example?

Hint 1: The facts

All squares are rectangles, and all rhombi are rectangles, in this course; not all rhombi are squares.

Hint 2: The form

Sharing a predicate (“are rectangles”) does not force the two subjects to be the same class.
