Latin · Capitulum XVI: deponent verbs, a first look

30 min · Latin · Capitulum XVI, Tempestās, begins. Original sentences only; the book’s story is read in the book, not copied here.

Learn

Key terms

deponent verb
Definition
A deponent verb has forms that look passive but are translated as active. In this first look, the course uses the third person and the present infinitive only: loquitur, he or she speaks; loquuntur, they speak; loquī, to speak.
Example
Puer loquitur. — The boy speaks. Discipulī loquuntur. — The pupils speak. Loquī possum. — I am able to speak.
Not this
Not a first look at ordinary active present endings, closed last week, and not a first look at a true passive meaning. A deponent is not “passive in sense.” Do not invent first- and second-person deponent forms today; they are reserved.
Connects to
The school name deponent comes from the idea that these verbs have laid aside their active forms. This lesson teaches the behavior, not a history of the name.
loquitur and cōnātur
Definition
Loquitur means he or she speaks; cōnātur means he or she tries. Both look as if they were passive, but they are deponent and are translated as active. The infinitives are loquī, to speak, and cōnārī, to try.
Example
Magister loquitur. Discipulus recitāre cōnātur. — The schoolmaster speaks. The pupil tries to recite.
Not this
Not recitat used as a first look (the ordinary active was already taught). Not a passive sentence such as “he is spoken.”
Connects to
Cōnārī, like posse and licet, is often followed by an infinitive: recitāre cōnātur, he tries to recite.

Capitulum XVI is called Tempestās, the storm. The book’s story stays in the book. The first new grammar is a kind of verb that looks passive and is not.

A deponent verb is translated as active, even though its ending looks like a passive ending. In this first look we use only the third person and the present infinitive. First- and second-person deponent forms are reserved.

Mathematics · Geometry XLIV: the volume of a right cylinder

45 min · Method: V = B × h with a circular base, so V = πr²h, using last week’s circle area

Learn

Key terms

volume of a right cylinder
Definition
A right cylinder has two parallel circular bases and a height perpendicular to those bases. Its volume is V = B × h, with B the area of a circle, already taught as πr². So V = πr²h, in cubic units.
Example
A classroom cylinder of radius 3 and height 10 has V = π × 3² × 10 = 90π cubic units.
Not this
Not last week’s circle area taken as a volume, and not yesterday’s triangular prism. Not a first look at π or at A = πr². Not the volume of a sphere, which is reserved. Not a drawing of a cylinder in perspective, already used in the atelier.
Connects to
The packing idea is the same as for a right prism: the base area, now a circle, times the perpendicular height. Exact answers stay in terms of π unless a labeled approximation is asked for.

Yesterday’s formula V = B × h still holds when the base is a circle. Last week’s area of a circle is B = πr². So the volume of a right cylinder is

V = πr²h.

For the classroom cylinder in the diagram, r = 3 and h = 10, so V = π × 9 × 10 = 90π cubic units. Leave exact answers in terms of π. A decimal using π ≈ 3.14 is only an estimate, and only if it is asked for.

The height h is perpendicular to the base. A slanted length along the side is not h. The radius is the radius of the base, not the height.

Remember

A right cylinder has V = πr²h, which is V = B × h with last week’s B = πr². Leave exact answers in terms of π. This is not a first look at circle area, and not the volume of a sphere.

Key terms: volume of a right cylinder.

  3. 90π is a volume. The base area 9π is an area. They are not interchangeable.

Writing & Rhetoric · A précis of Dewing I.1.50–53

40 min · Method: a précis, already taught, of Dewing, Buildings I.1.50–53. I.1.54–57 is reserved for tomorrow.

Learn

Key terms

précis of a set building extract
Definition
A précis of a set building extract, using the précis already taught, is a short restatement in your own words that keeps Dewing’s points, order, and proportion, without adding a new opinion and without simply quoting. Today’s extract is Buildings I.1.50–53.
Example
A sound précis of I.1.50–53 will still have Justinian, Anthemius, and Isidorus securing the church’s stability by “many skilful devices,” of which Dewing records only one for now: four-cornered courses of worked stone, held by lead poured into the joints, not by lime (“asbestus”) or by asphalt.
Not this
Not a first look at the précis (Day 15 / week 14), and not a quotation. Not a first look at Justinian, Anthemius, or Isidorus, already named in history. Not a modern engineering verdict, and not an invented measurement.
Connects to
History this week reads the same stretch. Writing’s job is the précis, not a new close reading of every Greek gloss.

A précis, already taught, is a short restatement in your own words. It keeps the author’s points, in their order and in fair proportion. It is not a quotation, not a loose summary that drops a main point, and not a reaction.

Today’s set extract is Dewing, Buildings I.1.50–53. The later gold ceiling and the two stoas (I.1.54–57) wait until tomorrow.

I.1.50: “It was by many skilful devices that the Emperor Justinian and the master-builder Anthemius and Isidorus secured the stability of the church, hanging, as it does, in mid-air. Some of these it is both hopeless for me to understand in their entirety, and impossible to explain in words; I shall record only one of them for the present, from which it should be possible to gain an impression of the strength of the whole work.”

I.1.51–52: the piers “are not constructed in the same way as other structures.” “The courses of stone were laid down so as to form a four-cornered shape, the stones being rough by nature but worked smooth; and they were cut to the angles when they were destined to form the projecting corners of the sides of the pier, but when they chanced to be assigned to a position between the angles, they were cut in rectangles.”

I.1.53: “These were held together neither by lime (titanos), which they call “asbestus”, nor by asphalt, the material which was the pride of Semiramis in Babylon, nor by any other such thing, but by lead (molibdos) poured into the interstices, which flowed about everywhere in the spaces between the stones and hardened in the joints, binding them to each other.”

Physics · From elastic potential energy to kinetic energy

40 min · Method: if friction is neglected, ½kx² = ½mv², from OpenStax 16.2 and 16.5, using the already-taught work–energy idea

Learn

Key terms

conversion of elastic potential energy
Definition
If friction and other non-conservative losses are neglected, the elastic potential energy stored in a Hooke’s-law spring can be converted into kinetic energy: ½kx² = ½mv². This is the already-taught conservation of mechanical energy, applied to PE_el.
Example
OpenStax Example 16.2 continues with a 2.00 g dart and the 0.563 J already found. Then ½mv² = 0.563 J, so v = 23.7 m/s. Those figures are OpenStax’s, not invented.
Not this
Not a first look at kinetic energy, work–energy, or conservation of mechanical energy (Days 21–28), and not a first look at PE_el = ½kx², taught Monday. Not a claim that friction is always negligible, and not last week’s period formula used as a speed.
Connects to
OpenStax 16.1 Example 16.2 already carries that conversion, neglecting friction and the mass of the spring. Section 16.5 is a related later discussion of energy in SHM, not the source of the 0.563 J and 23.7 m/s figures.

Monday stored energy in the spring: PEel = ½kx². Kinetic energy, ½mv², and the conservation of mechanical energy were already taught in Days 21–28. Today those are used together.

If friction is neglected, the energy stored in the spring can become kinetic energy of a dart or a mass:

½kx² = ½mv².

OpenStax Example 16.2, part of the same toy-gun account, uses m = 2.00 g = 0.00200 kg and PEel = 0.563 J:

½ (0.00200) v² = 0.563, so v² = 563, and v = 23.7 m/s.

A check: ½ × 0.00200 × (23.7)² ≈ 0.562 J, which agrees with 0.563 J within the book’s rounding. Those figures are OpenStax’s. Invented Together and practice masses are labeled invented.

The formula PE_el = ½kx² and Example 16.2 (toy gun: k = 50.0 N/m, x = 0.150 m, PE_el = 0.563 J; a 2.00 g projectile, friction and the mass of the spring neglected, v = 23.7 m/s) are in that section. History · Gold, the prevailing light, and the two stoas (Dewing I.1.54–57)

40 min · Method: a close reading of Dewing, Buildings I.1.54–57; no invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

light from the stones prevails (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.54, “The whole ceiling is overlaid with pure gold, which adds glory to the beauty, yet the light reflected from the stones prevails, shining out in rivalry with the gold.” The gold is present and adds glory; Dewing’s claim is that the stones’ light nevertheless prevails.
Example
A reading that says the gold outshines everything has reversed the rivalry. The gold is not denied; it does not, in this sentence, win the comparison.
Not this
Not a first look at the church’s light in earlier Dewing stretches, and not Monday’s lead joints as a first look. Not an invented brightness or an official modern reflectance.
Connects to
Dewing I.1.54, as in the source note.
two stoas (Dewing)
Definition
In Dewing I.1.55–57 there are “two stoa-like colonnades (stoai), one on each side,” not separated from the church, “making the effect of its width greater,” running its whole length, and lower than the interior. The assignment is Dewing’s. So is the claim that the two stoas are equal and similar. This lesson does not invent a width or a seating count.

Atelier · Careful halftones and edges, still monochrome

60 min · Method: one careful halftone or one edge relation, still inside the already-checked families, still monochrome

Learn

Key terms

careful halftone and edge
Definition
A careful halftone, at this stage, is a small monochrome value inside the light that states a turn without flattening the light. A careful edge is the way that turn meets another form or meets the terminator: lost or found, soft or firm, still without a new outline and still without full color.
Example
One honest lost edge where a light form turns away, or one found edge where a plane meets the light more firmly, is enough. Both must still belong to the families already checked.
Not this
Not a first look at modeling in the light, or at yesterday’s family check. Not a catalogue of every edge in the head, not a finish of the features, and not full color.
Connects to

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of traer

40 min · Method: the irregular preterite of traer (traj-), taught in English, contrasted with yesterday’s dij- but not as a first look at decir

Learn

Key terms

traje
Definition
Traje is the first-person singular preterite of traer, to bring. The stem is traj-: traje, trajiste, trajo, trajimos, trajisteis, trajeron. There is no i in trajeron (not trajieron), the same spelling caution as dijeron.
Example
Ayer traje el libro. — Yesterday I brought the book. (Invented.) Ellos trajeron las cartas. — They brought the letters. (Invented.)
Not this
Not a first look at dije, and not the present traigo. Not a spelling trajieron. Not last week’s pude or puse as first looks.
Connects to
This lesson teaches the forms fully. The school sense is a completed bringing on that occasion.

Traer, to bring, has an irregular preterite stem traj-:

Word How to say it Meaning
traje TRAH-heh I brought
trajiste trah-HEES-teh you (singular, informal) brought
trajo TRAH-hoh he, she, or you (formal) brought
trajimos trah-HEE-mohs we brought
trajisteis trah-HEES-tayss you (plural, informal, Spain) brought
trajeron trah-HEH-rohn they, or you (plural) brought

The third-person plural is trajeron, not trajieron, as dijeron is not dijieron. The stems themselves remain different: dij- for decir, traj- for traer.

Remember

Traer in the preterite uses traj-: traje, trajiste, trajo, trajimos, trajisteis, trajeron. There is no i in trajeron. The school sense is a completed bringing. Dije is a contrast, not a first look today.

Elective · Art history: Dewing’s lead joints, and gold against the light of the stones

40 min · Method: a rereading of Dewing I.1.50–57, already opened in history this week, for the joints and the gold-and-light rivalry. No invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

lead joints and prevailing light (Dewing)
Definition
This elective gathers two claims already read in history this week. The one device of I.1.50–53 binds four-cornered courses with lead poured into the joints, not with lime (“asbestus”) or asphalt. In I.1.54 the ceiling is overlaid with pure gold, “yet the light reflected from the stones prevails, shining out in rivalry with the gold.”
Example
The two claims belong to one stretch, but they are not the same claim: one is about how the piers were held; the other is about gold and light in the remaining portions of the church.
Not this
Not a first look at this week’s history extract, and not a first look at Justinian, Anthemius, or Isidorus. Not an invented strength of lead, and not a claim that the gold is denied.
Connects to
Dewing I.1.50–54, as in the source note. The two stoas of I.1.55–57 may be named as context; they are not a new first look here.

This elective rereads the same stretch as this week’s history, for the joints and the gold-and-light rivalry, without inventing a measurement.

History this week has already read I.1.50–57. This elective holds two of those claims side by side, without adding a measurement or a modern materials lecture.

The one recorded device: four-cornered courses of worked stone, held by lead poured into the interstices, not by lime (“asbestus”) or by asphalt.

Then, in the remaining portions: a ceiling overlaid with pure gold, “which adds glory to the beauty, yet the light reflected from the stones prevails.”

A near-miss is to treat the gold as if Dewing had said it outshone the stones, or to treat lead and gold as a single mixed method. They belong to two sentences in two parts of the account. Keep “skilful” and “asbestus” inside quotations when you quote.

Remember

Dewing’s one device binds the courses with lead, not lime or asphalt. The ceiling is gold, yet the light from the stones prevails. Both claims are already in this week’s history reading. No invented measurements.
