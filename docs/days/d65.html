Latin · Capitulum XI: Friday reading (neuters and AcI)

30 min · Text: an original Friday reading, using Cap. XI grammar · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • third-declension neuter — Day 61
  • accusative and infinitive (AcI) — Day 63

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today’s Latin is a short original reading that uses this week’s work: third-declension neuters, and a little AcI. It is not a page of the book, and it is not a story to copy from a grammar site.

Read it slowly, name the case of each body noun, and find the one accusative-and-infinitive.

Puer caput et pectus habet. Corpus sānum est. Puella avem volāre videt. Magister puellam cantāre dīcit. Putō crūs longum esse.

Remember

Third-declension neuters: nominative equals accusative in the singular, and those cases end in -a in the plural. After vidēre, dīcere, or putāre, an accusative plus an infinitive (including esse) names a second action.

Watch me

In the set reading, name the case and job of caput, then of corpus, then point to the AcI in Puella avem volāre videt.

  1. caput is accusative singular, the object of habet (same form as the nominative, because it is neuter).
  2. corpus is nominative singular, the subject of est.
  3. avem is the accusative subject of the infinitive volāre; videt is the finite verb. That is AcI.

Together

In Putō crūs longum esse, name the job of crūs, of longum, and of esse.

Hint 1: The construction

This is AcI with esse, after putō.

Hint 2: Agreement

crūs is the accusative subject; longum is an accusative predicate matching that neuter singular; esse is the infinitive.

Check

Use only this week’s grammar. Answers may be short.

  1. In Puer caput et pectus habet, why do caput and pectus not end in -em?
  2. Write Latin for “The girl thinks that the body is healthy.”
  3. In the set reading, which sentence is AcI with esse?
  4. Correct, from the reading’s grammar: *Magister puella cantat dīcit.

Exercises

  1. Write a new original sentence with a third-declension neuter as an object.
  2. Write a new original AcI after vidēre.
  3. Write a new original AcI with esse.

Mathematics · Friday mix: similar triangles

40 min · Method: a mix of this week’s similarity, not a new theorem

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • similar triangles — Day 61
  • AA similarity — Day 62
  • scale factor (similarity) — Day 63

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today is a short mix of this week’s similarity, not a new test. You may be asked to decide whether two triangles are similar, to name the scale factor, or to find a missing side or a height from a shadow. Keep corresponding parts together, and use AA when two pairs of angles are what you are given.

Congruence is still available when the sides are actually equal. If they are only proportional, the right word is similarity.

Remember

Similar triangles: corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides proportional. AA is enough to establish similarity. A scale factor from one corresponding pair finds a missing side. Congruence is same size as well as same shape.

Watch me

△ABC has angles 40° and 70°. △DEF has angles 70° and 70°. Are they similar? If not, say why; if they are, find the third angles and match the vertices.

  1. In △ABC the third angle is 70°. In △DEF the third angle is 40°. So both are 40-70-70 triangles.
  2. Yes, they are similar. Match 40° with 40° and each 70° with a 70°: △ABC ∼ △FDE, for example, if ∠A = 40°, ∠B = 70°, ∠C = 70°, ∠D = 70°, ∠E = 70°, and ∠F = 40°.
  3. The sides need not be equal; this is similarity, not automatically congruence.

Together

A 1.2 m stick casts a 1.8 m shadow. A wall in the same light casts a 6.0 m shadow. Find the height of the wall.

Hint 1: AA and a proportion

1.2/1.8 = h/6.0.

Hint 2: The height

h = 4.0 m.

Check

Use only this week’s methods. Show the proportion or the angle match.

  1. Sides 6, 8, 9 and 9, 12, 13.5: are the triangles similar? If so, give the scale factor from the first to the second.
  2. △PQR ∼ △XYZ, PQ = 10, XY = 4, QR = 15. Find YZ.
  3. △ABC has ∠A = 55° and ∠B = 55°. △DEF has ∠D = 70° and ∠E = 55°. Are they similar?
  4. A 4 m pole casts a 5 m shadow. A tree’s shadow is 15 m in the same light. Find the height of the tree.

Exercises

  1. Why is AA enough, without first measuring the sides?
  2. Sides 3, 5, 7 and 6, 10, 16: similar or not? Explain.
  3. The scale factor from △ABC to △DEF is 5/2, and AC = 6. Find DF.

Physics · Friday mix: torque, rotational equilibrium, and a lever

50 min · Method: a mix of this week’s rotation work, not a new law

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • rotational equilibrium — Day 61
  • fulcrum — Day 63
  • the lever rule — Day 63

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

A Friday mix of this week’s rotation work, not a new condition. You may be asked for a torque, for Στ = 0 about a named point, for a lever with a fulcrum, or for the support force that makes ΣF = 0. The numbers remain invented classroom figures. The unit of torque is still N·m, not the joule.

Remember

τ = F × d⊥, in N·m. Rotational equilibrium means Στ = 0 about the chosen point. A lever is that condition about a fulcrum; if the body is at rest, ΣF = 0 as well, and a force through the fulcrum gives no torque.

Watch me

A 2.00 m light rod is supported at its center. 18 N acts downward at the left end. Find the downward force at the right end for Στ = 0, and the support force at rest.

  1. Each arm is 1.00 m, so the right-hand force is 18 N downward.
  2. The support supplies 36 N upward.

Together

A light lever has a 50 N load, 0.40 m from the fulcrum. The effort is 1.00 m from the fulcrum, on the other side. Find the effort for Στ = 0.

Hint 1: The lever rule

50 N × 0.40 m = E × 1.00 m.

Hint 2: The effort

E = 20 N.

Check

Take torques about the named point. Use N·m, not joules.

  1. A 12 N force acts with d⊥ = 0.25 m. Find the torque, and name the unit.
  2. A 4.00 m light rod is supported at its center. 16 N downward at the left end: find the right-hand downward force for Στ = 0, and the support at rest.
  3. Why does a force through the fulcrum not appear in the lever rule, even when ΣF = 0 needs that force?
  4. Why is “equilibrium price” the wrong meaning of equilibrium in this lesson?

Exercises

  1. A 36 N load sits 0.25 m to the left of a fulcrum. Find the effort 0.75 m to the right for Στ = 0, and the support at rest.
  2. If 10 N acts downward 0.50 m to the left of a support, and 10 N downward 0.50 m to the right, is Στ = 0? Is ΣF = 0 without a support force?
  3. A student reports a torque of 4 J. What should be written instead, and why?

The method is the one already taught at the end of the first head weeks: name the largest strengths, the largest remaining errors of proportion or value, and what a later sitting would repair first. Remember

Critique in this order: set-up, tilt and center line, large proportions, planes and terminator, values as one light, then features. Name a strength, a remaining large error, and the first repair for a later sitting. The plate wins.

Watch me

Elective · Anatomy: the leg — femur, tibia, and fibula

30 min · Method: the three long bones of the leg, named and distinguished, without invented counts

Learn

Key terms

femur
Definition
The thigh bone: the long bone of the upper leg, between the hip and the knee. It is the single bone of the thigh.
Origin
From Latin femur, “thigh” (English Wiktionary, entry “femur”).
Example
In a simple standing diagram, the femur runs from the hip joint down to the knee, above the two bones of the lower leg.
Not this
Not the tibia or the fibula, which are the two bones of the lower leg, below the knee. Not a muscle, and not a joint.
Connects to
Earlier anatomy lessons treated the trunk, the arm, and the hand. Today’s elective is the leg, beginning with the three long bones.
tibia
Definition
The shinbone: the inner (medial) and usually the larger of the two long bones of the lower leg, between the knee and the ankle.
Origin
From Latin tībia, “shin bone, leg” (English Wiktionary, entry “tibia”).
Example
The tibia can be felt along the front of the shin. It carries more of the body’s weight than the fibula, which lies beside it.
Not this
Not the femur (the thigh bone, above the knee), and not the fibula (the thinner, outer bone of the same lower-leg pair).
Connects to
The lower leg has two long bones, the tibia and the fibula. The tibia is the larger, inner one.
fibula
Definition
The thinner, outer (lateral) of the two long bones of the lower leg, beside the tibia, between the knee and the ankle. The name comes from a clasp: the two bones together were compared to a brooch, with the fibula as the pin.
Origin
Borrowed from Latin fībula, “buckle, clasp, pin.” The bone is so named because the shape it makes with the tibia resembles a clasp, the fibula being the pin (English Wiktionary, entry “fibula”).
Example
The fibula lies along the outer side of the tibia. It is the slender companion of the shinbone, not the main weight-bearing bone of the pair.
Not this
Not the tibia, and not the femur. A common mix-up is to treat the lower leg as a single bone, as the thigh is; the lower leg has two.
Connects to
Tibia and fibula together make the lower-leg pair. The etymology (a clasp and its pin) is a memory aid, not a claim about how the bones look in every view.

Friday electives in odd-numbered weeks have been anatomy. Today is the leg: three long bones, named accurately, without invented muscle counts or a classroom diagnosis.

The femur is the thigh bone, the single long bone of the upper leg, from hip to knee. Below the knee the lower leg has two long bones. The tibia is the shinbone, the inner and usually the larger of the two, and the one you can feel along the front of the shin. The fibula is the thinner, outer bone, beside the tibia, down to the ankle. A common mix-up is to treat the lower leg as one bone, as the thigh is; it is two.

The word fibula also names an ancient clasp. Wiktionary records that the bone was so called because the pair resembled a clasp, with the fibula as the pin. That is a name, not a drawing you have to force onto every view of the leg.

Remember

The femur is the thigh bone (hip to knee). The tibia is the larger, inner bone of the lower leg (the shinbone). The fibula is the thinner, outer bone of that same pair. The lower leg has two long bones, not one. No invented muscle counts.

Key terms: femur, tibia, fibula.

Watch me

  1. Femur (thigh), then tibia and fibula (lower leg), down to the ankle.
  2. The lower leg, below the knee, has two long bones: tibia (inner, larger) and fibula (outer, thinner).

Hint 1: The thigh

Hint 2: The pair

Vocabulary review

15–20 min · Spaced review of weeks 1–13 terms · Use your own words; then check the key

Learn

This is not a new lesson. Retrieval — saying a term from memory, then checking — is how a word becomes usable, not merely familiar. Close the glossary and the day's cards first. For each item, explain or apply the term, or distinguish it from a near-miss.

Remember

A definition you can only recognize on the card is not yet yours. Give the meaning, a fresh example, and a near-miss.

Watch me

A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.

  1. Question: How is the triple point of water different from the ice point at one atmosphere, and why must 0.01 °C not be treated as a typo for 0 °C?
  2. The triple point is the unique condition where ice, liquid, and vapor coexist: 273.16 K, which is 0.01 °C. The ice point at 1 atm is the familiar about 0 °C, for ice and liquid at one atmosphere.
  3. They are different conditions, from the IAPWS FAQ, not two names for the same point. Treating 0.01 °C as a typo would blend them.
  4. A near-miss is to say “water freezes at the triple point, 0 °C, which is also 100 °C’s partner on ITS-90.” That mixes the ice point, the triple point, and the historical boiling point.

Together

In your own words, what is AA similarity, and how is it different from congruence?

Hint 1: The test

If two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, the third pair is equal as well (the angle sum is 180°), and the triangles are similar.

Hint 2: Congruence

Congruence is same shape and same size. AA gives similarity; the sides may still stand in a ratio other than 1.

  3. What is a third-declension neuter rule for the nominative and the accusative, and how is AcI different from posse + infinitive?
  4. What is rotational equilibrium, and why is a joule the wrong name for the unit of torque?
  5. Quote the first line of the set Longfellow extract, or give the first nine words of the set Bliss Lincoln extract. (Either set text is accepted.)
