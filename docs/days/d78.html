Latin · Capitulum XIV: mēcum and tēcum, and a dative of interest

30 min · Latin · personal pronouns with -cum, and a first look at the dative of interest. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

mēcum
Definition
Mēcum means with me. With the personal pronouns, cum is written after the pronoun and joined to it: mēcum, tēcum, sēcum, nōbīscum, vōbīscum, not cum mē.
Example
Venī mēcum. — Come with me.
Not this
Not cum mē as a standard form, and not a new preposition besides cum. The joining of cum to these pronouns is the point.
Connects to
Ordinary cum with a noun still stands before the noun (cum amīcō). With these pronouns it is enclitic: mēcum, tēcum.
dative of interest
Definition
The dative of interest (dativus commodi) names the person for whom something is so, or for whom it matters: Mihi necesse est labōrāre, it is necessary for me to work.
Example
Mihi necesse est legere. — It is necessary for me to read.
Not this
Not the dative of possession already met in Capitulum XII (the ‘is-to-me’ having), used as if it were a new first look at that earlier job. Today’s dative names the person for whom the fact holds.
Connects to
Capitulum XII taught the dative of possession. Today’s dative of interest is a different job for the same case: for whom it is necessary, or for whom it matters (compare Allen and Greenough on the dative of interest or reference).

With mē, tē, sē, nōbīs, vōbīs, the preposition cum is written after the pronoun and joined to it:

Word How to say it Meaning
mēcum MAY-koom with me
tēcum TAY-koom with you (sing.)
nōbīscum NO-bees-koom with us
mihi MI-hee to/for me (dative)

Venī mēcum. — Come with me. Tēcum labōrō. — I work with you. Not cum mē in standard use.

A dative of interest names the person for whom something holds:

Mihi necesse est legere. — It is necessary for me to read.

This is not a reteaching of the dative of possession (Capitulum XII) as a first look. The case is the same; the job today is “for whom.”

Original sentences only. Instruction stays in English.

Remember

Mēcum, tēcum, nōbīscum: cum is joined after the pronoun. Mihi necesse est… is a dative of interest, ‘for me,’ not a first look at the dative of possession.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXVI: the area of a trapezoid

60 min · Geometry

Learn

Key terms

trapezoid
Definition
In the American usage used on this site, a trapezoid is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides, called the bases. The height is the perpendicular distance between those bases.
Example
A quadrilateral with parallel sides 6 and 10, and perpendicular height 4, is a trapezoid of area ½(6 + 10) × 4 = 32.
Not this
Not a parallelogram (which has two pairs of parallel sides). Not the British use of ‘trapezium’ for this shape; this course says trapezoid.
Connects to
Yesterday a parallelogram (two pairs of parallel sides) had area b × h. A trapezoid has one pair of parallel sides; its area uses both bases.
area of a trapezoid
Definition
The area of a trapezoid is the average of the two parallel bases times the perpendicular height: A = ½(a + b) × h.
Example
Bases 6 and 10, height 4: A = ½(16) × 4 = 32.
Not this
Not the product of one base and a slanted side, and not ½bh using only one of the two bases.
Connects to
You can think of a trapezoid as a parallelogram made by placing a copy of the trapezoid so that the two non-parallel sides join; the combined base is a + b and the height is still h, so twice the trapezoid has area (a + b)h, and one trapezoid has half of that.

A trapezoid, in the American sense used here, has exactly one pair of parallel sides (the bases). The height is the perpendicular distance between those bases.

Its area is the average of the two bases times the height:

A = ½(a + b) × h.

Classroom lengths: bases 6 and 10, height 4. A = ½(6 + 10) × 4 = ½ × 16 × 4 = 32.

Why the average? Place a second copy of the same trapezoid so the two non-parallel sides join; the two copies make a parallelogram of base a + b and height h, area (a + b)h. One trapezoid is half of that, ½(a + b)h.

If the two bases were equal, the shape would be a parallelogram, not a trapezoid in this course’s usage, and the formula would reduce to b × h, which matches Monday.

Remember

A trapezoid has exactly one pair of parallel sides (the bases). A = ½(a + b)h, with h perpendicular to the bases. A slanted side is not the height.

Key terms: trapezoid, area of a trapezoid.

Writing & Rhetoric · A multi-paragraph outline, for an analytical essay

45 min · Writing · a first look at a multi-paragraph outline; the analytical essay itself is not a first look

Learn

Key terms

multi-paragraph outline
Definition
A multi-paragraph outline plans an analytical essay before the draft: a thesis, then a short plan for each paragraph (a claim and the grounds or quotations it will use), written in order. It extends the single-paragraph outline already taught on Day 6.
Example
Thesis: Virgil’s offer to be a guide is a rescue, not a sightseeing plan. Paragraph 1: the dark wood and the beast (grounds from Inferno I). Paragraph 2: ‘I will be thy guide’ as the turning offer. A short other-side note: the speech also warns of pain ahead.
Not this
Not a first look at the analytical essay, the thesis, or the single-paragraph outline, which were taught on Days 6 and 9. Not a draft of full paragraphs. Not a précis (week 14), which shortens a source rather than planning an argument.
Connects to
Day 9’s analytical essay asked for a thesis, introduced quotations, and an other side. Week 13 added Toulmin grounds; last week and this week added fallacies to avoid. Today the new tool is a plan that stretches the Day 6 outline across more than one paragraph.

The analytical essay (Day 9) is not a first look today. What is new is a multi-paragraph outline: a plan, written before the draft, that extends the single-paragraph outline (Day 6) across the whole short essay.

The outline has:

  1. A thesis — one sentence that can be argued against (Day 9).
  2. A plan for each paragraph: a claim (as a topic sentence would), and the grounds or short quotations it will use.
  3. A note on the other side, or on a limit of the claim, so the essay does not pretend there is nothing to answer.

Use a public-domain line already in this course, not a new unsourced story. A fair model, using Longfellow’s Inferno I (already read in weeks 13–15):

  • Thesis: Virgil’s offer to be a guide is a rescue from the beast, not a casual walk.
  • Paragraph 1: the speaker has turned back from the beast (grounds: the speech just before the Greyhound passage, or the earlier turn).
  • Paragraph 2: the offer itself, ‘I will be thy guide’ (lines 112–114, already taught).
  • Other side: the same speech also promises ‘desperate lamentations,’ so the rescue is not a promise of ease.

Do not support the thesis with circular reasoning or with a post hoc jump. Do not draft the full essay yet; tomorrow writes from the outline.

Remember

A multi-paragraph outline plans a thesis, a claim and grounds for each paragraph, and a note on the other side, before the draft. The analytical essay and the single-paragraph outline are already taught. Avoid last week’s and this week’s fallacies.

Physics · The simple pendulum, for a small swing

45 min · Physics · the formula from OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.4

Learn

Key terms

simple pendulum
Definition
A simple pendulum, in the school model used here, is a bob on a light string or rod of length L that swings through a small angle. For amplitudes less than about 15°, OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.4, gives the period T = 2π√(L/g). The period depends on the length and on g, and is independent of mass.
Example
If L = 1.00 m and g is the already-taught Earth figure, about 9.8 m/s², then T = 2π√(1/9.8), which is about 2.0 s. That ‘about’ is a calculation from those two figures, not a new official timing from a national laboratory.
Not this
Not a heavy rod whose mass cannot be ignored, and not a large swing, for which OpenStax limits the simple formula to amplitudes less than about 15°. Not Monday’s general oscillation treated as if the formula had already been used.
Connects to
Monday defined period and frequency. Today those words are applied to a simple pendulum, with the OpenStax formula and the already-taught g of about 9.8 m/s² on Earth.

Use the already-taught Earth figure g ≈ 9.8 m/s², not a new official measurement. For L = 1.00 m, T = 2π√(1/9.8). √(1/9.8) is √0.1020…, about 0.319, and 2π times that is about 2.0 s. That is a calculation from the formula and from g, not a certificate that a particular classroom bob was timed at exactly 2 s.

If you double the length, T becomes √2 times as long, because T goes with the square root of L. Changing the mass of the bob, in this model, does not change T.

Classroom lengths other than this 1.00 m example are invented and labeled invented unless you actually measure a string and say so. Never invent an official g to more digits than this course has already taught.

History · A structure that seems to float, in Dewing I.1.33–34

45 min · History · Buildings I.1.33–34; no invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

seeming to float (Dewing I.1.34)
Definition
In Dewing’s translation of Buildings I.1.33–34, the upper part of the east structure ends in “the fourth part of a sphere,” and above it a crescent-shaped structure rises, “marvellous in its grace, but by reason of the seeming insecurity of its composition altogether terrifying.” It “seems somehow to float in the air on no firm basis,” yet “actually it is braced with exceptional firmness and security.”
Example
Dewing’s contrast is between how the structure seems (unsecured, terrifying) and how it is (firmly braced). The lesson does not invent a measurement of the span or of the crescent.
Not this
Not a claim that the masonry has no supports, and not last week’s light-in-the-shrine wording treated as this floating-and-braced contrast. “Marvellous” is Dewing’s spelling, kept inside the quotation.
Connects to
Monday’s half-cylinder is the lower east structure. Today’s sentences sit on top of it: a quarter-sphere, then a crescent-shaped part that seems to float and is, Dewing says, actually secure.

Monday’s extract ended with the half-cylinder “rising precipitously to a height.” Dewing continues, I.1.33–34:

The upper part of this structure ends in the fourth part of a sphere, and above it another crescent-shaped structure rises, fitted to the adjoining parts of the building, marvellous in its grace, but by reason of the seeming insecurity of its composition altogether terrifying. For it seems somehow to float in the air on no firm basis, but to be poised aloft to the peril of those inside it. Yet actually it is braced with exceptional firmness and security.

Two jobs are being contrasted: how it seems (to float, with no firm basis, to the peril of those inside), and how it is (braced with exceptional firmness and security). “Marvellous” is Dewing’s spelling and stays inside the quotation; a paraphrase may say marvelous.

“The fourth part of a sphere” is a quarter-sphere, in this wording. This lesson does not invent a radius, a span, or a height in meters. A classroom sketch, if used, is a sketch, not a plan of the church.

Atelier · A value map of the whole head, still in one light

90 min · Atelier · unify the families before a grisaille; still drawing

Learn

Key terms

value map of the head
Definition
A value map of the head is a slow, whole-head statement of the light family and the shadow family, in one held light, without adding a new medium and without going back to a feature-by-feature pass as if the families had not been grouped.
Example
Stand back, see whether the forehead, the cheek in light, and the jaw in shadow still read as one head in one light, and adjust a slipped family before adding detail.
Not this
Not a first look at a value pass on a single feature (week 15), and not yet grisaille in paint (tomorrow). Not a second lamp, and not a finished portrait.
Connects to
Monday and Tuesday grouped the families and checked the terminator. Today those checks are unified on the whole head, so tomorrow’s grisaille, if you begin it, has a map to follow rather than a set of separate features.

The last two sittings grouped the light family and the shadow family and checked the terminator. Today you make a value map of the whole head: one slow statement of those families, still in drawing, still in one held light.

Stand back. This is not a new medium. Do not rush into paint in order to look as if a new medium had started. The map is the work.

choosing fui, tuve, estuve, or hice
Definition
Fui is the preterite of ser or ir; tuve is tener; estuve is estar; hice is hacer (with z in hizo). They are different verbs, already taught, and they are not interchangeable because they happen to be irregular.
Example
Fui a la tienda (ir); tuve tiempo (tener); estuve en casa (estar); hice la tarea (hacer). Invented drills, labeled invented.
Not this
Not a first look at any of these four sets. Not a new irregular verb. Not the regular -ar / -er / -ir preterites treated as if they had not been taught.
Connects to
Last week contrasted ser, ir, and hacer. This week added tener, estar, and andar. Today the job is to choose the right one of the irregulars already in hand.

No new verb is begun. The job is to choose among irregular preterites already taught:

  • fui, fuiste, fue… — ser or ir
  • tuve… — tener
  • estuve… — estar
  • anduve… — andar
  • hice, hiciste, hizo… — hacer

Invented contrast drills, labeled invented:

  • Ayer fui a la tienda. (ir)
  • Ayer tuve tiempo. (tener)
  • Ayer estuve en casa. (estar)
  • Ayer hice la tarea. (hacer)

Fue and estuvo are not the same sentence. Fui and tuve are not the same verb. Instruction stays in English. Regular preterites (hablé, comí, viví) may be reused as a recap, not a first look.

Remember

Choose among fui (ser/ir), tuve (tener), estuve (estar), anduve (andar), and hice (hacer). They are not interchangeable. No new verb is begun.

Elective · Art history: columns in a choral dance, from Dewing I.1.35

30 min · Art history · Buildings I.1.35, still without invented measurements

Learn

Key terms

columns in a choral dance (Dewing I.1.35)
Definition
In Dewing’s translation of Buildings I.1.35, the columns on either side of the east structure “do not stand in a straight line, but they retreat inward in the pattern of the semicircle, as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance,” and above them hangs a crescent-shaped structure.
Example
Dewing’s simile is a choral dance: the columns recede in a semicircle, not in a straight row. The lesson does not invent a number of columns or a measurement of the semicircle.
Not this
Not a first look at the half-cylinder or at the seeming-to-float crescent, which today’s history block (or Monday’s and Wednesday’s history) already teaches from I.1.31–34. Today’s new extract is the columns of I.1.35 and Dewing’s dance simile.
Connects to
History this week reads the east end in Dewing. This art-history block stays with the same source and isolates the columns’ arrangement and the simile, still without inventing a plan of the church.

Dewing, Buildings I.1.35, after the crescent that seems to float, continues:

On either side of this are columns arranged on the pavement; these likewise do not stand in a straight line, but they retreat inward in the pattern of the semicircle as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance, and above them hangs a structure of crescent shape.

Two facts, and one simile:

  • The columns are not in a straight line.
  • They retreat inward in a semicircle.
  • Dewing’s comparison: “as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance.”

A classroom sketch of a semicircle of columns may help if it is labeled as a sketch, not as a surveyed count or a measured radius. This lesson does not invent how many columns there were, or a length in meters.

The crescent above them is the same kind of crescent-shaped structure already met in I.1.33–34; it is not a new official measurement today.

The 1940 Dewing translation, as used all this week, is public domain in the United States. “Marvellous,” if you quote I.1.33, stays in Dewing’s spelling.

Remember

In Dewing I.1.35, the columns recede in a semicircle, “as if they were yielding to one another in a choral dance,” with a crescent-shaped structure above. They are not a straight row. No official count or measurement is invented.
