Latin · A negative result with ut nōn, a first look

40 min · Method: ut nōn + the already-taught present subjunctive; contrasted with nē and with a positive result; original sentences

Learn

Key terms

a negative result with ut nōn, a first look
Definition
A negative result, in this first look, is marked by ut nōn and the already-taught present subjunctive, not by nē. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur may be glossed she comes so slowly that she does not follow him. Nē still marks a negative purpose, as in last week’s Puella manet nē eum sequātur, she stays in order that she not follow him.
Example
In an original sentence, Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur: she comes so slowly that she does not follow him. The signal is tam; the negative in the result clause is nōn, not nē. The sentence is original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg.
Not this
Not nē eum sequātur, last week’s negative purpose. Not a positive result with ut and no nōn. Not sequētur, the future. Not ut nōn used as if it were a purpose clause.
Connects to
Yesterday’s positive result with ut, and last week’s purpose clauses with ut and with nē. Ēgredior in a result clause waits until tomorrow.

Yesterday’s result clause was positive: Puella tam celeriter venit ut eum sequātur, she comes so quickly that she follows him. A negative result does not use nē. It uses ut nōn and the same present subjunctive. Puella tam lentē venit ut eum nōn sequātur: she comes so slowly that she does not follow him. Last week’s nē still marks a negative purpose: Puella manet nē eum sequātur, she stays in order that she not follow him. Keep the two jobs distinct. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the English gloss.

Mathematics · A function not continuous at a point where it is not defined

40 min · Method: if f is not defined at a, f is not continuous at a, even when the limit exists; week 32’s invented hole at x = 2; a formal epsilon argument reserved

Learn

Key terms

a function not continuous at a point where it is not defined
Definition
If a function is not defined at a, it is not continuous at a, even when the limit as x approaches a exists. Week 32’s invented function f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not defined at 2, and so is not continuous at 2, even though lim x→2 (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = 4 was already found by factorization. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved.
Example
f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not defined at 2, because the denominator is 0 there. The limit as x approaches 2 is 4, already taught. Continuity at 2 would also require f(2) to be defined and equal to 4. Since f(2) is not defined, f is not continuous at 2.
Not this
Not a claim that the limit fails to exist at 2. The limit is 4. Not a claim that a missing value may be ignored when we ask about continuity. Not a formal epsilon argument, which remains reserved.
Connects to
Yesterday’s definition of continuity at a point, and week 32’s limit where the function is not defined at the point. Filling the hole by defining a new function at 2 remains reserved.

Yesterday’s first look said that f is continuous at a only when f is defined at a and lim x→a f(x) = f(a). Both conditions are required. Week 32 taught an invented function that is not defined at the point in question: f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2). At x = 2 the denominator is 0, so f(2) is not defined. The limit, found by factorization, is 4: (x − 2)(x + 2)/(x − 2) = x + 2 for x ≠ 2, and that approaches 4. The limit exists, but f is still not continuous at 2, because f is not defined there. The same holds for invented g(x) = (x² − 9)/(x − 3) at 3: the limit is 6, already practiced in week 32, but g(3) is not defined, so g is not continuous at 3. Filling a hole by defining a new function at the missing point remains reserved. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. The functions are invented classroom examples.

Remember

If f is not defined at a, f is not continuous at a, even when lim x→a f(x) exists. Week 32’s invented f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2) has limit 4 at 2, but is not continuous at 2.

Watch me

  2. p(x) = x + 2 is defined at 2, p(2) = 4, and lim x→2 (x + 2) = 4, so p is continuous at 2.
  3. The two functions agree for every x other than 2. Continuity at 2 still requires a defined value there.

Learn

Key terms

official Example 20.9, peak voltage and peak power for AC
Definition
OpenStax official Example 20.9 asks for the peak voltage for 120-V AC power, and for the peak power consumption rate of a 60.0-W AC light bulb. Official household V_rms is 120 V, already taught last week. The book’s relation is V_rms = V₀/√2, so V₀ = √2 V_rms. The official arithmetic is V₀ = 1.414 × 120 V = 170 V. The official peak power is P₀ = 2 × 60.0 W = 120 W. Those figures are official book figures. The HTML of the book may drop the radical and look like V_rms = V₀/2; that dropped radical is not to be taught. The relation is V₀ = √2 V_rms, and the official factor is 1.414.
Example
V₀ = 1.414 × 120 V = 170 V, official. P₀ = 2 × 60.0 W = 120 W, official. The AC voltage swings from 170 V to −170 V, in the book’s wording, and the power averages 60 W.
Not this
