Latin · Capitulum XIV: rēs, nihil, and inquit

30 min · Latin · rēs as a fifth-declension noun, nihil, and inquit. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

rēs
Definition
Rēs, reī is a fifth-declension feminine noun meaning a thing, a matter, or an affair. Nominative and accusative plural are rēs; genitive plural is rērum.
Example
Rēs magna est. — It is a great matter (or: a great thing).
Not this
Not the Res Gestae of Day 33, which is a specific first-person record of deeds, used as if that title were today’s grammar lesson. Today’s rēs is the ordinary noun.
Connects to
Fifth-declension patterns were listed among Capitulum XIII’s grammar in the course plan; today rēs is taught as a working noun in original sentences (compare Allen and Greenough on the fifth declension).
inquit
Definition
Inquit means says or said, and it is typically inserted inside direct speech, rather than heading the sentence as a plain dīcit might.
Example
‘Venī mēcum,’ inquit. — ‘Come with me,’ he (or she) says.
Not this
Not every verb of speaking, and not a participle. Inquit is the inserted speech-verb.
Connects to
Yesterday’s mēcum can sit inside the quoted speech; inquit stands beside the quote, not in place of it.

Rēs, reī (feminine, fifth declension) is a thing, a matter, or an affair.

Mathematics · Geometry XXXVII: a regular polygon’s area, and the apothem

60 min · Geometry

Learn

Key terms

apothem
Definition
The apothem of a regular polygon is the perpendicular from the center to the middle of a side. It is the height of each of the congruent isosceles triangles that fill the polygon.
Example
In a regular hexagon, the apothem is the perpendicular from the center to a side, and it is also the height of one of the six equilateral triangles.
Not this
Not a side, not a radius to a vertex unless those two lengths happen to be compared later, and not a first look at the name ‘regular polygon’ (that name, and the interior angle, were taught on Day 37). Today’s new job is the apothem and the area.
Connects to
Day 37 defined a regular polygon. Last week an isosceles triangle’s altitude met the base at its midpoint. The apothem is that kind of perpendicular, drawn from the center to a side.
area of a regular polygon
Definition
A regular polygon divides into n congruent isosceles triangles from the center. Its area is n times the area of one of those triangles, or ½ × (the perimeter) × (the apothem).
Example
A regular polygon with perimeter 48 and apothem 7.2 (invented classroom measurements, labeled invented) has area ½ × 48 × 7.2 = 172.8.
Not this
Not the area of a circle, which is reserved for a later week, and not an interior-angle calculation (Day 37) presented as if it were an area.
Connects to
Each isosceles triangle has area ½ × side × apothem, so n of them give ½ × (n × side) × apothem, and n × side is the perimeter.

A regular polygon is already known from Day 37: all sides equal, all interior angles equal. Today’s job is its area, not a second first look at the name.

From the center, the polygon divides into n congruent isosceles triangles (n is the number of sides). The perpendicular from the center to a side is the apothem. It is the height of each of those triangles (the altitude meets the side at its midpoint, as in last week’s isosceles work).

Area of one triangle: ½ × (one side) × (apothem). All n of them: A = ½ × (perimeter) × (apothem).

Writing & Rhetoric · From the outline, one paragraph of the essay

45 min · Writing · draft one paragraph from yesterday’s outline, not a new kind of essay

Learn

Key terms

paragraph from the outline
Definition
A paragraph written from a multi-paragraph outline opens with the planned claim, introduces and explains a short quotation or ground, and stays inside that paragraph’s job. It does not swallow the whole thesis in one paragraph, and it does not support the claim with a fallacy already taught.
Example
A model opening: ‘Virgil’s offer is a rescue because the speaker has already turned back from the beast.’ Then a short, introduced quotation, then a sentence that explains it, without a circle or a post hoc jump.
Not this
Not a new first look at a topic sentence or at introducing a quotation (Days 6 and 9). Not the whole essay in one sitting. Not a précis of the canto.
Connects to
Yesterday’s outline is the plan. Today one paragraph is written from it, with Day 9’s habit of introducing a quotation, and with this week’s fallacies used as a check.

Yesterday you wrote a multi-paragraph outline. Today you write one paragraph from it — usually the first body paragraph — not the whole essay.

The paragraph should:

  1. Open with the claim you planned for that paragraph (a topic sentence).
  2. Introduce a short quotation or a ground, then explain it (Day 9). Use Longfellow’s wording exactly if you quote, including ‘I will be thy guide’ or another already-taught line.
  3. Stay inside that paragraph’s job. Do not try to prove the whole thesis in these five or six sentences.
  4. Check the draft against this week’s fallacies: no circular restatement, no post hoc jump, and no straw man, false dilemma, or ad hominem from last week.

A model shape (you may write a better paragraph of your own):

Virgil’s offer is a rescue because the speaker has already been turned back by the beast. In Longfellow’s translation, the speaker has asked for protection, and Virgil answers by offering to ‘be thy guide’ and to lead him ‘hence through the eternal place,’ quoted as in the 1867 text. The offer comes after that turning back, but the cause is not the mere order of the lines; the speech itself names a road out.

(Quote accurately; do not invent a Longfellow wording. If you are unsure of a line, look back at Days 72 and 74 rather than guessing.)

Science · Water: solubility, at a specified temperature

45 min · Science · solubility from IUPAC Gold Book S05740; no official table is invented

Learn

Key terms

solubility
Definition
In the IUPAC Gold Book (S05740), the analytical composition of a saturated solution, expressed as the proportion of a designated solute in a designated solvent, is the solubility of that solute. It may be expressed as a concentration, a molality, a mole fraction, or another composition measure.
Example
The solubility of a given solute in a given solvent is the composition of the saturated solution of that pair, at the specified conditions. This lesson does not invent an official percentage or a table of numbers.
Not this
Not a synonym for ‘saturated,’ and not last week’s qualitative ‘concentrated.’ Solubility is the composition of the saturated solution, as a quantity, not the leftover solid itself.
Connects to
Yesterday’s saturated solution is the solution at that equilibrium. Today names the composition of that solution as solubility, from S05740. Temperature still has to be specified, as in S05471.

Yesterday a saturated solution was defined at a specified temperature and pressure. Today names the composition of that solution.

The IUPAC Gold Book (S05740) says: the analytical composition of a saturated solution, expressed as the proportion of a designated solute in a designated solvent, is the solubility of that solute. It may be written as a concentration, a molality, a mole fraction, or another composition unit.

In school language: solubility is how much solute is in the saturated solution, for that solute and that solvent, at the stated conditions. It is a quantity, not a second word for leftover solid.

If the temperature changes, the solubility may change. That is why the definition sits on a specified temperature (and pressure). This lesson does not invent an official percentage, a textbook table, or a molarity. If a later source gives a tabulated solubility, it will be cited then.

A classroom statement such as ‘this sugar-water looks stronger when warm’ is an observation, not an official number, and must be labeled as a classroom observation if you use it.

Definitions of a saturated solution and of solubility follow the IUPAC Gold Book: a saturated solution is a solution which has the same concentration of a solute as one that is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at specified values of the temperature and pressure (Gold Book S05471, https://doi.org/10.1351/goldbook.S05471, https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05471.html); solubility is the analytical composition of a saturated solution, expressed as the proportion of a designated solute in a designated solvent (Gold Book S05740, https://doi.org/10.1351/goldbook.S05740, https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05740.html). No official percentage, solubility table, or molarity figure is invented this week. Classroom dishes and leftover solid, if used as a demonstration, are labeled invented.

Remember

Solubility, IUPAC Gold Book S05740, is the composition of a saturated solution, as a proportion of a designated solute in a designated solvent. It may change with temperature. No official percentage or table is invented this week.

Literature · Inferno II, lines 1–42: day departing, and a will that unwills

45 min · Literature · Canto II begins; the later speech of the saintly Lady is reserved

Learn

Key terms

a will that unwills (Inferno II)
Definition
In Longfellow’s Inferno II, lines 37–42, the speaker compares himself to one “who unwills what he willed, / And by new thoughts doth his intention change, / So that from his design he quite withdraws.” On “that dark hillside,” thinking “consumed the emprise, / Which was so very prompt in the beginning.”
Example
The speaker has already said he is not Æneas and not Paul (II.31–32), and he fears the journey may be “ill-advised” (II.34–35). The simile of un-willing what one willed follows that fear.
Not this
Not a first look at Canto I’s dark wood or at Virgil’s offer to be a guide, already taught. Not the later speech of the “fair, saintly Lady” (from II.52), which is reserved for a later sitting. Not an identification of “the Chosen Vessel” beyond the canto’s own contrast with Paul in these lines.
Connects to
Canto I ended with “Then he moved on, and I behind him followed.” Canto II opens at nightfall, invokes the Muses, and then lets the speaker’s courage fail before the arduous pass. Today stops at line 42.

Canto I ended with the speaker following Virgil. Canto II opens, in Longfellow:

Day was departing, and the embrowned air
Released the animals that are on earth
From their fatigues; and I the only one
Made myself ready to sustain the war,
Both of the way and likewise of the woe,
Which memory that errs not shall retrace.

He calls on the Muses and on memory (II.7–9), then asks Virgil to “Regard my manhood, if it be sufficient” (II.10–12), before the “arduous pass.”

He contrasts himself with “Silvius the parent” (Aeneas, II.13–15) and with “the Chosen Vessel” (II.28–30), and then says, in lines 31–36:

But I, why thither come, or who concedes it?
I not Æneas am, I am not Paul,
Nor I, nor others, think me worthy of it.
Therefore, if I resign myself to come,
I fear the coming may be ill-advised;
Thou’rt wise, and knowest better than I speak.

The simile that closes today’s extract (37–42):

And as he is, who unwills what he willed,
And by new thoughts doth his intention change,
So that from his design he quite withdraws,
Such I became, upon that dark hillside,
Because, in thinking, I consumed the emprise,
Which was so very prompt in the beginning.

“Æneas” and “honored,” when they appear in this translation, are quoted as in the 1867 text. The fair, saintly Lady of II.52 and the speech that follows are reserved. “The Chosen Vessel” is read as the canto’s own contrast with Paul, not as a new historical essay.

Quotations from Dante, Inferno, follow Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s 1867 translation, as posted at Wikisource, Divine Comedy (Longfellow 1867), Volume 1: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_1 and https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy_(Longfellow_1867)/Volume_1/Canto_2 (page text from the linked scan pages). Spelling and punctuation, including “Greyhound,” “Feltro,” “behoves,” “Æneas,” and “honored,” are quoted as in that text. Longfellow’s English is unrhymed and is not treated as keeping Dante’s terza rima. Lines 1–90 and 112–136 of Canto I were set in weeks 13–15. Today’s new lines are taught as they stand, not from memory.

Atelier · Grisaille: a first look, as monochrome value

90 min · Atelier · grisaille begins; a rehearsal in drawing is enough if paint is not ready

Learn

Key terms

grisaille
Definition
Grisaille is monochrome value painting: light and shadow stated in one hue, or in gray, rather than in full color. In this course it is the Term 2 step from a drawn value map toward paint, and it may be begun in oil or acrylic if those materials are ready, or rehearsed in a monochrome drawing if they are not.
Example
A head stated only in a range of gray, or in one earth color plus white and a dark, so that the light family and the shadow family remain distinct, is a grisaille (or a grisaille rehearsal, if it is still in graphite or charcoal).
Not this
Not a full-color portrait, and not a first look at the light family, the core shadow, or the value map, which this week and earlier weeks already taught. Not a requirement that oil be used today if it is not in the room.
Connects to
Term 2’s atelier plan names grisaille after the full-head sitting. Yesterday’s value map is the plan the grisaille, or the rehearsal, should follow. Color is reserved for Term 3.

Grisaille is monochrome value painting: the form stated in gray, or in one hue, not in full color. It is a Term 2 step, laid out in the scope, from the drawn head toward paint.

If oil or acrylic (and a simple gray or earth-color range) is ready, you may begin a grisaille of the head, following yesterday’s value map: light family and shadow family still distinct, terminator still a changing edge, one light still held. Keep the range limited. Do not start Term 3 color today.

If paint is not ready, a grisaille rehearsal in graphite or charcoal is the honest sitting: the same monochrome job, still a map of value, clearly named as a rehearsal rather than as a claim that paint was used.

In either case, do not average the families, do not move the lamp, and do not invent a living, named sitter. The site holds no identifying personal information.

Remember

Grisaille is monochrome value painting, in gray or in one hue, following the value map. Paint may be begun if it is ready; otherwise a rehearsal in drawing is enough and is named as a rehearsal. Full color is reserved. One light, held.

Elective · Economics: finding the principal from the amount

30 min · Economics · A = P + I, solved for P; invented rates labeled

Learn

Key terms

principal from the amount
Definition
If the amount after simple interest is A = P + I, and I = P × r × t, then A = P(1 + r t), so the principal is P = A / (1 + r t). The rate r is a fraction or a decimal for the time unit used in t.
Example
Invented (labeled invented): A is 1,200, r is 0.05 per year, t is 4 years. Then 1 + r t = 1.20, and P = 1,200 / 1.20 = 1,000.
Not this
Not a first look at simple interest or at A = P + I, which were taught in weeks 14–15. Not compound interest, which is not begun today. Not an official bank rate.
Connects to
Last week taught the amount A = P + I after simple interest. Today that same relation is solved for P when A, r, and t are known. Classroom rates and amounts are invented and labeled invented.

Last week the amount after simple interest was A = P + I, with I = P × r × t. Those are not first looks today.

If I is P × r × t, then

A = P + P r t = P(1 + r t),

and the principal is

P = A / (1 + r t).

Invented classroom figures, labeled invented: A = 1,200, r = 0.05 per year, t = 4 years.

1 + r t = 1 + 0.05 × 4 = 1.20.
P = 1,200 / 1.20 = 1,000.
Check: I = 1,000 × 0.05 × 4 = 200, and A = 1,000 + 200 = 1,200.

The 5% and the 1,200 are not an official bank offer. Compound interest is not begun this week.

Remember

From A = P(1 + r t), the principal is P = A / (1 + r t). Check by computing I and then A. Rates and amounts in the exercises are invented and labeled invented. Compound interest is reserved.
