Latin · Capitulum V, part 1: the ablative, and in, ex, ab

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Lingua Latina per se Illustrata, Pars I: Familia Romana, Cap. V · Method: the natural method

Learn

Key terms

ablative
Definition
The case used in this chapter after the prepositions in (when it means ‘in’ or ‘on’), ex (‘out of’), and ab (‘away from’), and later also after cum and sine. In the first and second declensions its singular ends in a long vowel (−ō or −ā) and its plural ends in −īs.
Origin
From Middle English, from Old French ablatif, from Latin ablātīvus (‘expressing removal’), from ablātus (‘taken away’), from auferre (‘to take away’).
Example
in hortō (in the garden), ex vīllā (out of the country house), ab oppidō (away from the town).
Not this
The ablative is not the accusative: in hortum would be a different pattern (motion into), which this chapter does not yet use with in. Do not drop the macron in writing: rosā (ablative) is not rosa (nominative).
Connects to
Days 1–4 gave nominative, genitive, accusative, vocative. The ablative is a new case, learned here with a closed list of prepositions.
preposition (with the ablative)
Definition
A small word placed before a noun to show a relation such as place or accompaniment. In Capitulum V the prepositions in, ex, ab, cum and sine are used with the ablative.
Origin
From Middle English preposicioun, from Old French, from Latin praepositiō, from praepōnere (‘to place before’). So called because it is placed before the word it is used with.
Example
in ātriō (in the atrium); ex hortō (out of the garden); ab ōstiō (from the door).
Not this
The case is chosen by the preposition, not by a guess from English ‘in’ or ‘from’. Today only in, ex and ab are practiced; cum and sine follow tomorrow.
Connects to
The natural method still holds: read the Latin sentence first, then name the case you have just seen.

Read the opening of Capitulum V in the book (the plan of the villa and garden). Do not copy the book's sentences here; notice the new endings, then practice on original sentences.

The new case. The ablative singular of the second declension ends in −ō (hortō, ātriō, cubiculō); of the first, in −ā (vīllā). The plural, for all three genders, ends in −īs (hortīs, vīllīs, cubiculīs).

Three prepositions today. in + ablative = in/on (place where); ex + ablative = out of; ab + ablative = away from. The book uses the forms ex and ab (not yet ē and ā).

Original sentences, not from the book: Mārcus est in hortō. Iūlia est in vīllā. Puer ex hortō venit.

Remember

Ablative singular: −ō (2nd declension), −ā (1st). Plural: −īs. in, ex, ab take the ablative in this chapter. in hortō = in the garden; ex vīllā = out of the country house.

Key terms: ablative, preposition (with the ablative).

Mathematics · Geometry V: constructions with compass and straightedge

60 min · Method: Kiselev, Planimetry, constructions; Singapore pictorial habit (draw the figure first)

Learn

Key terms

construction
Definition
A drawing made with only an unmarked straightedge and a compass, justified by axioms and earlier theorems, not by measuring with a ruler.
Origin
From Middle English construccioun, from Old French, from Latin cōnstructiō, from cōnstruere (‘to pile up, to build’). In English, construct + -ion.
Example
To copy segment AB, open the compass from A to B and swing that same opening from a new point A′.
Not this
A construction is not a freehand sketch, and it is not a measurement taken off a marked ruler. The compass opening is a transferred length, not a number of centimeters.
Connects to
Each construction will later be used as a step inside a proof. Day 8's locus is the reason the perpendicular bisector works.
perpendicular bisector
Definition
The line that cuts a segment in half and meets it at a right angle. It is the locus of points equidistant from the two endpoints.
Origin
Perpendicular is from Middle French perpendiculaire, from Latin perpendiculum (‘plumb line’). Bisector is from bisect + -or.
Example
Every point P on the perpendicular bisector of AB satisfies PA = PB. The midpoint of AB lies on it.
Not this
It is not merely ‘a line through the midpoint’ (that line might not be perpendicular), and it is not a perpendicular that misses the midpoint.
Connects to
Day 8 defined a locus in two directions: every point on the figure has the property, and every point with the property lies on the figure.

A construction uses only an unmarked straightedge and a compass. You do not read a length in centimeters off a ruler and you do not trust a freehand right angle. Each step is justified by a definition, an axiom, or a theorem already proved.

To copy a segment. Given AB, draw a ray from a new point A′. Open the compass from A to B and, with the point at A′, mark A′B′ equal to AB.

To copy an angle. Given ∠BAC, draw a ray A′D. Swing an arc centered at A that cuts both sides of the angle, at P and Q. With the same opening, swing an arc centered at A′ that cuts A′D at P′. Open the compass from P to Q and, with the point at P′, mark Q′ on the new arc. The ray A′Q′ makes ∠Q′A′P′ equal to ∠BAC (SSS on the two small triangles).

The perpendicular bisector of AB. With opening more than half of AB, swing an arc centered at A and an arc centered at B, so that they meet at two points, C and D. The line CD is the perpendicular bisector of AB.

Why it works (locus). C is drawn so that CA = CB, and likewise CD's other point D. So C and D both lie on the locus of points equidistant from A and B, which Day 8 already named: that locus is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Then CD is that line, and it meets AB at its midpoint, at right angles.

Remember

Straightedge and compass only. The perpendicular bisector of AB is the locus of points P with PA = PB. Copy a length with one compass opening; copy an angle by building a congruent triangle.

Key terms: construction, perpendicular bisector.

Watch me

Construct the perpendicular bisector of a segment AB, and give the reason that the two crossing points lie on it.

  1. Opening the compass more than half of AB, swing an arc from A and an arc from B. They meet at two points, C and D. Draw CD.
  2. By construction, CA = CB (the same opening), and DA = DB. So C and D are each equidistant from A and B.
  3. The locus of points equidistant from A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB, so CD is that line.

Together

Hint 2: Two sides

There is one such point on each side of AB (and also the midpoint, which is 3 cm from each end, not 5). So yes: P must lie on the perpendicular bisector, and there are two points at 5 cm from both ends.

Writing & Rhetoric · Sentence combining and transitions

45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, sentence combining, then planned transitions

Learn

Key terms

sentence combining
Definition
The skill of joining two or more short, complete sentences into one sentence that keeps all the meaning and is easier to follow, using a conjunction, a relative clause, an appositive, or a joining punctuation mark.
Example
The fresco is damaged. It is still readable. → The fresco is damaged, but it is still readable. Or: The fresco, which is damaged, is still readable.
Not this
Combining is not gluing sentences with a comma alone (a comma splice), and it is not leaving out a fact in order to make the sentence short.
Connects to
Days 1–3 already gave because/but/so, appositives, and subordinating conjunctions. Today those tools are used on purpose, not one at a time as drills.
transition
Definition
A word or short phrase that shows the relation between two sentences or two parts of a sentence: addition, contrast, cause, result, or sequence (for example, however, therefore, next, for instance).
Origin
From Middle French transition, from Latin trānsitiō; in English, transit + -ion.
Example
The treaty is early. It is not the only evidence. → The treaty is early; however, it is not the only evidence.
Not this
A transition is not a decoration added at the start of every sentence, and ‘however’ is not a synonym of ‘and’. The relation has to be real.
Connects to
Tomorrow’s copywork will show how a strong model chooses a transition rather than repeating the same conjunction.

Two short sentences are often clearer after they become one, if the joining word tells the real relation.

Combine with a purpose. Because / but / so remain the first tools (Day 1). A relative clause or an appositive (Day 3) can tuck a fact beside a noun. A semicolon plus a conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, meanwhile) is for two closely related complete sentences; that mark will be named carefully on Day 13.

Transitions between sentences. After a sentence has ended, the next sentence may need a clear relation: for example, however, therefore, next, in the same way. Choose the word that matches the thought, or use none if the relation is already obvious.

A test. If you can delete the transition and the two sentences still seem to have the same relation, the transition was only decoration. If the relation disappears or reverses, the transition was doing work.

Remember

Combine two short sentences so that no fact is lost and the joining word matches the relation (because, but, so, a relative, an appositive, or a transition). Do not splice with a comma alone.

Key terms: sentence combining, transition.

Watch me

  2. With a relative clause: The fresco, which is damaged, is still readable.
  3. Both keep the two facts. A comma splice (‘The fresco is damaged, it is still readable’) is not a legal combination.

Physics · Friction and the normal force

45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student

Learn

Key terms

normal force N
Definition
The force a surface exerts on an object, perpendicular to the surface. On a level surface, if the object is not accelerating up or down, the normal force balances the object’s weight.
Origin
In the earliest English sense, normal means ‘perpendicular, forming a right angle’ (from Latin nōrmālis, from nōrma, a carpenter’s square). That is the sense used here, not ‘ordinary’.
Example
A 5 kg block on a level table has weight 5 × 9.8 = 49 N downward, so the normal force is 49 N upward.
Not this
‘Normal’ here does not mean ‘usual’. It means perpendicular (the older English sense). The normal force is not always equal to the weight: on a slope, or if you push the object down, it changes.
Connects to
Day 8’s second law still governs: the net force, including the normal force and friction, equals m a.
friction f = μN
Definition
A force between surfaces in contact that resists their relative sliding. Kinetic friction acts while they slide, often written fk = μk N. Static friction acts to prevent sliding, up to a maximum fs,max = μs N, and can be less than that maximum if a smaller force already keeps the object still.
Origin
From Middle French friction and from Latin frictiō (‘a rubbing’).
Example
If N = 49 N and μk = 0.2, then fk = 9.8 N, opposite the slide. If μs = 0.4, a horizontal pull of 15 N, which is less than 19.6 N, need not start the block moving.
Not this
Friction is not ‘what stops everything by itself’, and μ is not a force. μ is a measured ratio, different for static and kinetic cases, and it is not a property of one surface alone.
Connects to
Friction is a real force in Newton’s second law (Day 8). It is not a third-law partner of the weight.

A surface pushes back perpendicular to itself: that push is the normal force N. On a level table, with no vertical acceleration, N equals the weight mg.

Friction is the force that resists sliding, along the surface. While the object slides, kinetic friction is often well modeled as fk = μk N, opposite the velocity along the surface. Before it starts to slide, static friction matches the sideways push, up to μs N, and then gives way.

The coefficients μs and μk are measured ratios, not explanations. Typically μs is a little larger than μk. Always draw the forces before writing Fnet = ma.

A clean example, with g = 9.8 m/s². A 5 kg block is on a level surface, so N = 49 N. If μk = 0.2, then fk = 9.8 N. A horizontal pull of 19.8 N gives Fnet = 19.8 − 9.8 = 10 N, so a = 10/5 = 2.0 m/s².

Remember

N is perpendicular to the surface; on the level, with no vertical acceleration, N = mg. Kinetic friction f_k = μ_k N, opposite the slide. Static friction ≤ μ_s N. Draw forces, then F_net = ma.

Key terms: normal force, friction.

Watch me

  2. fk = 0.2 × 49 = 9.8 N, opposite the pull.
  3. Fnet = 19.8 − 9.8 = 10 N, so a = 10/5 = 2.0 m/s² in the direction of the pull.

History · Egypt: the Old Kingdom and the pyramids

45 min · Method: chronological study from primary sources; later accounts judged against them

Learn

Key terms

Old Kingdom
Definition
The period of Egyptian history in which the great pyramids of Giza were built, conventionally the Third to Sixth Dynasties. Exact years in BCE depend on which chronology is used; this course will not treat a single year-number as certain.
Example
The Great Pyramid at Giza is attributed to the 4th-Dynasty king Khufu (called Cheops in later Greek). Published chronologies often place his reign in the mid-third millennium BCE, plus or minus decades.
Not this
The Old Kingdom is not ‘all of ancient Egypt’, and it is not a name Egyptians used for themselves in this form. Later periods (Middle Kingdom, New Kingdom) are different, and must not be collapsed into one ‘pyramid age’.
Connects to
Day 1 already warned that dates for the ancient Near East can differ by decades between books. Use that warning here, rather than memorizing one year as a fact.
pyramid (Egyptian)
Definition
A royal tomb of this period, with a square base and four sloping faces, built in stone, of which the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza is the largest. It is a monument of kingship and of the afterlife, not a granary and not a temple of the later Greek or Roman type.
Origin
From Latin, from Ancient Greek πυραμίς. (A suggested Egyptian source is not settled, and is left out here.)
Example
The Giza plateau holds three large royal pyramids (attributed to Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure) and many smaller tombs. The form developed from earlier mastaba tombs and stepped pyramids.
Not this
A pyramid is not a mystery object without a purpose in the evidence we have: it is a royal tomb, surrounded by a funerary complex. Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BCE, is a later traveler, not a 4th-Dynasty official.
Connects to
Tomorrow’s lesson turns to words written inside a later Old Kingdom pyramid (the Pyramid Texts of Unas), so that the tomb is not only a stone shape.

The Old Kingdom is the period of the great stone pyramids. The Great Pyramid at Giza is attributed to Khufu (Greek: Cheops), a king of the 4th Dynasty. This course will say ‘mid-third millennium BCE, with uncertainty of decades’, not a single year treated as exact (Day 1’s warning on Near Eastern chronology).

What the monument is. A royal tomb, in a complex of temples, causeways, and smaller tombs. The form has a history (mastaba; the stepped pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, earlier in the Old Kingdom). It is not useful, in this course, to treat the pyramids as an unsolved riddle with no evidence.

A later source, used carefully. Herodotus, in Book 2 of the Histories, describes the pyramids centuries later and reports figures for labor and time that he was told. Those figures are evidence for what a 5th-century Greek traveler heard, not measurements from Khufu’s building office. Read a short public-domain passage of Herodotus 2 on the pyramids (for example Macaulay’s translation, already used in Week 1), and list: what he claims, how he says he knows, and what you would need in order to test it.

Primary, from the time, looks different. Inscriptions, the tombs themselves, later king-lists, and, from the end of the 5th Dynasty, texts written inside royal pyramids (tomorrow).

Remember

Old Kingdom: the pyramid age (3rd–6th Dynasties); years in BCE are uncertain by decades. The Great Pyramid is Khufu’s royal tomb at Giza, not a riddle without evidence. Herodotus is a later traveler: use him as a source for what he heard, not as a 4th-Dynasty record.

Key terms: Old Kingdom, pyramid (Egyptian).

Atelier · The cube: planes and foreshortening

45 min · Method: sight-size or a fixed viewing point; one main light · After the egg (Day 6) and the first cast

Learn

Key terms

plane (of a form)
Definition
A flat face of a form, or a flattened patch used to understand a curve. A cube has six square planes; a drawing of the cube starts by finding those planes and the angles between them, not by rounding the corners early.
Origin
In the geometric sense, from Latin plānum (‘flat surface’), the neuter of plānus (‘plain’). The word was introduced in the seventeenth century to distinguish this sense from other senses of plain.
Example
In a cube under one light, one plane may be in full light, one in halftone, and one in shadow. The edge where two planes meet is a change of value as well as a change of direction.
Not this
A plane is not a scribble of tone, and it is not ‘shading for its own sake’. If the planes are wrong, later modeling will not save the cube.
Connects to
Day 4’s value scale and Day 8’s light and shadow families are how you paint the planes, once the construction is true.
foreshortening
Definition
The apparent shortening of a length or a shape that recedes from you. A square plane of the cube that turns away no longer looks square on the page; a side that comes toward you looks shorter than you know it is.
Example
The top of a cube below eye level appears as a flattened shape, not a true square. You measure the shape you see, from a fixed viewing point, rather than the square you know is there.
Not this
Foreshortening is not a trick of style, and it is not an excuse to guess. It is the same proportion skill as Day 2, applied to a length that points toward or away from you.
Connects to
Sight-size (Day 1) and a still viewing point make the foreshortened shape comparable with a pencil at arm’s length.

A cube is the first faceted solid. It teaches planes and foreshortening before the cylinder and the sphere.

Set-up. One cube (wood or paper, large enough to see), one main light, a fixed viewing point, the same sight-size habit as Week 1. Do not move the light mid-drawing.

Construction, not decoration. First, the overall block: the height, width, and the angles of the front edges. Then the receding edges. A receding square is foreshortened: you measure the shape on the picture plane, not the shape you know. Check parallels: edges that are parallel on the cube should converge only as perspective requires, and they should not wander independently.

Light on planes. Each visible plane gets one family of value, nearly even, with a clear change at the edge. A cube is not an egg: do not melt the edges into a curve. Reserve a highlight only if a plane actually catches a glare; most matte cubes simply have a light plane, a mid plane, and a shadow plane, plus a cast shadow on the table.

A common error. Drawing the top as a full square ‘because I know it is square’, or rounding the corners to make it ‘look nice’. Both flatten or destroy the form.

Timed block-in first (10 minutes), then a measured correction, then the three plane values.

Remember

A cube is planes, not a rounded box. Foreshortening: measure the receding shape you see, from a fixed point, not the square you know. Each visible plane takes a family of value, with a clear change at the edge.

Key terms: plane (of a form), foreshortening.

Modern language · Spanish: stem-changing verbs, e → ie

30 min · Method: comprehensible input, then explicit conjugation · New forms are heard in Spanish; instruction stays in English

Learn

Key terms

stem-changing verb (e → ie)
Definition
A verb whose stem vowel e becomes ie in the present, in every form except nosotros and vosotros. The infinitive still shows e.
Example
Pensar: pienso, piensas, piensa, pensamos, pensáis, piensan. Querer and preferir follow the same pattern.
Not this
A stem change is not a new tense, and it is not ‘irregular in every form’. Nosotros and vosotros keep the e: pensamos, no ‘piensamos’.
Connects to
You already conjugate regular -ar, -er, and -ir verbs (Days 5 and 8). This is a change inside the stem, before those same endings.
diphthong (ie)
Definition
Two vowel sounds in one syllable. In these verbs, the e of the stem becomes the diphthong ie when that syllable is stressed.
Origin
From French diphtongue, from Latin diphthongus, from Ancient Greek δίφθογγος (‘two sounds’), from δίς (‘twice’) + φθόγγος (‘sound’).
Example
In pienso the stress is on pien-, so you hear ie. In pensamos the stress is on -sa-, so the stem stays e.
Not this
A diphthong is not a separate word, and it is not an accent mark. The written accent, when it appears (empiezo has none extra; prédica would be a different word), is a spelling fact, not the cause of the stem change.
Connects to
Hearing the stressed syllable tells you where the change will appear.

Some present-tense verbs change the stem in the forms that stress that stem. Today: e → ie.

The pattern. The change happens in yo, tú, él/ella/usted, ellos/ellas/ustedes. It does not happen in nosotros or vosotros, where the stress has moved onto the ending.

pensar (to think): pienso, piensas, piensa, pensamos, pensáis, piensan.

querer (to want): quiero, quieres, quiere, queremos, queréis, quieren.

preferir (to prefer): prefiero, prefieres, prefiere, preferimos, preferís, prefieren.

empezar (to begin) is the same: empiezo, empiezas, empieza, empezamos, empezáis, empiezan.

Remember

Logic · De Morgan’s laws

30 min · Method: explicit rule, then truth tables · After Day 1’s propositions and Day 6’s validity

Learn

Key terms

De Morgan’s laws ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q; ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q
Definition
Two equivalences that move a negation through ‘and’ and ‘or’, and switch the connective: not (P and Q) is equivalent to (not P) or (not Q); not (P or Q) is equivalent to (not P) and (not Q).
Example
‘It is not the case that the figure is both a square and a rhombus that fails to be a square’ is messy. Clearer: ‘either it is not a square, or it is not a (non-square) rhombus’ — but the clean work is on symbols: ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q.
Not this
De Morgan does not say that ‘not P and Q’ is the same as ‘not (P and Q)’. Parentheses matter: ¬P ∧ Q is a different claim from ¬(P ∧ Q).
Connects to
Day 1’s truth tables are the test. An equivalence holds when the two sides have the same truth value in every row.
logical equivalence
Definition
Two statements that have the same truth value in every possible case. We write ⇔ or ‘is equivalent to’ only after the tables (or a proof) show that match.
Example
P and ¬¬P are equivalent: double negation does not change the value. ¬(P ∧ Q) and ¬P ∨ ¬Q are equivalent by De Morgan’s first law.
Not this
Equivalence is not ‘sounds similar in English’, and it is not the same as ‘one implies the other’ in a single direction (Day 6’s validity can hold one way without equivalence).
Connects to
If even one row differs, the two sides are not equivalent.

A negation in front of a whole conjunction or disjunction can be rewritten by pushing the negation in and switching ∧ and ∨.

The two laws (named for the nineteenth-century logician Augustus De Morgan; the pattern is older than the name):

  1. ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q. To deny both at once is to say that at least one is false.
  2. ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q. To deny an ‘or’ is to deny each part.

Why parentheses matter. ¬P ∧ Q means: P is false, and Q is true. That is not the same as ¬(P ∧ Q), which is true whenever at least one of P or Q is false.

How we check. Build a truth table with columns for P, Q, the inner compound, the negated inner compound, and the proposed right-hand side. The two sides are equivalent only if those columns match in every row.

English is slippery (‘not both’, ‘neither…nor’). Translate into symbols first, then apply the law.

Remember

¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q and ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q. Check by a full truth table. ¬P ∧ Q is not the same as ¬(P ∧ Q).

Key terms: De Morgan’s laws, logical equivalence.

Watch me

Fill the four rows for ¬(P ∧ Q) and for ¬P ∨ ¬Q, and say whether they match.

  1. Rows (P, Q): T T, T F, F T, F F.
  2. P ∧ Q is T only in the first row, so ¬(P ∧ Q) is F, T, T, T.
  3. ¬P ∨ ¬Q is F ∨ F = F in the first row, and T in the other three. The columns match: the first law holds.
