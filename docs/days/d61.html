Latin · Capitulum XI, part 1: third-declension neuters (corpus, caput)

30 min · Text: Ørberg, Familia Romana, Cap. XI · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences

Learn

Key terms

third-declension neuter
Definition
A neuter noun of the third family (genitive singular in -is). In the singular, the nominative and the accusative have the same form; in the plural, both of those cases end in -a. The stem is still read from the genitive: corpus, corporis (stem corpor-); caput, capitis (stem capit-).
Example
Corpus magnum est. Puella caput videt. (Here caput is the object, and it looks just like the nominative.)
Not this
Not a masculine or feminine third-declension noun such as rēx, rēgem, whose accusative singular ends in -em. Do not invent *corporem or *capitem.
Connects to
Week 7 and Capitulum X already used third-family nouns with genitive -is. Today’s job is the neuter pattern: nominative equals accusative, and the plural of those two cases is in -a.
Word How to say it Meaning
corpus, corporis n. KOR-poos, KOR-po-ris body; of the body
caput, capitis n. KA-put, KA-pi-tis head; of the head
magnum MAG-num large (neuter nominative or accusative singular)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Capitulum XI turns to the human body, and with it a useful pattern in the third family. Neuter nouns such as corpus and caput still form the genitive singular in -is, so the stem is still read there: corpor-, capit-.

What is new is the neuter rule. In the singular, the nominative and the accusative are the same form: corpus can be the subject or the object. In the plural, those two cases both end in -a: corpora, capita. Do not reach for a made-up accusative in -em.

Original sentences, not lines copied from the book: Corpus magnum est. Puella caput videt. Puer corpus sānum habet. Capita puerōrum parva sunt.

Remember

Third-declension neuters have genitive singular in -is. Nominative and accusative are the same in the singular, and both end in -a in the plural. Read the stem from the genitive: corpus, corporis; caput, capitis.

Key terms: third-declension neuter.

Mathematics · Geometry XXV: similar triangles

40 min · Method: a picture of two same-shape triangles, then equal angles and proportional sides

Learn

Key terms

similar triangles
Definition
Two triangles are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are proportional — they are the same shape, not necessarily the same size. We write △ABC ∼ △DEF when angle A = angle D, angle B = angle E, angle C = angle F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD.
Origin
From French similaire, from Medieval Latin similaris, from Latin similis, “like,” akin to simul, “together” (English Wiktionary, entry “similar”).
Example
A triangle with sides 3, 5, and 6 is similar to a triangle with sides 6, 10, and 12: each side of the second is twice the corresponding side of the first, and the angles match.
Not this
Not congruence. Congruent triangles (△ABC ≅ △DEF) can be placed exactly on one another; similar triangles may differ in size. Equal angles alone, without a check of which angles correspond, is not yet a similarity statement.
Connects to
Congruence, corresponding parts, and the angle sum of a triangle were taught in Term 1. Today those ideas are used for same-shape triangles whose sides stay in one ratio.

Two triangles can be the same shape even when they are not the same size. That is similarity. Corresponding angles are equal, and corresponding sides are proportional: they stand in one constant ratio, the scale factor.

If △ABC ∼ △DEF, then ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, ∠C = ∠F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD. The letters tell you which vertex matches which. A scale factor of 2, as in the figure, means every side of the larger triangle is twice the matching side of the smaller one: 3 maps to 6, 5 to 10, and 6 to 12.

Congruence is the stricter matching you already know: same shape and same size. Similar triangles need only the same shape. Do not write ≅ when you only have ∼.

Remember

Similar triangles have corresponding angles equal and corresponding sides proportional. We write △ABC ∼ △DEF. Congruence (≅) is same shape and same size; similarity (∼) is same shape, with a scale factor that may be other than 1.

Key terms: similar triangles.

  3. The triangles are not congruent, because the sides are not equal. Similarity is the right word.

Writing & Rhetoric · The Toulmin model: claim, grounds, and warrant

40 min · Method: the already-taught claim, evidence, and warrant, now named as a Toulmin model

Learn

Key terms

Toulmin model
Definition
A named way of analyzing an argument into a claim, the data or grounds that support it, and a warrant — the general rule that makes those grounds count as support for that claim. In this course the three-part model is taken from Stephen Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958).
Example
Claim: the window is open. Grounds: the curtain is moving, and the air in the room is cold. Warrant: an open window lets in a draft, which moves a curtain and cools a room.
Not this
Not a new replacement for the claim, evidence, and warrant already taught on Day 8. Toulmin’s word for the supporting material is data or grounds; this course already called that material evidence. The model does not, by itself, make a claim true.
Connects to
Day 8 taught claim, evidence, and warrant. Today those same three parts are named as a Toulmin model, so they can be used on a longer public-domain passage later this week.
grounds (Toulmin)
Definition
Toulmin’s name for the supporting material under a claim — a fact, a detail, or a short quotation given accurately. In this course it is the same job as evidence, taught on Day 8.
Example
In the window argument, “the curtain is moving” and “the air in the room is cold” are grounds, not the claim and not the warrant.
Not this
Not the claim itself, and not the general rule (the warrant) that joins the grounds to the claim. A restated claim is not grounds.
Connects to
Day 8’s evidence is the same job under a different name. Tomorrow the three parts are applied to a short, named public-domain extract.

On Day 8 you already named a claim, the evidence that is supposed to support it, and the warrant that joins the two. Term 2 starts with those same three parts, now under a name you can use on a longer passage: the Toulmin model, from Stephen Toulmin, The Uses of Argument (1958).

Toulmin calls the supporting material data or grounds. That is the same job as evidence. The claim is still a sentence that can be argued with. The warrant is still the general rule that makes those grounds count as support for that claim, not a second copy of the claim.

A short invented example, so the parts are visible before a real text is used. Claim: the window is open. Grounds: the curtain is moving, and the air in the room is cold. Warrant: an open window lets in a draft, which moves a curtain and cools a room. If the warrant were only “therefore the window is open,” it would be a restated claim, not a warrant.

Remember

The Toulmin model names a claim, the grounds (data) that support it, and a warrant that joins the two. Grounds are the same job as Day 8’s evidence. A warrant is a general rule, not a restated claim.

Key terms: Toulmin model, grounds (Toulmin).

Physics · Rotational equilibrium of a rigid body

50 min · Method: the already-taught torque, then Στ = 0 and ΣF = 0 for a body at rest

Learn

Key terms

rotational equilibrium
Definition
A rigid body is in rotational equilibrium about a point or an axis when the sum of the torques about that point is zero (Στ = 0): the clockwise torques and the counterclockwise torques balance, so there is no net tendency to start turning. A body that remains at rest also has no net force (ΣF = 0).
Origin
From Latin aequilibrium, from aequus, “equal,” and lībra, “balance” (English Wiktionary, entry “equilibrium”).
Example
A light rod, 2.00 m long, is supported at its center. A 30 N force downward at the left end is balanced by a 30 N force downward at the right end: each torque about the center is 30 N × 1.00 m = 30 N·m, in opposite senses, so Στ = 0. The support then supplies 60 N upward, so ΣF = 0 as well.
Not this
Not the already-taught market equilibrium of supply and demand. Not a claim that one force of zero is enough, or that torques may be added without naming the point they are taken about. The unit of each torque is still the newton-meter (N·m), not the joule.
Connects to
Day 33 taught torque as τ = F × d⊥, in N·m, and already hinted that equal-and-opposite torques can balance. Today that hint becomes a condition: Στ = 0, and, for a body at rest, ΣF = 0 as well.

Day 33 treated torque as a first look: τ = F × d⊥, measured in newton-meters, not joules. A force can turn a body about a point; a force through the point gives no torque.

If a rigid body does not start to turn, the torques about that point add to zero. That is rotational equilibrium: Στ = 0. Choose a sense (clockwise or counterclockwise) and treat the other sense as the opposite sign, so that equal torques in opposite senses cancel.

If the body also remains at rest, the forces themselves must balance: ΣF = 0. A support can supply the upward force that makes the vertical forces add to zero, even while the torques are balanced about the same support.

A labeled invented example. A light rod, 2.00 m long, is supported at its center. 30 N acts downward at the left end, 1.00 m from the support, and 30 N downward at the right end, also 1.00 m from the support. Each of those forces gives a torque of 30 N × 1.00 m = 30 N·m, in opposite senses, so Στ = 0. The support supplies 60 N upward, so 30 N + 30 N downward and 60 N upward give ΣF = 0. These numbers are invented classroom figures, not a measurement from a named machine.

Remember

Rotational equilibrium about a point means Στ = 0: clockwise and counterclockwise torques balance. A body that stays at rest also has ΣF = 0. Torque is still τ = F × d⊥, in N·m, not joules. The market sense of equilibrium is a different word.

Key terms: rotational equilibrium.

  2. The two downward forces add to 40 N, so the support supplies 40 N upward if ΣF = 0.
  3. The units of each torque are N·m, not joules.

History · 410 CE: Augustine on the sack of Rome

40 min · Method: a short attributed extract from Augustine, with the edition’s date 410

Learn

Key terms

late antiquity
Definition
The name later writers give to the long transition from the later Roman world to the early medieval world. It is a period name, not a single year, and it is not a claim that the empire vanished on one date.
Example
The sack of Rome in 410 CE, and the conventional later marker of 476 CE taught later this week, both sit in late antiquity. The eastern empire continued.
Not this
Not a single day on a calendar, and not a synonym for “the fall of Rome” as if everything ended at once. This week’s work starts from a named source, not from a slogan.
Connects to
Term 1 ended with Flavian Rome and with Pliny’s later letter. Term 2 begins in the later Roman world, with a primary source for 410.
sack of Rome (410)
Definition
The taking and plundering of Rome in 410 CE by the Goths under Alaric, as later dated in the Dods edition of Augustine. This course uses Augustine’s own later account of why he wrote The City of God, not a battlefield report, and it does not invent a death toll.
Example
Augustine writes that Rome having been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king, some pagans attributed the calamity to the Christian religion, and that this prompted him to write in defense of the city of God.
Not this
Not a first-person description of the sack written from the walls, and not a source for a death toll or for a three-day timetable this course has not set. 410 is the year given in the edition’s footnote, not a figure invented here.
Connects to
The set extract is from Augustine, Retractations II.43, as quoted in Dods’s preface. It tells us why Augustine wrote, and that the sack had happened, under Alaric.

Term 2 of history begins in late antiquity: the long transition out of the later Roman world, not a single morning on which “Rome fell.” A first dated event in that stretch is the sack of Rome in 410 CE, when the Goths under Alaric took the city. This course will not invent a death toll, or color that is not in the set source.

The set extract is Augustine’s later account of why he wrote The City of God. It is not a camera at the walls. In Marcus Dods’s translation, a footnote dates the sack A.D. 410. Augustine is looking back, and he tells us how some people explained the calamity, and what that explanation prompted him to write.

Rome having been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king, the worshippers of false gods, or pagans, as we commonly call them, made an attempt to attribute this calamity to the Christian religion, and began to blaspheme the true God with even more than their wonted bitterness and acerbity. It was this which kindled my zeal for the house of God, and prompted me to undertake the defence of the city of God against the charges and misrepresentations of its assailants.

Augustine, Retractations II.43, as quoted in Marcus Dods’s translator’s preface to The City of God, in Philip Schaff, A Select Library of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, First Series, Vol. 2; text from the Christian Classics Ethereal Library. A footnote in that edition dates the sack A.D. 410. https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf102.iv.i.html

Read what is there: Rome had been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric; some pagans attributed that calamity to the Christian religion; Augustine says this kindled his zeal and prompted the defense of the city of God. Do not add a death toll, a gate, or a number of days that the extract does not give.

Remember

In 410 CE, as footnoted in the Dods edition, Rome was sacked by the Goths under Alaric. The set extract is Augustine’s later account of why he wrote The City of God, not a battlefield report. Late antiquity is a period name, not a single day. No death toll is invented.

60 min · Method: the already-taught head block-in, now as a full-head sitting from a still plate or plaster

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • block-in (head) — Day 21

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Modern language · Spanish: the preterite of regular -ar verbs

30 min · Method: a model verb (hablar), then the six regular -ar endings

Learn

Key terms

preterite of regular -ar verbs
Definition
A simple-past tense of Spanish regular -ar verbs, used for a completed action in the past. The endings, shown on hablar, are -é, -aste, -ó, -amos, -asteis, -aron: hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablasteis, hablaron. Instruction in this lesson is in English; the Spanish words are the forms being taught.
Origin
From Middle English preterit, from Old French preterit, from Latin praeteritum (as in tempus praeteritum, “time past”), the past participle of praetereō, “to go past” (English Wiktionary, entry “preterite”).
Example
Ayer yo hablé con el maestro. Ella cantó una canción. Ellos compraron pan.
Not this
Not the already-taught present (hablo, hablas, habla…). Not an irregular past such as fui or tuve, which this week does not teach. Note that nosotros hablamos looks like the present; the time word or the context tells you it is past.
Connects to
Term 1 ended with the present. Today begins a past tense: the preterite of regular -ar verbs only.

Term 1 used the present: hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan. Today begins a past tense, the preterite, for a completed action: I spoke, she sang, they bought. For regular -ar verbs the endings are the same on every such verb. Hablar is the model.

Logic · Celarent, and the major and minor terms

30 min · Method: a second valid first-figure mood, beside the already-taught Barbara

Learn

Key terms

Celarent
Definition
The traditional name for a valid syllogism in the first figure whose vowels are E, A, and E: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. The middle term is the subject of the first premise and the predicate of the second, as it was in Barbara.
Example
No metals are gases. All irons are metals. Therefore no irons are gases.
Not this
Not Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), and not an invalid form with an undistributed middle. Celarent’s conclusion is negative and universal (No S are P), not an affirmative All S are P.
Connects to
Day 31 taught the syllogism and Barbara (AAA in the first figure). Day 36 warned against the undistributed middle. Today adds a second valid first-figure mood, Celarent (EAE).
major term and minor term
Definition
In a syllogism, the major term (P) is the predicate of the conclusion, and the minor term (S) is the subject of the conclusion. The middle term (M), already taught, links them in the premises and does not appear in the conclusion.
Example
In the Celarent example, gases is the major term, irons the minor term, and metals the middle term: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P.
Not this
Not a claim that “major” means more important in ordinary speech. The names mark jobs in the syllogism: S and P in the conclusion, M in both premises.
Connects to
The middle term was already named with Barbara on Day 31. Today the other two terms are named as well, so Celarent’s pattern S, M, and P can be read clearly.

Day 31 taught the syllogism and one valid first-figure mood, Barbara: All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P. The middle term M linked the other two and did not appear in the conclusion. Today adds a second valid mood in the same figure: Celarent.

The vowels in the name record the pattern: E, A, E. That means: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. The figure is still the first figure: M is the subject of the first premise and the predicate of the second.

The major term (P) is the predicate of the conclusion; the minor term (S) is the subject of the conclusion; the middle term (M) is already known. In this example, gases is P, irons is S, and metals is M:

No metals are gases.
All irons are metals.
Therefore no irons are gases.

The conclusion is a universal negative, not Barbara’s universal affirmative. An undistributed middle, already taught as invalid, is still invalid here: the middle term must do real linking work in the premises.

Remember

Celarent is the first-figure mood EAE: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. The major term P is the predicate of the conclusion; the minor term S is its subject; the middle term M links them and is absent from the conclusion. This is not Barbara (AAA), and it is not an undistributed middle.
