Latin · Capitulum XIV: the present participle

30 min · Latin · a first look at the Latin present participle. Original sentences only.

Learn

Key terms

present participle (Latin)
Definition
The Latin present participle describes an action going on at the same time as the main verb. It is formed with -ns, -ntis (legēns, legentis), declines like a third-declension adjective, and agrees in gender, number, and case with the noun it describes.
Example
Puer legēns rīdet. — The boy, reading, is smiling. (The reading is going on as he smiles.)
Not this
Not the main verb of the sentence, and not the Spanish present participle (-ando / -iendo) already taught on Day 22, which is a different language’s form. Not an English dangling participle from Day 23 treated as if it were Latin grammar.
Connects to
English participial phrases were taught on Day 23; Spanish -ando / -iendo on Day 22. Today is the Latin form, in original sentences, with the usual third-declension -nt- pattern (compare Allen and Greenough on the present participle).

The present participle in Latin names an action that is going on at the same time as the main verb. You can hear the -nt- in the stem (and -ns in many nominative singulars).

Word How to say it Meaning
legēns LEH-gens reading (present participle, nom. sing.)
scribēns SKREE-bens writing
audientēs ow-dee-EN-tace listening, hearing (nom. pl.)

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

It agrees with a noun, like an adjective: puer legēns, puellae scribentēs.

Original sentences:

  • Puer legēns rīdet. — The boy, reading, is smiling.
  • Puellae scribentēs sedent. — The girls, writing, are sitting.

The participle is not the main verb: rīdet and sedent are the main verbs. A longer set of cases is not dumped today; the job is to recognize the -nt- form, agree it, and read it as a same-time action.

Sentences are original, not copied from the book. Instruction stays in English.

Remember

The Latin present participle (-ns, -ntis) agrees with its noun and names a same-time action, not the main verb. Original sentences only.

60 min · Geometry

Key terms

triangle as half a parallelogram
Definition
A diagonal of a parallelogram splits it into two triangles of equal area. Each triangle has the same base and the same perpendicular height as the parallelogram, so its area is ½bh — the formula already taught last week, now seen as half the parallelogram.
Example
A parallelogram of base 8 and height 5 has area 40, so each of the two triangles made by one diagonal has area 20, which is also ½ × 8 × 5.
Not this
Not a new formula in place of last week’s ½bh, and not a claim that any two triangles in the figure have equal area — only the two made by one diagonal of the parallelogram.
Connects to
Last week, A = ½bh for a triangle, with h perpendicular to the base. Today that half is the half of a parallelogram on the same base and height.

Writing & Rhetoric · Post hoc

45 min · Writing

Learn

Key terms

post hoc
Definition
Post hoc (short for post hoc, ergo propter hoc: after this, therefore because of this) is the flaw of treating a later event as caused by an earlier one only because it came after.
Example
Invented (labeled invented): ‘I wore a green shirt, and then the team won; the shirt caused the win.’ The win came after the shirt, but that order alone does not show a cause.
Not this
Not circular reasoning (yesterday’s restatement of a claim), and not a careful causal argument that offers a mechanism or a controlled comparison. Sequence alone is not a cause.
Connects to
A ground can include a time order, but time order is not enough. Toulmin’s warrant would have to say why this earlier event produced the later one, not only that it came first.

Post hoc, from the Latin phrase post hoc, ergo propter hoc (‘after this, therefore because of this’), is the flaw of treating what came later as caused by what came earlier, only because of the order.

Invented classroom examples, labeled invented:

  • ‘I wore a green shirt, and then the team won; the shirt caused the win.’
  • ‘The bell rang, and then it rained; the bell caused the rain.’

The later event did come after. That is not in dispute. The flaw is using order alone as if it were a cause.

A better causal claim would need a warrant you can inspect: a mechanism, a comparison, or a sourced account — still labeled invented if the story is invented, or sourced if it is not.

Yesterday’s circle restates a claim. Post hoc jumps from sequence to cause. They are different flaws.

Remember

Post hoc treats a later event as caused by an earlier one only because it came after. Sequence alone is not a cause. Invented examples are labeled invented.

Science · Water: a saturated solution

45 min · Science · Water continues; saturation was reserved last week

Learn

Key terms

saturated solution
Definition
In the IUPAC Gold Book (S05471), a saturated solution is a solution which has the same concentration of a solute as one that is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at specified values of the temperature and pressure.
Example
If, at a stated temperature, leftover undissolved solute remains in contact with the solution and no more will dissolve, the solution is saturated in the Gold Book’s sense. A classroom dish used to show leftover solid is invented and labeled invented, not an official solubility trial.
Not this
Not merely a ‘strong’ or concentrated solution. Last week’s concentrated meant relatively much solute in a given amount of solution, qualitatively; a solution can be concentrated without being in equilibrium with leftover solid, and a saturated solution is defined by that equilibrium at a stated temperature and pressure.
Connects to
Last week taught solution, solute, solvent, dissolution, and dilute versus concentrated, from the Gold Book, and reserved saturation. Today uses S05471. No official percentage is invented.

Last week a solution had a solute in a solvent, and dilute versus concentrated were qualitative. Saturated was reserved. Today it is taught from the IUPAC Gold Book.

A saturated solution (Gold Book S05471) has the same concentration of a solute as a solution that is in equilibrium with undissolved solute, at specified temperature and pressure.

In school language: at that temperature and pressure, no more of that solute will dissolve, and leftover solid can remain in contact with the solution. Adding still more solid does not, at equilibrium, raise the concentration.

That is not the same as last week’s concentrated. Concentrated means relatively much solute. Saturated means the equilibrium with leftover solute has been reached, at a stated temperature and pressure. A dilute solution is not automatically unsaturated in a way that invents a number; the Gold Book’s contrast today is with the equilibrium itself.

Classroom leftover solid in a dish, if you demonstrate it, is labeled invented. No official percentage, solubility, or molarity is invented today. Solubility, as a named quantity, is Thursday’s job.

Definitions of a saturated solution and of solubility follow the IUPAC Gold Book: a saturated solution is a solution which has the same concentration of a solute as one that is in equilibrium with undissolved solute at specified values of the temperature and pressure (Gold Book S05471, https://doi.org/10.1351/goldbook.S05471, https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05471.html); solubility is the analytical composition of a saturated solution, expressed as the proportion of a designated solute in a designated solvent (Gold Book S05740, https://doi.org/10.1351/goldbook.S05740, https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/S05740.html). No official percentage, solubility table, or molarity figure is invented this week. Classroom dishes and leftover solid, if used as a demonstration, are labeled invented.

Remember

A saturated solution, IUPAC Gold Book S05471, has the same concentration as a solution in equilibrium with undissolved solute at a specified temperature and pressure. That is not the same as last week’s ‘concentrated.’ No official percentage is invented.

Atelier · Checking the terminator, on the head

90 min · Atelier · the line where the families meet, applied to the head

Learn

Key terms

terminator (on the head)
Definition
The terminator is the turning where the light family ends and the shadow family begins — the place the form stops facing the lamp. On the head it runs, in one held light, across forehead, cheek, and jaw as a changing edge, not as a cut-out outline.
Example
If the lamp stands to the left, the terminator falls down the far side of the nose and into the cheek, and then along the jaw, still as the meeting of two families, not as a drawn hoop.
Not this
Not a first look at the turn of the form (Day 43), and not a new core shadow. Not a cartoon outline around the profile. Not a second lamp.
Connects to
Yesterday grouped values into two families. Today those families are checked where they meet. The sphere already had a turn from midtone into core shadow; the head has the same kind of meeting, along a more complicated form.

Yesterday the head’s values were grouped into a light family and a shadow family. Today you check the terminator: the turning where those families meet, where the form stops facing the lamp.

On a sphere, that meeting was the turn from midtone into core shadow (Day 43). On the head the same job runs across several planes: forehead, temple, cheek, jaw, sometimes the side of the nose. The terminator is a changing edge, softer where the form turns slowly, sharper where a plane breaks. It is not a hoop drawn around the profile, and it is not a cartoon outline.

Work in the same held light as yesterday. If a feature’s value from last week’s pass fights the terminator — a cheek midtone that has slipped into the shadow, or a core shadow that has crept into the light — correct the family, not the feature’s outline first.

Modern language · Spanish: estar and andar, the same uv pattern

25 min · Spanish · estuve and anduve, with yesterday’s tuve. Instruction in English.

Learn

Key terms

uv-stem preterite
Definition
Tener, estar, and andar share a uv-stem in the preterite: tuve, estuve, anduve (and tuviste, estuviste, anduviste, and so on). The endings are the same set; the stems are tuv-, estuv-, and anduv-.
Example
Ayer estuve en casa; ella anduvo al parque. (Invented drills, labeled invented.)
Not this
Not the fui set of ser and ir, and not the present estoy or ando. Not a first look at the preterite tense itself.
Connects to
Yesterday taught tuve. Today adds estar and andar to the same pattern, so the three verbs can be learned as one stem habit rather than as three unrelated lists.

Yesterday tener used tuv-. Today estar and andar use the same kind of stem: estuv- and anduv-, with the same preterite endings.

Word How to say it Meaning
estuve es-TOO-beh I was (preterite of estar)
estuvo es-TOO-boh he/she/you formal was
anduve ahn-DOO-beh I walked, I went (on foot), preterite of andar
anduvo ahn-DOO-boh he/she/you formal walked
  • estar: estuve, estuviste, estuvo, estuvimos, estuvisteis, estuvieron
  • andar: anduve, anduviste, anduvo, anduvimos, anduvisteis, anduvieron

So tuve, estuve, anduve are one pattern, not three exceptions to memorize as if they had nothing in common.

Invented drills, labeled invented: Ayer estuve en casa. Él anduvo al parque.

Fui remains ser or ir, already taught. Estuve is estar. Do not swap them. Instruction stays in English.

Remember

Estar and andar share the uv-stem pattern with tener: estuve, anduve, tuve, and the rest of that ending set. Fui remains ser or ir. Instruction stays in English.

Elective · Music: the deceptive cadence

30 min · Music · a first look at V–vi; the half cadence is already taught

Learn

Key terms

deceptive cadence
Definition
A deceptive cadence is a common-practice close that goes from V to vi (or V to VI in a minor key), so the ear, prepared for I, lands on the relative tonic instead. It is a cadence, but it is not an authentic V–I close, and it is not a half cadence, which ends on V.
Example
In C major, a G-major chord (V) followed by an A-minor chord (vi) is a deceptive cadence: the leading tone in G may still resolve, but the bass does not land on C.
Not this
Not a first look at an authentic cadence (V–I) or a plagal cadence (IV–I), taught last week, and not a first look at a half cadence, which was taught on Day 52 as a pause that ends on V. Today’s new job is the V–vi landing.
Connects to
Last week contrasted authentic and plagal closes. Day 52 taught the half cadence as a pause on V. The deceptive cadence uses V as well, but it continues to vi rather than ending there or closing to I.

Authentic (V–I) and plagal (IV–I) cadences were taught last week. A half cadence, ending on V, was taught on Day 52. None of those is a first look today.

A deceptive cadence also starts from V, but it lands on vi (in a major key; VI in a minor key). The ear is prepared for I and is led elsewhere — hence “deceptive.” It is still a cadence, not a random pair of chords.

In C major, G to A minor is the model: V to vi. The bass does not arrive on C. In A minor, E major (V) to F major (VI) is the parallel model, if you use it; today’s main job is V–vi in a major key.

Do not call a half cadence a deceptive cadence: a half cadence ends on V. Do not call V–I deceptive: that is the authentic close already taught.

A short invented classroom phrase, labeled invented as a drill, may end V–vi instead of V–I, so the ear can hear the difference.

Remember

A deceptive cadence is V–vi (or V–VI in minor): the ear is prepared for I and lands on vi instead. It is not a half cadence (ends on V, Day 52) and not an authentic V–I. Last week’s authentic and plagal closes stay those jobs.
