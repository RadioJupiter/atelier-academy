Latin · Sequar and ēgrediar, mixed with already-taught deponent tenses

40 min · Method: future, present, and pluperfect of sequor and ēgredior, mixed; original sentences

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • future of sequor — Day 136
  • future of ēgredior — Day 137
  • pluperfect of sequor — Day 131
  • pluperfect of ēgredior — Day 132

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today mixes the new futures with the already-taught presents and pluperfects. Sequar, I shall follow, is still ahead; sequor is present; secūtus eram is already completed before another past moment. Ēgrediar, ēgredior, and ēgressus eram stand in the same three-way contrast. A fair mix: Puer mē sequētur, quia ego ēgrediar; or Puella ēgressa erat, tum tacēbat. Do not swap the verbs, and do not treat a future as a pluperfect. Sentences stay original.

Remember

Sequar is I shall follow; sequor is I follow; secūtus eram is I had followed. The same three-way contrast holds for ēgrediar, ēgredior, and ēgressus eram.

Watch me

Choose the form of sequor for “they will follow,” and the form of ēgredior for “I had gone out,” and put each in a short original sentence.

  1. They will follow is sequentur; I had gone out is ēgressus eram (or ēgressa eram).
  2. A fair pair: Amīcī mē sequentur. Ego iam ēgressus eram.
  3. Do not write sequuntur for the future, or ēgrediar for the pluperfect.

Together

Which form is needed for “she will go out,” and which for “we had followed”?

Hint 1: Future of ēgredior

She will go out is ēgrediētur.

Hint 2: Pluperfect of sequor

We had followed is secūtī erāmus (or secūtae erāmus).

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Give the first-person singular present, future, and pluperfect of sequor, with meanings.
  2. Translate: Puella ēgrediētur. Puer eam secūtus erat.
  3. Why is sequuntur a miss for they will follow?

Exercises

  1. Form the future of “we go out” and the present of “he follows.”
  2. Write two original sentences, one with sequēmur and one with ēgressī erant.
  3. Why must Puer or Puella, not an unquoted English identity word, mark the person in a Latin sentence of this kind?

Mathematics · Geometry LXXXI: the law of cosines, finding an angle from three sides

40 min · Method: cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab); invented figures labeled invented

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the law of cosines — Day 136

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

The same formula can be rearranged when all three sides are known and an angle is wanted: cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab), where c is the side opposite the angle being found. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, the cosine of the angle opposite 5 is (9 + 16 − 25)/(2·3·4) = 0/24 = 0, so that angle is 90°. On an invented equilateral triangle with sides 5, 5, and 5, cos C = (25 + 25 − 25)/50 = 1/2, so C = 60°. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 5, and 7, the cosine of the angle opposite 7 is (9 + 25 − 49)/(2·3·5) = (−15)/30 = −1/2, so that angle is 120°. The side lengths are invented classroom numbers, chosen so the cosine is a value already known from the unit circle. Do not invent a degree measure that the cosine does not give exactly, and do not switch to radians.

Remember

cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab). If that cosine is 0, C is 90°; if it is 1/2, C is 60°; if it is −1/2, C is 120°.

Watch me

Find the angle opposite 7 in an invented triangle with sides 3, 5, and 7.

  1. cos C = (9 + 25 − 49)/30 = −15/30 = −1/2, so C = 120°.
  2. The sides 3, 5, and 7 are invented classroom numbers.
  3. Do not take the angle as 60°, which would require cosine +1/2.

Together

On an invented 3-4-5 triangle, why is the angle opposite 5 equal to 90°, and why is that not a new official measurement?

Hint 1: The cosine

cos C = (9 + 16 − 25)/24 = 0, so C = 90°.

Hint 2: Invented figures

The sides are invented classroom numbers, even though 3-4-5 is a familiar right triangle.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. Rearrange the law of cosines to give cos C in terms of the three sides.
  2. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, what is the angle opposite 5?
  3. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 5, and 7, what is the angle opposite 7?

Exercises

  1. On an invented equilateral triangle with sides 5, 5, and 5, what is each angle, and how is it found?
  2. Why would taking the angle opposite 7 as 60° be a miss?
  3. What remains reserved?

Physics · Official Examples 17.2 and 17.3, and a factor of two in intensity

40 min · Method: official OpenStax arithmetic; Table 17.3; invented frequencies stay labeled invented if used

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • sound intensity level (the decibel) — Day 136

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Example 17.2 in OpenStax 17.3 calculates the sound intensity level of a sound wave in air at 0°C with pressure amplitude 0.656 Pa. The official speed of sound in air at 0°C is 331 m/s, and the official density of air at atmospheric pressure and 0°C is 1.29 kg/m³. The intensity is I = (Δp)² / (2 ρ vw) = (0.656 Pa)² / (2 × 1.29 kg/m³ × 331 m/s) = 5.04×10^−4 W/m². Then β = 10 log10(I/I0) = 10 log10(5.04×10^8) = 10 × 8.70 = 87 dB. These are official Example 17.2 figures, not invented official measurements of a classroom sound.

Example 17.3 shows that if one sound is twice as intense as another, the levels differ by 10 log10(2.00) = 3.01 dB, or about 3 dB. Table 17.3 lists the official correspondences: an intensity ratio of 2.0 with 3.0 dB; 5.0 with 7.0 dB; 10.0 with 10.0 dB. OpenStax notes that a 56.0 dB sound is twice as intense as a 53.0 dB sound, and that a 97.0 dB sound is half as intense as a 100 dB sound. Do not invent official dB values for real places not in the table. The sound pressure level remains reserved.

Remember

Example 17.2: I = 5.04×10^−4 W/m², β = 87 dB. Example 17.3: a factor of two in intensity is 3.01 dB, about 3 dB.

Watch me

Show the official Example 17.3 step: if I2/I1 = 2.00, what is β2 − β1?

  1. β2 − β1 = 10 log10(I2/I1) = 10 log10(2.00) = 3.01 dB, about 3 dB.
  2. The result depends only on the ratio, so it holds for any pair of intensities in the ratio 2 to 1.
  3. Table 17.3 lists 2.0 with 3.0 dB, the same fact rounded as the table gives it.

Together

In Example 17.2, why is I = 5.04×10^−4 W/m², and why is that 87 dB not an invented official classroom measurement?

Hint 1: The intensity formula

I = (0.656)² / (2 × 1.29 × 331) = 5.04×10^−4 W/m², using the official 331 m/s and 1.29 kg/m³ at 0°C.

Hint 2: The level

I/I0 = 5.04×10^8, and 10 log10(5.04×10^8) = 87 dB, official Example 17.2.

Check

Write in your own words, then look at the key.

  1. What official values of I and β does Example 17.2 give, and from what official Δp, vw, and ρ?
  2. If one sound is twice as intense as another, what official difference of levels does Example 17.3 give?
  3. What official differences does Table 17.3 list for intensity ratios 2.0, 5.0, and 10.0?

Exercises

  1. A 100 dB sound is how many times as intense as a 97.0 dB sound, according to OpenStax?
  2. If an invented intensity is ten times 5.04×10^−4 W/m², what is the new level, using the factor-of-10 rule, and why is the intensity invented?
  3. What remains reserved?

History · The first-voyage letter: Juana, Cathay, and La Española, in Major’s wording

40 min · Method: the letter’s own names and league figures; no official modern identifications

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the first-voyage letter (to Luis de Santangel) — Day 136

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

The letter continues, still in Major’s 1870 wording: “When I reached Juana, I followed its coast to the westward, and found it so large that I thought it must be the mainland,—the province of Cathay.” He kept on, then turned back to a harbor, and sent two men ashore. They “journeyed for three days and found countless small hamlets with numberless inhabitants, but with nothing like order; they therefore returned.” Then: “In the meantime I had learned from some other Indians whom I had seized, that this land was certainly an island; accordingly, I followed the coast eastward for a distance of one hundred and seven leagues, where it ended in a cape. From this cape, I saw another island to the eastward at a distance of eighteen leagues from the former, to which I gave the name of La Española. Thither I went,”

“Harbour,” “Cathay,” and “La Española” stay inside the quotations as Major prints them. The “one hundred and seven leagues” and the “eighteen leagues” are the letter’s figures in this translation, not official modern conversions into miles or degrees, and not official identifications of Juana or La Española with later names. Major’s footnote “Hispaniola or San Domingo” is an editorial identification, not taught as official, and not taught as the letter’s own wording. The Treaty of Tordesillas remains a later document, already taught, not this report.

Remember

The letter names Juana, at first taken for “the mainland,—the province of Cathay,” then treated as an island, and names a further island “La Española.” Its league figures are the letter’s, not official modern conversions.

Watch me

What does the letter say he thought Juana was, and what name does he give the island seen from the cape, in Major’s wording?

  1. He thought Juana “must be the mainland,—the province of Cathay,” then learned “that this land was certainly an island.”
  2. He “gave the name of La Española” to the island seen from the cape, “eighteen leagues from the former.”
  3. Do not add “Cuba” or “Hispaniola” as official identifications.

Together

Hint 1: Whose figures

Hint 2: The course rule

Official modern conversions are not invented, just as the 370 leagues of Tordesillas were not converted.

  2. What league figures does the letter give for the eastward coast and for the distance to the next island, and what name does he give that island?
  3. Why is Major’s footnote “Hispaniola or San Domingo” not taught as official?

Modern language · Spanish: the present subjunctive contrasted with the indicative, the future, and the conditional

40 min · Method: regular present subjunctive beside already-taught tenses; invented sentences

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • present subjunctive of a regular -ar verb — Day 136
  • present subjunctive of regular -er and -ir verbs — Day 137

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Today mixes regular present subjunctives with the already-taught present indicative, future, and conditional. Hable is not habla, not hablará, and not hablaría. Coma is not come, not comerá, and not comería. A “quiere que” frame still asks for the subjunctive in these invented classroom sentences. Do not invent irregular subjunctives. Keep the four tenses distinct, then choose the one the sentence actually needs.

Remember

The present subjunctive of a regular verb is not the indicative, not the future, and not the conditional. “Quiere que” in these invented sentences takes the subjunctive.

Watch me

  1. Hable: the frame “quiere que” takes the present subjunctive in this lesson.
  2. Hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen.
  3. Habla is indicative, hablará is future, hablaría is conditional.

Hint 1: Tomorrow

Hint 2: The frame

There is no “quiere que” here, so the subjunctive is not being asked for.

Seminar · Art history: Dewing, Buildings I.3.16–18, the rebuilt shrine of Michael

40 min · Method: Dewing 1940, LacusCurtius; exact wording; no official measurements; I.4 reserved

Learn

Key terms

the rebuilt shrine of Michael (Dewing, Buildings I.3.16–18)
Definition
In Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, Buildings I.3.16–18, the small, badly lighted shrine of Michael, torn down last week “even to the lowest foundations,” is rebuilt: Justinian, “increasing its size to the proportions which it now displays, … transformed it into a marvellously beautiful building.” The church “is in the form of a rectangle (tetrapleuron), and the length appears not much greater than the width.” Official measurements are not invented.
Example
Dewing I.3.17–18: a thick east wall “of many fitted stones,” “in the middle it is drawn back so as to form a recess”; “columns of naturally variegated hues”; the west wall “is pierced by the doors which lead into the church.”
Not this
Not last week’s unseemly small shrine as if it still stood. Not Book I.4, the churches of Peter and Paul and of Saints Sergius and Bacchus, which remain reserved. Not an official length or an official count of columns.
Connects to
Last week’s Buildings I.3.10–15, including the tearing down of the old Michael shrine. Later Buildings remain reserved.

Last week, in Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, Buildings I.3.14–15, Justinian found a shrine of the Archangel Michael “small and very badly lighted,” “quite like a tiny bedroom,” and “tore this down, even to the lowest foundations.” Today the same translation continues, I.3.16–18, still as posted on LacusCurtius. “And increasing its size to the proportions which it now displays, he transformed it into a marvellously beautiful building.” “Marvellously” is Dewing’s wording and stays inside the quotation. Teaching prose uses US spelling.

“For the church is in the form of a rectangle (tetrapleuron), and the length appears not much greater than the width.” “And at either end of the side which faces the east a thick wall was perfectly constructed of many fitted stones, but in the middle it is drawn back so as to form a recess.” “On either side of this rise columns of naturally variegated hues which support the church. The opposite wall, which faces approximately the west, is pierced by the doors which lead into the church.” Dewing does not give an official length or an official count of those columns, and the classroom invents none. Book I.4, beginning with a church of Peter and Paul and the shrines of Saints Sergius and Bacchus, remains reserved.

Remember

Dewing I.3.16–18 rebuilds the Michael shrine, torn down last week, into “a marvellously beautiful building,” a rectangle whose “length appears not much greater than the width.” Official measurements are not invented. I.4 remains reserved.
