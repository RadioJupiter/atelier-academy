Latin · The future perfect of ēgredior, a first look

40 min · Method: ēgressus erō, eris, erit, erimus, eritis, erunt; contrasted with ēgrediar, ēgressus sum, and secūtus erō; original sentences

Learn

Key terms

future perfect of ēgredior
Definition
The future perfect of ēgredior is ēgressus erō, ēgressus eris, ēgressus erit, ēgressī erimus, ēgressī eritis, ēgressī erunt. It marks a going out that will already be completed at some later time. It is not the future ēgrediar, not the perfect ēgressus sum, and not secūtus erō.
Example
In an original sentence, Puella ēgressa erit: she will have gone out. The going out will already be completed at the later time in view.
Not this
Not ēgrediar, I shall go out, still ahead. Not ēgressus sum, I went out, or I have gone out. Not ēgressus eram, I had gone out. Not secūtus erō, yesterday’s future perfect of sequor, a different verb.
Connects to
Yesterday’s future perfect of sequor, and the already-taught tenses of ēgredior.

Ēgredior already has a present, a future ēgrediar, a perfect ēgressus sum, and a pluperfect ēgressus eram. Today the future perfect: ēgressus erō, I shall have gone out; ēgressus eris; ēgressus erit; ēgressī erimus; ēgressī eritis; ēgressī erunt. The participle agrees. The tense marks a going out that will already be completed at some later time. It is not ēgrediar, and not ēgressus sum. It is also not secūtus erō, yesterday’s future perfect of sequor; the two verbs must not be swapped. The sentences are original, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the gloss.

Mathematics · Geometry LXXXVIII: the two-triangle case of SSA, with exact invented sides

40 min · Method: invented A = 30°, b = 2, a = √2; sin B = 1/√2; B = 45° or 135°; two triangles

Learn

Key terms (from earlier lessons)

  • the ambiguous case (SSA) — Day 146

Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.

Yesterday, on invented figures with acute A, a was compared with h = b sin A. Today the case h < a < b, which gives two triangles. On invented figures, A = 30°, b = 2, and a = √2. Then h = 1, and 1 < √2 < 2, so there are two triangles. The figures are invented, not official measurements.

The law of sines gives sin B / b = sin A / a, so sin B = b sin A / a = 2 × (1/2) / √2 = 1/√2. So B is 45°, or 180° − 45° = 135°. Both can occur, because a is shorter than b, and B may be acute or obtuse. If B = 45°, then C = 180° − 30° − 45° = 105°. If B = 135°, then C = 180° − 30° − 135° = 15°. There are two different triangles: 30°-45°-105°, and 30°-135°-15°. Taking only 45° and ignoring 135° is a miss. 30° is also π/6, and 45° is also π/4, already taught; the work today is kept in degrees.

Remember

On invented figures with A = 30°, b = 2, and a = √2, there are two triangles, because 1 < √2 < 2. B is 45° or 135°, so the triangles are 30°-45°-105° and 30°-135°-15°.

Key terms

Ohm’s law
Definition
In OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 20.2, Ohm’s law is the observed relation that the current through most substances is directly proportional to the voltage applied: I ∝ V. Resistance R is defined so that I = V / R, and the unit of resistance is the ohm, Ω, with 1 Ω = 1 V/A. Official Example 20.4, the automobile headlight, waits until Thursday. Official household voltages remain reserved.
