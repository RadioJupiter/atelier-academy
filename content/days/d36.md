## Mathematics · Geometry XVII: the tangent-chord angle (Euclid III.32) {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: a picture, then a proof from facts already taught (radius to a tangent; inscribed and central angles)</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-alternate-segment-iii32">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="alternate segment theorem (III.32)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear alternate segment theorem (III.32)">Hear</button> alternate segment theorem (III.32)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The angle between a tangent and a chord, at the point of contact, equals the inscribed angle in the alternate segment of the circle (the segment on the other side of the chord). This is Euclid, Elements, Book III, Proposition 32.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a tangent at A meets a chord AB, and C is a point on the circle in the segment of the chord that does not lie against that tangent-chord angle, then the tangent-chord angle equals ∠ACB.</dd></div>

**The fact.** A tangent at A and a chord AB form an angle (marked 1 in the figure). That angle equals the inscribed angle in the **alternate segment** (marked 2: ∠ACB). “Alternate” means the segment of the circle on the other side of the chord from angle 1.

**A proof, from what you already have.**

1. The radius OA is perpendicular to the tangent at A (Day 19).
2. OA = OB (both radii), so in triangle OAB the base angles are equal. Let ∠OAB = α. Then ∠OBA = α, and the central angle ∠AOB = 180° − 2α (Day 6, angle sum).
3. The tangent-chord angle is 90° − α, because it sits next to α in the right angle between tangent and radius.
4. The inscribed angle in the alternate segment, standing on the same arc AB, is half the central angle (Day 21, III.20): ½(180° − 2α) = 90° − α.

The two angles are equal. That is III.32. The figure’s numbers 1 and 2 are those two angles.

**A near-miss.** The inscribed angle on the *same* side as the tangent-chord angle is a different angle; the theorem names the alternate segment, not “any angle in the figure.”

## Physics · A first look at gravitation: an inverse-square force {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: the qualitative inverse-square pattern, then Newton’s formula with G named as a standard constant, not a classroom measurement</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-newton-gravitation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Newton’s law of gravitation (first look)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Newton’s law of gravitation (first look)">Hear</button> Newton’s law of gravitation (first look)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Every two masses attract each other with a force along the line joining them. The size of that force is F = G Mm / r², where M and m are the two masses, r is the distance between their centers (in this first look, treated as particles or as spheres), and G is the universal gravitational constant.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If the distance r doubles, and the masses stay the same, the force becomes one fourth as large, because of the r² in the denominator. If one mass doubles, the force doubles.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a classroom measurement of G, and not a new value of g as if it were G. g (already used as 9.8 N/kg near the Earth) is the gravitational field, or the acceleration, near the Earth; G is the constant in the general formula. This lesson does not invent an orbital speed.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Power and torque last week were mechanical rates and turning measures. Today’s force is a different idea: an attraction that grows weaker with distance as 1/r².</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-inverse-square-gravitation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inverse-square (gravitation)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inverse-square (gravitation)">Hear</button> inverse-square (gravitation)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A pattern in which a quantity is proportional to 1/r²: if r is multiplied by 2, the quantity is multiplied by 1/4; if r is multiplied by 3, the quantity is multiplied by 1/9.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Three times as far from a mass, with nothing else changed, the gravitational force is one ninth as large.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “half as far, twice the force” (that would be a simple inverse, 1/r, not 1/r²), and not a claim about sound or light in this lesson.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The formula F = G Mm / r² is the inverse-square pattern, with the two masses in the numerator.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The formula, as a first look.**

**F = G Mm / r²**

- *M* and *m* are the two masses.
- *r* is the distance between centers (in this first model).
- *G* is the gravitational constant. This course uses the standard value **G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²**, labeled as a standard constant, **not** as a number you measured in class.

**The pattern to practice.** Keep the masses fixed and change *r*:

- r → 2r, then F → F/4
- r → 3r, then F → F/9
- r → (1/2)r, then F → 4F

## Logic · A first look at the undistributed middle {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: contrast Barbara with one invalid form that looks like a syllogism</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-undistributed-middle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="undistributed middle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear undistributed middle">Hear</button> undistributed middle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An invalid syllogistic form in which the middle term never refers to the whole of its class in either premise, so it does not actually link the other two terms. A stock example: All dogs are mammals; all cats are mammals; therefore all cats are dogs. The middle term “mammals” is not distributed in either premise.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>“All squares are rectangles; all rhombi are rectangles; therefore all rhombi are squares.” That is the same invalid form: “rectangles” is not used in its whole-class sense in either premise.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not Barbara (All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P), in which the middle term is distributed in the first premise (“All M…”). And not a claim that every argument with a repeated word is invalid.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 6 taught validity and two propositional fallacies (affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent). Today’s fallacy belongs to the syllogism, after last week’s Barbara. “Distributed” here means, in this first look, that the term is used of the whole class (as in “All M…”).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Barbara, recalled.** All M are P. All S are M. Therefore all S are P. The middle term M is used as a **whole class** in “All M are P.” That is what lets it link S to P.

**An invalid look-alike.** All S are M. All P are M. Therefore all S are P. Here M appears in both premises, but always as the predicate of an A-sentence: “All … are M.” In this first look, that use does **not** speak of **all** M, so M is **undistributed**. The two classes S and P may both sit inside M without being the same.

Geometry you already know can host the same form, wrongly: All squares are rectangles; all rhombi are rectangles; therefore all rhombi are squares. Both premises can be true (in this course’s geometry, with a rhombus that is not a square), and the conclusion is false. So the form is invalid.

This lesson does not teach a full theory of distribution for E, I, and O. It teaches one named failure, in contrast to Barbara.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

The undistributed middle is an invalid form: the middle term never covers its whole class, so it does not link S to P. Barbara is different: “All M are P” distributes M. Day 6’s propositional fallacies are a different family.


<p>Key terms: undistributed middle.</p>

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Write the stock mammals example, mark the middle term, and say why it is not Barbara.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>All dogs are mammals. All cats are mammals. Therefore all cats are dogs.</li>
