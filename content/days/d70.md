This sitting uses the week’s jobs: the Pythagorean theorem, a similar-triangle proof from an altitude to the hypotenuse, a missing side, and the converse. There is no new theorem today.

In a right triangle, *a*² + *b*² = *c*², with *c* the hypotenuse. If the three sides of a triangle already satisfy that equality, the converse says the triangle is right-angled opposite *c*. 3-4-5 is one integer triple, not the only right triangle.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

c² = a² + b² in a right triangle. A missing side is found from squares, not by adding the sides. If a² + b² = c², the triangle is right (the converse). 3-4-5 is not the only example.

</div>

Last week named the three long bones of the leg: **femur**, **tibia**, and **fibula**. Today names the three long bones of the **arm**: the **humerus** in the upper arm, and the **radius** and **ulna** in the forearm.

The humerus is a **single** bone, from the shoulder to the elbow. Below the elbow, **two** bones run to the wrist. In a palm-forward view, the **radius** is on the **thumb side**, and the **ulna** is on the **little-finger side**. The ulna also makes the point of the elbow at its upper end. This first look uses that palm-forward position; the way the radius crosses the ulna when the hand is turned is a later job, not today’s.

A simple classroom diagram of the bones is enough. No person is identified, and no muscle count or invented measurement is added. The names are the standard names of the bones, not a quotation from a long anatomical treatise.


</div>

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>A model answer for a near-miss item, so the others can be written in the same shape.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Question: What is the Pythagorean theorem, and why is “every right triangle is a 3-4-5 triangle” a miss?</li>
<li>In a right triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the two legs: a² + b² = c², with c the side opposite the right angle.</li>
<li>3-4-5 is one integer example (9 + 16 = 25). 5-12-13 is another, and a triangle with legs 1 and 1 has hypotenuse √2, which is not an integer. The theorem is not a claim that every right triangle is 3-4-5.</li>
<li>A near-miss is to say “the sides add, so 3 + 4 = 7 is the hypotenuse,” or to treat 3-5-6 as right without testing the squares.</li>
</ol>

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. If a² + b² = c², the triangle is right-angled opposite c. 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13², so a 5-12-13 triangle is right-angled opposite 13.
2. The possessor is in the dative, the thing possessed in the nominative, with a form of esse: for example, Mārcō gladius est.
3. 72.74 mN/m, with an uncertainty of 0.36 mN/m.
4. Longfellow: “And lo! almost where the ascent began, / A panther light and swift exceedingly, / Which with a spotted skin was covered o’er!” Dewing: “In our own age there has been born the Emperor Justinian.”
5. comí; viví. They share the regular -er / -ir yo ending -í. The regular -ar yo form is hablé, with -é.
6. Darii is AII: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The conclusion stays particular; “all S are P” would claim more than the particular minor gives.
7. I = P × r × t, on the original principal only; the amount is P + I. The rate in a classroom example is invented, not an official rate at a named bank.
8. Humerus: the single bone of the upper arm (shoulder to elbow). Radius: the forearm bone on the thumb side, in a palm-forward view. Ulna: the forearm bone on the little-finger side, which forms the point of the elbow.
