A **quadratic inequality** asks where a quadratic is positive, negative, or zero. We already graph y = ax² + bx + c and we already find roots. Today we read the inequality from that graph.

**The method, when the quadratic factors (or the roots are already known).**

1. Write one side as zero: for example (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0, which is y < 0 for y = (x − 2)(x − 6).
2. The roots split the number line. Here the roots are 2 and 6.
3. Look at the already-taught shape. If a > 0, the parabola opens upward: it is below the x-axis *between* the roots, and above the x-axis *outside* them. If a < 0, those regions swap.
4. A strict inequality (< or >) does not include the roots (there y = 0). A ≤ or ≥ does include them.

**A worked shape.** y = (x − 2)(x − 6) = x² − 8x + 12 opens upward. y < 0 between the roots: **2 < x < 6**. y > 0 to the left of 2 and to the right of 6: x < 2 or x > 6. The pictured shading is the < 0 region.

**What we will not do today.** We will not invent a root we have not found, and we will not solve a quadratic inequality by “moving pieces” as if it were linear (for example, we will not jump from (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0 to “x − 2 < 0 and x − 6 < 0” as a complete method: both factors negative is only one of the cases, and it is not the region between the roots).

**The computation.**

G M = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.42 × 10²³ = 4.28 × 10¹³ (three significant figures).  
R² = (3.396 × 10⁶)² = 1.153 × 10¹³.  
g = 4.28 × 10¹³ / 1.153 × 10¹³ ≈ **3.71 m/s²**, which we report as **about 3.7 m/s²**, the same two-figure value the fact sheet lists. G and M do not support a tighter official figure here.

## Logic · Disjunctive syllogism, and an inclusive “or” {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: a valid “or” form, beside last week’s conditional forms; inclusive “or” as the default unless a problem says exactly one</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-disjunctive-syllogism">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="disjunctive syllogism" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear disjunctive syllogism">Hear</button> disjunctive syllogism</h5>

<article class="term" id="term-inclusive-or-in-a-first-look">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inclusive or, in a first look" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inclusive or, in a first look">Hear</button> inclusive or, in a first look</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An “or” that is true when at least one side is true, including the case where both are true. This is the default in this course unless a problem says exactly one (an exclusive “or”).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>“The figure has four right angles or it has four equal sides” can be true of a square, which has both. From “it has four right angles” you may not infer that it lacks four equal sides.</dd></div>

Last week taught two **valid** forms that use a conditional: modus ponens and modus tollens. Today is a different valid form, which uses **or**. Necessary and sufficient conditions were already taught on Day 16; they are not a first look today.

**Disjunctive syllogism.** P or Q. Not P. Therefore Q. (Or: P or Q; not Q; therefore P.) From the “or” and the denial of one side, you may take the other side.

**The default “or.”** In this course, “or” is **inclusive** unless a problem says *exactly one*. Inclusive “or” is true when at least one side is true, including both. So from P you may **not** infer not-Q. That second move would need an exclusive “or,” and only when the problem has said so.

**A worked invented pair.** A labeled invented box holds a square or a circle (or, under the default, possibly a description that could allow both, but here the two shapes are alternatives in the invented pair). The figure is not a square. Therefore it is a circle. That is disjunctive syllogism.

**A near-miss.** The figure is a square. Therefore it is not a circle — *only if* the problem has said exactly one. Under the inclusive default, that second sentence is not given.

We will not begin a full catalogue of exclusive-or symbols today, and we will not treat everyday English “or” as always exclusive.
