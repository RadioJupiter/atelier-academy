## Mathematics · Geometry XVI: loci — a circle, and the perpendicular bisector {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: a concrete string or a fold, then the definition of a locus as a set of points satisfying a condition</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-locus">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="locus" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear locus">Hear</button> locus</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The set of all points that satisfy a stated condition. (The plural is often written loci.) From Latin locus, “place.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The locus of points at a fixed distance r from a point O is the circle with center O and radius r.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a single point chosen at random, and not a graph of a function unless that graph is being described as a set of points (x, f(x)). A locus is named by a condition, not by a plotted table alone.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The circle as a set of points was already used informally in the circle chapters. Today the same idea is named, and the perpendicular bisector is recovered as a locus.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-perp-bisector-locus">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="perpendicular bisector as a locus" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear perpendicular bisector as a locus">Hear</button> perpendicular bisector as a locus</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The locus of points equidistant from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of the segment AB.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If P is on the perpendicular bisector of AB, then PA = PB. Conversely, if PA = PB, then P lies on that bisector.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “any line through the midpoint,” and not the circle with diameter AB (that is a different locus: the angle in a semicircle, already taught, is a property of points on that circle, not this equidistance condition).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The construction of the perpendicular bisector (compass arcs from A and from B with the same opening) is exactly a way of locating points equidistant from A and B.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**A locus is a set of points described by a condition.** Two conditions you already have the pictures for:

1. **A circle:** all points at a fixed distance r from a fixed center O. That is the definition of the circle, now named as a locus.
2. **The perpendicular bisector of AB:** all points P such that PA = PB. A fold of the paper that takes A to B has that line as its crease. The compass construction — the same opening from A and from B — finds two such points, and the line through them is the whole locus.

**A first contrast.** The circle with diameter AB is a different set of points (points from which AB subtends a right angle, Euclid III.31). Do not call that circle “the locus PA = PB.”

**A classroom string.** If one end of a string is held at O and a pencil is held at a fixed length, the path is a circle, the locus of “distance r from O.” If the string is slack, the condition has failed, and the path is not that locus.


</div>

## Science · From the retina to a signal {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: continue the path honestly, without invented cell counts</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-optic-nerve">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="optic nerve" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear optic nerve">Hear</button> optic nerve</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The nerve that carries a signal from the retina toward the brain. In this first look, it is named as the path of that signal, not as a counted bundle of fibers.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Light reaches the retina; a signal then leaves by the optic nerve. This lesson does not invent a travel time or a fiber count.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the cornea, and not a second “eye” at the back. It is the path out, not the receiving layer.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The camera analogy now fails in a useful way: a camera’s cable is manufactured; the optic nerve is living tissue, and there is a place on the retina where that nerve leaves — the blind spot, named below.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-blind-spot">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="blind spot" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear blind spot">Hear</button> blind spot</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A place on the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eye, and where there are no light-sensitive receptors, so no image is received there.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Each eye has a blind spot. You do not ordinarily notice it, because the two eyes and the brain fill in the gap; that “filling in” is named, not explained in detail, today.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a dark patch you should see in ordinary vision, and not a disease. It is a normal consequence of the nerve’s exit.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is a limit of the camera analogy: a manufactured sensor need not have a cable-exit gap of this kind. The eye does.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


The path continues: the **retina** receives the image; a signal leaves by the **optic nerve**. Where that nerve exits, there is a **blind spot** — a place with no receptors. We do not invent a count of rods or cones, a measured size of the blind spot, or a travel time for the signal. Those numbers, if they appear in other books, are not today’s lesson unless a later, sourced lesson sets them.

The useful conclusion is small and honest: seeing is not only “a picture on the back of the eye.” A signal must leave, and the receiving surface has a gap where that happens.
