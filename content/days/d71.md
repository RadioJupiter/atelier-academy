A year, in Latin, is **annus, -ī**, masculine. A month is **mēnsis, mēnsis**, masculine, a third-declension noun. A year has twelve months: *Annus duodecim mēnsēs habet.*

The twelve names, in order, are **Iānuārius, Februārius, Mārtius, Aprīlis, Maius, Iūnius, Iūlius, Augustus, September, October, November, December.** They are used as adjectives when they agree with a named day later this week. For today, learn the names and the two nouns.

Allen and Greenough §630 notes that the month once called **Quīntīlis** received the name **Iūlius**, and **Sextīlis** was called **Augustus**. This lesson does not invent a story around that renaming; it records the grammar book’s note so that Iūlius and Augustus are not treated as if they had always been the names.

## Mathematics · Geometry XXX: the area of a triangle {#math}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a triangle as half a rectangle; A = ½bh, with h perpendicular</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-area-of-a-triangle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="area of a triangle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear area of a triangle">Hear</button> area of a triangle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The number of unit squares a triangle covers. A triangle is half of a rectangle that has the same base and the same perpendicular height, so its area is one-half of base times height: A = ½bh, where h is perpendicular to the base.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A triangle with base 8 and perpendicular height 5 has area ½ × 8 × 5 = 20.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not one-half of base times a slanted side. The height must meet the base at a right angle. The area is also not the perimeter.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Tomorrow a height that is not given will be found, when the figure allows it, by last week’s Pythagorean theorem. Circle area remains reserved.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="perpendicular height" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear perpendicular height">Hear</button> perpendicular height</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The distance from a vertex of a triangle to the line of the opposite side, measured along a line that meets that side at a right angle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In the figure with base AB = 8 and vertex C, the dashed line from C to D on AB is the height only if angle at D is right.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a slanted side from C to A or C to B, even if that side is labeled with a length.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is the same idea as an altitude, already used last week to the hypotenuse. Here it is used to find area, not to prove the Pythagorean theorem again.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

## Writing & Rhetoric · Informal fallacies: a first look {#writing}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a fallacy is a flaw in reasoning, not merely a false claim</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-fallacy">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="fallacy" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear fallacy">Hear</button> fallacy</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A flaw in the reasoning of an argument: the support does not actually establish the claim in the way the argument pretends, even if some statement in it happens to be true, or happens to be false for another reason.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English, from Old French fallace, from Latin fallācia, “deception, deceit,” from fallāx, from fallere, “to deceive.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>An invented example: “This triangle has sides 3, 5, and 6, and I like the number 6, so it is a right triangle.” Liking 6 does not establish the claim; the squares should be checked (9 + 25 = 34, not 36).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not merely a false claim, and not a typo or a wrong calculation by itself. A true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacious route, and a false claim can be false without this week’s named patterns.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s précis restates a passage faithfully. This week asks whether an argument’s reasoning holds. Week 13’s warrant is the step that a fallacy often fakes or skips.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


A **fallacy** is a flaw in the **reasoning**. The support does not actually establish the claim in the way the argument pretends.

That is not the same thing as a **false claim**. A claim can be false because a fact is wrong, without this week’s named patterns. A claim can even be true, and the route to it can still be fallacious: the conclusion was not earned by the reasons given.

An invented example, labeled invented:

> “This triangle has sides 3, 5, and 6, and I like the number 6, so it is a right triangle.”

Liking the number 6 is not a warrant that the triangle is right. Last week’s converse is the test that was skipped: 3² + 5² = 9 + 25 = 34, which is not 6² = 36. The argument is fallacious, and (in this case) the claim is also false. The two facts are distinct.

A second invented example: “6-8-10 is a right triangle because I said so first.” Being first to speak is not a reason. The squares still have to be checked (they do hold: 36 + 64 = 100). The conclusion happens to be true; the reasoning is still a fallacy.

<p class="note">The classroom arguments in this week’s writing are invented examples, labeled invented. They are for practicing the method. No contemporary political case is used as the set example. A précis, already taught last week, is not retaught as a first look.</p>

## Physics · Stable, unstable, and neutral equilibrium {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: classify rest by what a small displacement does</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<div class="keyterms">

<article class="term" id="term-stable-equilibrium">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="stable equilibrium" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear stable equilibrium">Hear</button> stable equilibrium</h5>

Last week an object at rest could tip, or not, according to whether a vertical line from the center of mass fell inside the base of support. That test still holds. Today adds three names for **kinds of rest**, according to what a **small displacement** does.

**Stable equilibrium:** a small nudge leads to a restoring effect; the object tends to return. **Unstable equilibrium:** a small nudge leads to a further move away. **Neutral equilibrium:** a small nudge leads to a new rest nearby, without a tendency to return or to run away.

## Elective · Logic: Ferio, a first-figure EIO syllogism {#seminar}

<p class="meta">25 minutes · Method: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-ferio">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Ferio" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Ferio">Hear</button> Ferio</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A valid first-figure syllogism of the form EIO: no M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are not P. The major premise is universal negative, the minor particular affirmative, and the conclusion particular negative.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>No whales are insects; some sea animals are whales; therefore some sea animals are not insects.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not Darii (AII), already taught, and not Celarent (EAE), already taught. Ferio’s conclusion stays particular and negative: “some S are not P,” not “no S are P,” which the particular minor does not give.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Barbara, Celarent, and Darii have already been taught as first-figure forms. Ferio is the remaining standard first-figure form of that set. Informal fallacies, taught this week in writing, are a different kind of flaw, not a fourth syllogism.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Last week taught **Darii** (AII): all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. Earlier, **Celarent** (EAE) and **Barbara** were taught. Today is the remaining standard first-figure form in that set: **Ferio** (EIO).

- Major (E): **No M are P.**
- Minor (I): **Some S are M.**
- Conclusion (O): **Some S are not P.**

The middle term M still joins the premises. The conclusion stays **particular** and **negative**. The particular minor does not license “no S are P,” which would be Celarent’s kind of conclusion with a universal minor that Ferio does not have.

An invented example, for the form only: no whales are insects; some sea animals are whales; therefore some sea animals are not insects.

Do not turn the conclusion into a universal, and do not swap EIO for AII (Darii) or EAE (Celarent).
