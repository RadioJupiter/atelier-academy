## Mathematics · Geometry LXVIII: a line perpendicular to a plane, a first look {#math}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: a line perpendicular to two intersecting lines in the plane through the foot</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-a-line-perpendicular-to-a-plane">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="a line perpendicular to a plane" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear a line perpendicular to a plane">Hear</button> a line perpendicular to a plane</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A line is perpendicular to a plane if it meets the plane at a point P, the foot, and is perpendicular to every line in the plane that passes through P. In practice it is enough, and it is the method used today, to check two intersecting lines in the plane through P: if the given line is perpendicular to both, it is perpendicular to the plane.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On the invented rectangular box, the vertical edge DA meets the floor at A and is perpendicular to the two floor edges AB and AE that meet at A, so DA is perpendicular to the floor plane.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a line that merely meets the plane at one point, which may be oblique. Not perpendicular only to one line in the plane. Not an official claim about a real wall being square.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s three relations of a line and a plane. Two planes in space wait until tomorrow.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<p>Yesterday a line could meet a plane at one point without being perpendicular to it; that meeting may be oblique. Today a first look at the perpendicular case. If a line meets a plane at P, and is perpendicular to two lines in the plane that intersect at P, then it is perpendicular to every line in the plane through P, and we say the line is perpendicular to the plane. P is called the foot.</p>

## Science · Ochre, from rock to pigment, a first look {#science}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: USGS sources on earthy iron-oxide pigments; hematite red, hydrated oxides yellow to brown</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-ochre">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ochre" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ochre">Hear</button> ochre</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Ochre, as the term is used in USGS mineral sources, is an earthy, often clayey form of an iron-oxide mineral used as a pigment after milling and only minimal processing. Hematite gives red ochres; limonite or goethite, which are hydrated iron oxides, give yellow, buff, or brown ochres. Impurities also influence the shade.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Greek ōkhra, from ōkhros, pale yellow, through Latin ochra and Old French ocre (Etymonline). The further origin of the Greek word is unknown.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A yellow earth, ground and used as a paint, whose color comes from a hydrated iron oxide, is a yellow ochre in this sense.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a synthetic modern convenience color from a catalog, and not lapis lazuli, which remains reserved. Not an official iron percentage invented for a classroom lump of earth.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Term 3 science begins the module From rock to pigment. Hematite, as the red anhydrous oxide, is Thursday’s first look. The limited palette in the atelier uses yellow ochre as one of four paints.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>Term 3 science begins the module <em>From rock to pigment</em>. The first look is ochre, an earth pigment, not a synthetic catalog color. The U.S. Geological Survey, in the 2019 Minerals Yearbook article on iron oxide pigments (Amanda S. Brioche), and in the earlier Bulletin 430-G on mineral paints, treats natural iron-oxide pigments as minerals that can be used as colors after milling and minimal processing. Hematite (Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) is the usual red; goethite or limonite, hydrated iron oxides, vary from yellow to brown and include ochres. Bulletin 430-G says, as ordinarily used, “ocher” is applied to the earthy and pulverulent forms of hematite and limonite, made more or less impure by other oxides and by clay, and that hematites give red ochers while limonites give yellow, buff, or brown. The natural color depends on hydration, oxidation, and impurities.</p>
<p>Invent no official iron percentage for a classroom lump, and do not treat a store paint name as a mineral identification. Lapis lazuli and ultramarine remain reserved. Thursday takes hematite more closely, including the already-noted fact from Bulletin 430-G that a red ocher can be made by calcining common yellow ocher.</p>
