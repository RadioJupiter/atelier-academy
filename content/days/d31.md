<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-function">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="function" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear function">Hear</button> function</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A rule that assigns to each allowed input exactly one output. We write y = f(x), or simply f(x), for the output when the input is x.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-f-of-x">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="f(x)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear f(x)">Hear</button> f(x)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Function notation: the output of f when the input is x. To evaluate f(a), replace x by a in the rule.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If g(x) = x² − 1, then g(3) = 8. The 3 is the input, not a multiplier sitting in front of g.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not g times x, and not a request to solve g(x) = 0 unless that is asked. f(x) is the output, not automatically a root.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The parentheses mark the input. Tomorrow the same rule will be drawn as a graph.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**A function is a rule with exactly one output for each allowed input.** A table, a machine with an in-slot and an out-slot, or a formula can all present the same rule. If one input were given two different outputs, the rule would not be a function of that input.

**Notation.** We name the rule f (or g, or h) and write **f(x)** for the output when the input is x. If f(x) = 2x + 3, then f(0) = 3, f(4) = 11, and f(−1) = 1. The letter x is a placeholder; f(4) means “use 4 in the rule,” not “f times 4.”

**A first restriction, honestly named.** Today the “allowed inputs” are the numbers we are willing to put into the rule (for 2x + 3, any number we can compute with). A later course will call that the domain; we do not need the formal word to evaluate f(4). We will not pretend that every formula is a function of x if a later graph would fail the vertical-line test — that test belongs with tomorrow’s graphs.

## Physics · Power: work over time {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: the definition of power from work and time already taught, with units named honestly</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-power-mechanics">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="power (mechanics)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear power (mechanics)">Hear</button> power (mechanics)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The rate at which work is done: power is work divided by the time in which that work is done. In symbols, P = W / t, when W is work and t is the time taken.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a force does 200 J of work in 4 s, the average power is 200 J / 4 s = 50 W (50 joules per second).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the everyday sense of “a powerful person,” and not the same word as the exponent in algebra (x²). In this lesson, power is a mechanical rate.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Work and the joule were already taught. Today the new idea is the rate.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-watt">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="watt" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear watt">Hear</button> watt</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The SI unit of power: one watt is one joule per second. The unit is named after James Watt.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 50 W rate means 50 J of work each second, on average, over the interval you measured.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a unit of energy (that is the joule), and not a unit of force (that is the newton). A watt is a rate.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The name is a person-name unit, like the newton and the joule already taught: capital W for the symbol, and a lowercase unit name in running text (watt, watts).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Work is a transfer of energy by a force, measured in joules. **Power** is how quickly that work is done:

**P = W / t**

The unit is the **watt** (W): 1 W = 1 J/s. The name honors James Watt. You may compute an average power over an interval even when the rate was not steady; if the lesson does not give a varying rate, treat the given W and t as that interval’s totals.

A larger power does not always mean a larger force. A smaller force can do the same work more slowly, and then the power is smaller.


</div>

A short, conventional timeline (dates BCE, with the usual caution that ancient dates can be argued):

- **31 BCE:** Octavian (later Augustus) defeats Antony and Cleopatra at **Actium**. This ends the last great civil war of the period; it is a battle, not yet the later settlement of powers.

## Logic · The syllogism: a first look at Barbara {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: one valid form (Barbara) taught explicitly, after last week’s A, E, I, and O</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-syllogism">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="syllogism" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear syllogism">Hear</button> syllogism</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A form of argument with two premises and a conclusion, in which a middle term links the other two terms. The word comes from Latin syllogismus, from Greek συλλογισμός (a putting-together of thoughts).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>All rectangles have four sides. All squares are rectangles. Therefore all squares have four sides. The middle term is rectangle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a single if–then sentence (that was a conditional, already taught), and not any two sentences followed by a ‘therefore.’ If the middle term does not link the other two, the form has failed.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s A-sentences (“All S are P”) are the materials. Today we use them in one named form, not in a complete system of all syllogisms.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-barbara-first-look">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Barbara (first look)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Barbara (first look)">Hear</button> Barbara (first look)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The traditional name for one valid syllogism in the first figure: All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P. All three sentences are A-form (the vowels in the name Barbara are a reminder of that).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>All mammals are animals (All M are P). All dogs are mammals (All S are M). Therefore all dogs are animals (All S are P).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a license to rearrange the premises at will and keep the same validity without checking, and not a claim that every everyday argument is a Barbara. Other moods and figures are not taught as valid today merely by being named.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The middle term (M) appears in both premises and not in the conclusion. If you lose the middle term, you no longer have this form.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


A **syllogism**, in this first look, has two premises and a conclusion. One term — the **middle term** — appears in both premises and not in the conclusion.

**Barbara** (a traditional name) is this form, with three A-sentences:

1. All M are P.
2. All S are M.
3. Therefore all S are P.

The first premise relates the middle term to P; the second relates S to the middle term; the conclusion relates S to P. You may change the everyday content; you may not drop the middle term or change the three A’s and still call the result Barbara.

This lesson does not teach a full list of valid moods. If an argument is not in this form, we do not declare it valid today just because it sounds formal.
