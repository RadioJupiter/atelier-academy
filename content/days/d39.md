## Mathematics · Algebra XII: the inverse of a linear function {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: undo the operations in reverse order, then check by composition; a first look at the graph as a reflection in y = x</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-inverse-linear-function">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inverse of a linear function" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inverse of a linear function">Hear</button> inverse of a linear function</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The function that undoes a given linear function. If f(x) = mx + b with m ≠ 0, the inverse is f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m. The notation f⁻¹ is not a power; it names the inverse function. The word inverse here is from Latin inversus, from invertere, “to turn.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin inversus, from invertere, “to turn.” In this lesson the word names a function that undoes another, not the inverse of a conditional.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f⁻¹(x) = (x − 1)/2. Check: f(3) = 7, and f⁻¹(7) = 3. Also f(f⁻¹(x)) = x and f⁻¹(f(x)) = x.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the logic inverse of a conditional (Day 21: the inverse of “If P, then Q” is “If not P, then not Q”). And not 1/f(x), the reciprocal. And not an inverse for a quadratic in this lesson (a quadratic is not one-to-one on the whole line).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A linear function with m ≠ 0 has exactly one output for each input, and each output comes from exactly one input, so it can be undone. If m = 0, the rule is constant and cannot be inverted as a function of x.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Undo, in reverse order.** If f(x) = 2x + 1, the rule is: multiply by 2, then add 1. To undo: subtract 1, then divide by 2. So **f⁻¹(x) = (x − 1)/2**.

In general, if **f(x) = mx + b** and **m ≠ 0**,

**f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m**.

**The check.** The two functions undo each other: f(f⁻¹(x)) = x and f⁻¹(f(x)) = x. Always run this check.

**A first look at the graph.** The point (0, 1) on y = 2x + 1 is reflected to (1, 0) on the inverse, across the dashed line **y = x**. That reflection is a picture of undoing: input and output change places. Today we use the picture as a check, not as a full theory of inverse graphs.

**Two near-misses.** f⁻¹ is not a power (not 1/f). And this “inverse” is not the logic inverse of a conditional, already taught under the same English word.

## Science · Two eyes, and a first look at judging distance {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: two slightly different views, named honestly, without invented measurements of the space between the eyes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<article class="term" id="term-two-views-binocular">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="two views (binocular, first look)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear two views (binocular, first look)">Hear</button> two views (binocular, first look)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The two eyes, set a little apart, receive slightly different views of a near scene. That difference is a first help in judging distance. This lesson does not invent a measured distance between the eyes, or a calculated angle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A near pencil, seen first with one eye and then with the other, appears to jump against a far wall. The jump is a classroom sign that the two views are not the same.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a claim that a person with one eye cannot judge distance at all (other cues remain), and not a full theory of three-dimensional vision. Today is a first look at one cue.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s blind spot is still there in each eye. Two eyes also help us not to notice a single blind spot in ordinary looking; that is a related fact, not a new measurement.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Two views.** Your eyes are a little apart, so each has a slightly different picture of a near object. That difference is one cue the brain can use in **judging distance**. We will not invent a number for the space between the eyes, or a measured angle.

**A simple check.** Hold a finger in front of a far wall. Close one eye, then the other. The finger appears to shift. That shift is the two views, made visible. It is a qualitative check, not a measurement.

**A limit.** People can also judge distance with other cues (size, overlap, motion). This lesson does not say that two eyes are the only way, and it does not invent a percentage of “how much” of distance-judging they supply.

The blind spot of each eye (Day 34) is also easier to ignore when both eyes are open. That is named, not measured, today.
