<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="AcI with esse" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear AcI with esse">Hear</button> AcI with esse</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The same accusative-and-infinitive construction, with esse as the infinitive. A predicate word that describes the accusative subject is itself accusative: Putō corpus sānum esse, “I think that the body is healthy.”</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s AcI after vidēre, dīcere, and putāre is still the construction. Today the infinitive is esse, which you already know, and the predicate follows the accusative.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>The same construction works when the second verb is <em>esse</em>. The person or thing you are talking about still stands in the accusative, and <em>esse</em> is the infinitive.</p>
<p>A describing word that belongs with that accusative — a predicate adjective — is also accusative, and it agrees in gender and number: <em>Putō corpus sānum esse.</em> I think that the body is healthy. <em>Dīcō caput magnum esse.</em> I say that the head is large. <em>Puella crūs longum esse dīcit.</em> The girl says that the leg is long.</p>
<p>Keep this apart from the ordinary sentence <em>Corpus sānum est</em>, which has a nominative subject and a finite verb. Once you are inside an AcI, you do not go back to <em>est</em> and a nominative.</p>


</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

After putō or dīcō, esse takes an accusative subject, and a predicate adjective is also accusative: Putō corpus sānum esse. This is not the same as Corpus sānum est.

<p>Similar triangles are a tool for an indirect measurement: a height you cannot stand a tape against, found from a shadow you can measure, or from a second, smaller object standing in the same light.</p>
<p>A pole 2 m tall casts a 3 m shadow. A tree, nearby, casts a 9 m shadow in the same light. The sun’s rays are parallel, so the two right triangles — object, ground, ray — have the same angles, and they are similar by AA. The proportion is 2/3 = h/9, so h = 6 m.</p>
<p>The numbers here are a labeled classroom example. The method is the same whenever the light is the same and the two objects stand upright on level ground. Today does not use a new named theorem about right triangles; it uses AA and a scale factor, which you already have.</p>


</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

## Science · Water: the triple point, and the ice point at one atmosphere {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: the unique three-phase point, set beside Tuesday’s ice point at 1 atm</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-triple-point">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="triple point" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear triple point">Hear</button> triple point</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The unique pressure and temperature at which the solid, liquid, and vapor of a substance all coexist in equilibrium. For water, this point is exactly 273.16 K on ITS-90, which is 0.01 °C. It is not the same condition as the ice point at one atmosphere.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>At the triple point of water, ice, liquid water, and water vapor are all present together, at 0.01 °C (273.16 K). That is a different pressure from one atmosphere, and a slightly different temperature from the familiar ice point of about 0 °C at 1 atm.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the melting point at one atmosphere, and not the boiling point at one atmosphere. Those are two-phase points at a stated ordinary pressure. The triple point is the unique three-phase condition. Do not treat 0.01 °C as a typo for 0 °C, or treat them as two names for the same point.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Tuesday’s ice point and boiling point were at one atmosphere. The IAPWS FAQ is again the source: the triple point is the unique condition where solid, liquid, and vapor coexist, and on ITS-90 it is exactly 273.16 K.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>Tuesday’s figures were for one atmosphere: the familiar ice point, about 0 °C, and the boiling point, about 100 °C, with the sourced ITS-90 boiling temperature of about 99.974 °C. Those are two-phase points at a stated pressure: solid with liquid, or liquid with vapor, at 1 atm.</p>
<p>The <strong>triple point</strong> is a different condition. It is the unique pressure and temperature at which solid, liquid, and vapor all coexist. For water, the IAPWS FAQ gives that point as exactly 273.16 K on ITS-90. Since 0 °C is 273.15 K, the triple point is 0.01 °C.</p>
<p>That is why the ice point at one atmosphere and the triple point must be kept apart. The familiar “about 0 °C” describes ice and liquid water at 1 atm. The triple point is a unique three-phase condition at 0.01 °C, and the pressure there is not one atmosphere. Do not treat 0.01 °C as a typo for 0 °C, and do not blend the two into one official point.</p>
<p>The kelvin itself is no longer defined by locking the triple point at 273.16 K (the 2018 change, which the IAPWS FAQ also notes), but the best estimate of water’s triple point is still 273.16 K, and that is the figure this lesson uses.</p>

## Modern language · Spanish: ayer, anoche, and el lunes pasado, with the preterite {#language}
