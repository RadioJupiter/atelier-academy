<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-orbit-first-look">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="orbit (first look)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear orbit (first look)">Hear</button> orbit (first look)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A path in which a body keeps falling toward a larger body and yet does not arrive, because it also has motion to the side. In Newton’s thought-picture (a throw from a high mountain, often illustrated from his *System of the World*), a faster sideways throw curves with the Earth instead of hitting the ground nearby. This lesson gives no invented orbital speed, and it does not treat every orbit as a circle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The Moon is in orbit around the Earth: it is falling toward the Earth and moving aside, in a closed path. The path need not be a perfect circle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a claim that a circular orbit is the only possible path, and not a measured speed from this classroom. A picture of a throw is a thought-picture, not a classroom experiment from a real mountain.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Monday’s inverse-square force is the pull; today’s job is to name the path without inventing a number for how fast the Moon, or a spacecraft, moves.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**A mix, and one new picture.** You may use F = G Mm / r², the inverse-square pattern, and W = mg.

**Newton’s thought-picture.** From a high mountain, a sideways throw falls to the ground. A faster throw goes farther. In the thought-picture, a throw that is fast enough curves with the Earth and does not land: an **orbit**. The picture is a help, not a measured experiment, and this lesson will **not** invent the speed.

**A caution.** Orbits are not all circles. A circle is a special case. Do not draw every orbit as a perfect ring and call that a fact of nature.
