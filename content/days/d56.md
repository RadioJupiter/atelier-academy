Each case has a job. Nominative: the subject (*puella habet*). Accusative: the object (*rosam habet*). Genitive: “of” (*rosa puellae*, the girl’s rose). Dative: the person to or for whom (*puellae dat*). Ablative: place phrases you already know, such as *in hortō*.

**A model.** *Puer puellae rosam dat.* The boy gives the girl a rose. *Puer* is the giver (nominative). *rosam* is the thing given (accusative). *puellae* is the person to whom (dative).

We stay in Capitula I–X. Instruction is in English. The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

You already know how to **read a quadratic inequality from the graph**. Today you use that method again.

Write one side as zero. The roots split the number line. If the parabola opens upward (a > 0), y < 0 *between* the roots and y > 0 *outside* them. If it opens downward, those regions swap. A square (x − a)² is never negative, so (x − a)² < 0 has no real solution — we will not bring in complex numbers to force one.

**The same worked shape.** y = (x − 2)(x − 6) opens upward. y < 0 when **2 < x < 6**. The shading in the figure is that region.

Keep this picture separate from Euclid III.36. Roots are not automatically a tangent and a secant.

You already know the stems *because*, *but*, and *so*. Today you use them again on a source caution.

J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation (the text used here is the version posted at Attalus, which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified) renders a letter from Pliny the Younger to Tacitus. **The habit.** Because names what the letter can honestly do. But names a limit: it is a later letter, in translation, and 79 CE is the conventional event-year, not a year the set sentence states. So states what you will do: don’t invent a town or a death toll the extract does not give, and don’t treat Firth’s wording as a stopwatch reading.

A model: *Firth’s Pliny can describe a cloud of unusual size and shape because the letter says the mother pointed it out at about the seventh hour, but we are reading a later request in a 1900 translation, so I will not treat “the seventh hour” as a stopwatch reading, and I will not name Pompeii or a death toll here.*

The formula is one you already know: **g = G M / R²**, with **G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²** (three significant figures). Today you gather the three fields you have already computed.

**Earth**, from the NASA Earth Fact Sheet mass and equatorial radius used in week 10: about **9.79 m/s²**, beside this course’s working **9.8 m/s²**. The computation is limited by G’s three significant figures.

**The Moon**, from the Planetary Fact Sheet family: about **1.6 m/s²**, about one-sixth of 9.8, limited by the two significant figures in the sheet’s mass, and sitting beside NASA Science’s “one-sixth.” Those wordings are not blended into a homemade official third number.

**Mars**, from last week: mass **0.642 × 10²⁴ kg**, diameter **6,792 km** so R = **3,396 km**. g comes out about **3.71 m/s²**, reported as **about 3.7 m/s²**, the same two-figure value the fact sheet lists. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5.

We will not invent an orbital speed for any of the three, and we will not claim more significant figures than G and M support.

## Logic · Valid forms: ponens, tollens, and disjunctive syllogism {#seminar}

Three **valid** forms have already been taught. Today you gather them. Necessary and sufficient conditions were taught on Day 16; they are not today’s job.

**Modus ponens.** If P, then Q. P. Therefore Q.

**Modus tollens.** If P, then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P.

**Disjunctive syllogism.** P or Q. Not P. Therefore Q. This course’s default “or” is **inclusive** unless a problem says exactly one: from P you may **not** infer not-Q under that default.

**Invalid look-alikes.** Affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P). Denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q). A non-square rectangle remains the counterexample to those two.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q. Tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q. Default “or” is inclusive. Not affirming the consequent.

</div>

### Exercises

1. Why is “it is a rectangle; therefore it is a square” not modus ponens?
2. Give a fresh “or” pair and apply disjunctive syllogism in one direction.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. If P then Q; P; therefore Q. If it is a square, then it is a rectangle; it is a square; therefore it is a rectangle.
2. Modus tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. Denying the antecedent uses not-P to infer not-Q, and is invalid. The same non-square rectangle blocks that invalid form.

1. That is affirming the consequent, already taught as invalid. A non-square rectangle is a rectangle and not a square.
2. A sound invented pair, with not-P therefore Q (or not-Q therefore P).
