**One preposition, two cases.** English often uses two different words (‘into’ / ‘in’). Latin often uses *in* twice, and the ending changes.

Original sentences: *Mārcus in hortum it.* *Mārcus in hortō labōrat.* *Iūlia in aquam cadit; nunc Iūlia in aquā est.* *Puerī in vīllam intrant.*

Town names keep last week’s exception: *Rōmam it*, *Rōmae est*, *Rōmā venit* — not *in Rōmam* in the pattern this course has taught.

## Mathematics · Geometry XIII: intersecting chords {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Euclid III.35; a product of segments, practiced with a diagram and clean numbers</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-intersecting-chords">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="intersecting chords" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear intersecting chords">Hear</button> intersecting chords</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>If two chords of a circle meet at a point E inside the circle, then the products of the segments of each chord are equal: AE · EC = BE · ED. This is Euclid III.35.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If AE = 3, EC = 8, and BE = 4, then 3 × 8 = 4 × ED, so ED = 6.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The equality is of *products of segments*, not of the lengths of the chords, and not of the segments themselves. AE need not equal BE.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A reason you can follow with similar triangles (already in the course in outline): triangles AEB and CED (or AED and BEC, depending on the letters) are similar, so the corresponding sides are proportional, and the product equality follows. Today the skill is accurate use of the product rule.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**The fact (Euclid III.35).** Chords AC and BD cross at E inside the circle: AE · EC = BE · ED.

**Pictorial first.** The two segments of one chord, multiplied, match the two segments of the other. A bar-model is less natural than an area reading: the rectangle with sides AE and EC has the same area as the rectangle with sides BE and ED.

Worked numbers: 3 and 8 on one chord, 4 and *x* on the other: 24 = 4*x*, *x* = 6. Another: 2 and 9, 3 and *x*: 18 = 3*x*, *x* = 6.

For (2), the structural hints, not the political content, are: a short main clause that announces a claim, then a parallel series of *that*-clauses spelling out the claim.

Do not copy the model’s wording of rights, nature, or God into the reconstruction. The exercise is formal.

## Science · Photosynthesis as a net process {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a carefully labeled net equation, with its limits stated; no invented yields</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-photosynthesis">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="photosynthesis" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear photosynthesis">Hear</button> photosynthesis</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The process by which green plants (and some other organisms) use light to build sugars from carbon dioxide and water, and release oxygen as a product. A widely taught net equation is 6 CO<sub>2</sub> + 6 H<sub>2</sub>O + light energy → C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> + 6 O<sub>2</sub>. That equation is a school-level summary of a much longer process; it does not describe every intermediate step, and it is not a claim that a plant makes a single glucose molecule in one jump.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A leaf in the light takes in carbon dioxide and water and, with chlorophyll’s help in capturing light, builds carbohydrate and releases oxygen.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not respiration run backwards as a claim about every enzyme, and not “plants eat sunlight” as if light were a food. Light is the energy input; the carbon in the sugar comes from carbon dioxide.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s pigment is part of the light-capturing machinery. This lesson places it in the net process, without invented yields or rates.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-carbon-dioxide-photo">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="carbon dioxide" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear carbon dioxide">Hear</button> carbon dioxide</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A gas, CO<sub>2</sub>, that is one of the carbon sources for the sugars made in photosynthesis. In the net school equation, six molecules of CO<sub>2</sub> are used for each C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> written on the product side.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The carbon atoms in the sugar on the right-hand side of the net equation come from CO<sub>2</sub>, not from the light and not from the soil’s “food” in the everyday sense.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the same as oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>), which is written as a product in the net equation, and not the same as the carbon of a pigment molecule itself.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>You need the name, the formula, and the job in the net equation. A later course can treat how CO<sub>2</sub> actually enters a leaf.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The net process, stated carefully.** In the light, a green plant builds carbohydrate from carbon dioxide and water, and releases oxygen. Chlorophyll is part of how the light is captured; it is not itself the sugar.

**The school equation, and its limit.**

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

This is a widely taught *net* summary. Real photosynthesis is a long sequence of steps (including light-dependent reactions and a carbon-fixing cycle often named the Calvin cycle). This course names that those steps exist, and then stays with the net equation and with the bookkeeping of atoms: the carbon in the sugar comes from CO₂; the oxygen gas released comes, in the modern account, from water, not from the CO₂ — a fact we state, but do not pretend to have proved in this lesson.

**What we will not invent.** No grams of sugar per hour, no percentage of the sun’s energy “used,” no claim that every plant makes exactly glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆ is a convenient representative carbohydrate).

**A check of sense.** In the dark, this net process does not run as a light-driven whole. Plants also respire, which is a different process and is not today’s lesson.


</div>

## Economics & personal finance · Specialization and trade (a classroom table) {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: opportunity cost (Day 4) applied to an invented, clearly labeled table; no national statistics</p>

**An invented table, clearly labeled.** Suppose two students, Ana and Beau, have two hours. In one hour, Ana can either copy 5 pages of notes or rule 4 practice staves; Beau can either copy 2 pages or rule 10 staves. These numbers are invented for the classroom; they are not a survey.

**Without trade**, if each splits the two hours evenly, Ana has 5 pages and 4 staves; Beau has 2 pages and 10 staves. Together: 7 pages and 14 staves.

**With specialization**, Ana copies for two hours (10 pages, 0 staves); Beau rules for two hours (0 pages, 20 staves). Together: 10 pages and 20 staves — more of both. If they then trade some of the pile (for example, Ana gives Beau 3 pages for 8 staves), both can have more of both goods than in the split-time plan.

**Opportunity cost, made visible.** When Ana copies one page, she gives up 4/5 of a stave (because 5 pages take the hour that could have made 4 staves). When Beau copies one page, he gives up 5 staves (2 pages take the hour that could have made 10 staves). Ana is the lower-cost copyist; Beau is the lower-cost stave ruler. That is why this particular invented table rewards the split of tasks.

**A limit.** Real people care about more than two tasks, and learning both skills may still be the point of a school. The model shows a possible gain; it does not issue a life instruction.
