## Science · The specific heat of ice, and a comparison with melting {#science}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: ice Ih cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 0 °C, from the same Harvey/NIST table, compared with ΔfusH = 333.4 J/g</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-specific-heat-of-ice">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="specific heat of ice" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear specific heat of ice">Hear</button> specific heat of ice</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The specific heat of ice in this lesson is the Harvey/NIST figure for ice Ih at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa: cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. It is the energy, per gram per kelvin, to change the temperature of the ice at that point in the table, not the energy to melt it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Using that 0 °C figure, warming 1 g of ice by 1 °C (or 1 K) at that temperature takes 2.10 J. Melting that same gram at 0 °C takes 333.4 J, about 159 times as much (333.4 / 2.10 ≈ 158.8).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at ΔfusH, taught Tuesday, and not last week’s densities as a first look. Not an official liquid-water cp, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2.10 holds at every temperature; the same table gives other cp values at other temperatures, which are not today’s job.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A kelvin and a Celsius degree have the same size, so 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ may be used for a 1 °C change at this point. The comparison 333.4 / 2.10 uses both figures at 0 °C from the same table.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>Tuesday’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g is the energy to melt a gram of ice at 0 °C, with no temperature change. The same Harvey/NIST table gives a specific heat for ice Ih at 0 °C:</p>
<p>c<sub>p</sub> = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.</p>
<p>That figure is the energy, per gram per kelvin, associated with changing the temperature of the ice at that row of the table. Warming 1 g by 1 K (which is also 1 °C in size) at that point takes 2.10 J. Melting that gram at 0 °C takes 333.4 J.</p>
<p>333.4 / 2.10 ≈ 158.8, about 159 times. Melting 1 g at 0 °C takes about 159 times the energy of warming that gram of ice by 1 °C, using the two 0 °C figures from the same table.</p>
<p>For an invented 100 g, warming by 1 °C at that figure would take 210 J; melting at 0 °C still takes 33,340 J. The mass is invented. The two table figures are Harvey’s. Do not extend 2.10 over a large temperature range without noting that the table’s cp itself changes with temperature (for example, the table lists 2.02 at −10 °C, which is not used as today’s working figure). Do not invent a liquid-water cp in order to compare ice with liquid water.</p>
<p class="note">Numerical figures are from A. H. Harvey, “Properties of Ice and Supercooled Water,” a NIST update to a <em>CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics</em> table, at standard atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water (PDF: https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=926353). ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g. The specific heat of ice Ih at 0 °C is cp = 2.10 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Last week’s NIST/Harvey densities at 0 °C (ice Ih 0.9167 g/cm³; liquid water 0.99984 g/cm³) remain available as contrasts, not as a new first look. A liquid-water official cp is not taken from a table that was not opened as a definite fetched row for this week, and is not invented.</p>


</div>
