Capitulum XII is titled *Mīles Rōmānus* in the course’s chapter list. This sitting begins the chapter’s first new job: **third-declension adjectives**. You already know adjectives such as *magnus, magna, magnum*, which take first- and second-declension endings. A third-declension adjective uses a different set of endings, but it still **agrees** with its noun in case, number, and gender.

The first pair to learn is *fortis, -e*: *fortis* in the masculine or feminine nominative singular, *forte* in the neuter nominative or accusative singular. So *Mīles fortis est* (the soldier is brave), and *Scūtum forte est* (the shield is strong). The sense is the same kind of describing; the ending changes to match the noun.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: *Mīles fortis est.* *Scūtum forte est.* *Mīles nōn tristis est.* *Scūtum nōn forte est.*

The wider army vocabulary of the chapter, the dative of possession, comparatives, and the fourth declension are not today’s jobs.

## Mathematics · Geometry XXIX: the Pythagorean theorem {#math}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a right triangle, squares on the sides, then a² + b² = c²</p>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In a right triangle, the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. If the legs are a and b and the hypotenuse is c, then a² + b² = c².</dd></div>

In a **right triangle**, one angle is a right angle. The two sides that form that angle are the **legs**. The side opposite the right angle is the **hypotenuse**.

The **Pythagorean theorem** says: the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the two legs. If the legs are *a* and *b* and the hypotenuse is *c*, then

*a*² + *b*² = *c*².

The figure is a 3-4-5 triangle, with a square drawn on each side. The square on 3 has area 9, the square on 4 has area 16, and the square on 5 has area 25. Then 9 + 16 = 25, so 3² + 4² = 5². The right-angle mark is at the angle between the sides 3 and 4; that is the only right angle in the figure.

This is the content of Euclid I.47. Today’s job is the statement, the names (leg, hypotenuse), and a first numerical check. A second proof, using similar triangles from week 13, comes tomorrow. The converse (if *a*² + *b*² = *c*², then the triangle is right) is not yet today’s claim.

A **précis** is a short restatement of a passage **in your own words**. It keeps the author’s points, in their order and in fair proportion. It does not add a new opinion. It is not a **quotation**, which keeps the author’s wording. It is also tighter than a loose **summary** that drops a main point or rearranges the passage until the author’s shape is gone.

The set passage for this week’s writing is one sentence-group from Procopius, *The Buildings*, I.1.6, in H. B. Dewing’s 1940 translation (public domain):

> In our own age there has been born the Emperor Justinian, who, taking over the State when it was harassed by disorder, has not only made it greater in extent, but also much more illustrious, by expelling from it those barbarians who had from of old pressed hard upon it, as I have made clear in detail in the Books on the Wars.

<p class="note">Procopius, <em>The Buildings</em>, Book I, chapter 1, section 6, translated by H. B. Dewing, Loeb Classical Library, 1940, as posted at LacusCurtius. The 1940 copyright was not renewed and expired in 1968; the translation is in the public domain. https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/1A*.html Later sections of this chapter remain reserved for history; today’s writing job is the method of a précis, on this one sentence-group only.</p>

A sound précis of this passage will still have Justinian, the state he took over (harassed by disorder), and the two claims that he made it greater in extent and more illustrious, including by expelling barbarians who had long pressed upon it. It will not quietly drop “more illustrious,” or add a modern verdict on Justinian, or paste Dewing’s wording and call that a précis.

The **center of mass** of a system is its balance point: the point at which the system’s mass may be taken to act, for balance under gravity (in a uniform field, also called the **center of gravity**).

A **uniform** bar, with mass spread evenly, has its center of mass at the midpoint. That is a special case, not the general one.

For two masses on a light bar, the center of mass is the point at which the bar would balance, the same habit as last week’s lever: mass × distance on the left equals mass × distance on the right, in this course’s first model. In the figure, a labeled invented mass 2 sits 4 units from the fulcrum, and a labeled invented mass 4 sits 2 units from the fulcrum, because 2 × 4 = 4 × 2. The balance point is closer to the larger mass.

These masses and distances are **invented classroom numbers**, labeled invented. This lesson does not invent an official NASA or laboratory center-of-mass figure for a named spacecraft or a named building.

## Logic · Darii, a first-figure AII syllogism {#seminar}

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="DAH-ree-ee" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear DAH-ree-ee">Hear</button> Darii</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A valid first-figure syllogism of the form AII: all M are P; some S are M; therefore some S are P. The major premise is universal affirmative, the minor and the conclusion particular affirmative.</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>As with Celarent, the major term is P (the predicate of the conclusion), the minor term is S (the subject of the conclusion), and the middle term M appears in both premises and not in the conclusion.</dd></div>

Last week taught **Celarent** (EAE): no M are P; all S are M; therefore no S are P. Today is another valid first-figure form, **Darii** (AII):

- All M are P.
- Some S are M.
- Therefore some S are P.

The **major** premise is universal affirmative (all M are P). The **minor** premise and the **conclusion** are particular affirmative (some S are M; some S are P). The **middle term** M still joins the premises and does not appear in the conclusion. The major term P is the predicate of the conclusion; the minor term S is the subject of the conclusion — the same jobs as last week.

A model, in invented classroom wording, not a historical claim: “All soldiers are brave; some of these men are soldiers; therefore some of these men are brave.” The example is for the form, not a claim about every soldier in Procopius.

Darii is not Celarent. Do not swap “all” and “no,” or turn the conclusion into a universal (“all of these men are brave”), which the particular minor does not give.
