## Mathematics · Geometry XLII: the volume of a right rectangular prism {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: packing unit cubes, then V = length × width × height</p>

<article class="term" id="term-volume-of-a-solid">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="volume of a solid" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear volume of a solid">Hear</button> volume of a solid</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The volume of a solid is the amount of space it occupies, measured in cubic units. A classroom cube of edge 1 has volume 1 cubic unit. Volume is not the same as area, which measures a face, and not the same as last week’s density, which is mass per unit volume.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A classroom box that holds 24 unit cubes has volume 24 cubic units, however those cubes are arranged, so long as they fill the box without gaps or overlaps.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the area of a face, and not last week’s circle area πr², which is a measure of a flat region. Not a mass, and not a density.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week measured length around a circle and area inside it. Today begins the space inside a solid.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-volume-of-a-right-rectangular-prism">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="volume of a right rectangular prism" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear volume of a right rectangular prism">Hear</button> volume of a right rectangular prism</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A right rectangular prism, or classroom box, has three edges that meet at right angles: a length ℓ, a width w, and a height h. Its volume is V = ℓ × w × h, in cubic units. That product is the number of unit cubes that fill the box.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A classroom prism of length 6, width 3, and height 4 has volume V = 6 × 3 × 4 = 72 cubic units.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the surface area (the total area of the faces), which is reserved. Not a slanted solid, and not a cylinder, which comes later this week.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>If you think of a layer of ℓ × w unit cubes, there are h such layers, so the volume is (ℓ × w) × h.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>Last week’s work was length and area in a plane, including the area of a circle. Today is the space inside a solid, its <strong>volume</strong>, measured in cubic units.</p>
<p>A <strong>right rectangular prism</strong> is a classroom box: three edges meet at right angles. Pack it with unit cubes. One layer is a rectangle of ℓ by w cubes, so that layer has ℓ × w cubes. There are h layers. So</p>
<p>V = ℓ × w × h.</p>

## Physics · Elastic potential energy, PE_el = ½kx² {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: the energy stored in a Hooke’s-law spring is the area under the force–displacement graph, PE_el = ½kx², from OpenStax 16.1, with sourced Example 16.2</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-elastic-potential-energy">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="elastic potential energy" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear elastic potential energy">Hear</button> elastic potential energy</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in a system that obeys Hooke’s law when it is stretched or compressed from equilibrium. OpenStax 16.1 gives PE_el = ½kx², which is the area of the triangle under a straight-line graph of the applied force kx against the displacement x.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>OpenStax Example 16.2 uses a toy dart gun with k = 50.0 N/m and x = 0.150 m. PE_el = ½ × 50.0 × (0.150)² = 0.563 J. Those figures are OpenStax’s, not invented.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at Hooke’s law, F = −kx, or at the force constant k, taught last week. Not a first look at work, kinetic energy, or potential energy as kinds, already taught in Days 21–28. Not gravitational potential energy, and not a claim that every spring stores this energy once it has left Hooke’s-law behavior.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The minus sign in F = −kx is still the restoring force. The work you do to stretch the spring slowly is the stored energy, ½kx², and is not itself negative in this formula.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>Last week’s Hooke’s law was the force: F = −kx. Today is the energy stored when that law holds.</p>
<p>OpenStax, <em>College Physics 2e</em>, section 16.1 (CC BY), notes that the work done to stretch or compress such a spring equals the area under the graph of force against displacement. When the size of the force is kx and the graph is a straight line, that area is a triangle of base x and height kx:</p>
<p>PE<sub>el</sub> = ½kx².</p>

## Elective · Logic: Festino, a second-figure EIO syllogism {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: Festino as second-figure EIO, after last week’s Camestres, with the middle term as predicate of both premises</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-festino">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="fess-TEE-no" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear fess-TEE-no">Hear</button> Festino</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Festino is a second-figure syllogism of the form EIO: a universal negative premise, a particular affirmative premise, and a particular negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>No birds are cats. Some pets are cats. Therefore some pets are not birds. The middle term, “cats,” is the predicate of both premises.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at the second figure, or at Cesare (EAE-2) or Camestres (AEE-2), already taught. Festino’s conclusion is particular (Some S are not P), not universal. Not Baroco, which is reserved.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week: Camestres, AEE, second figure. Today: Festino, EIO, still second figure. The vowels in the name mark the three propositions: E, I, O.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>Last week’s Camestres was second-figure AEE: All P are M; No S are M; therefore No S are P. Today is the next second-figure mood taught in this course, <strong>Festino</strong>, whose vowels are E, I, O:</p>
<p>No P are M.</p>
<p>Some S are M.</p>
<p>Therefore Some S are not P.</p>
<p>The middle term M is still the predicate of both premises, which is what makes the figure the second figure, already taught. What is new is the particular premise and the particular conclusion.</p>
<p>A clean classroom example: No birds are cats. Some pets are cats. Therefore some pets are not birds.</p>
<p>A near-miss is to write a universal conclusion, “No pets are birds.” The premises do not give that: they give only that some pets, namely those that are cats, are not birds. Another near-miss is to begin with All P are M and still call the mood Festino. If the first premise is a universal affirmative about P, you are no longer in Festino (and you may have slipped toward a reserved or different mood).</p>


</div>
