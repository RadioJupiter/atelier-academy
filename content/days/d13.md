## Mathematics · Algebra IV: systems of linear equations {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Singapore model method first, then substitution and elimination (Gelfand, <em>Algebra</em>)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-system-of-equations">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="system of equations" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear system of equations">Hear</button> system of equations</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two or more equations that must be true at the same time, about the same unknowns. A solution is a pair (or triple, …) that fits every equation.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From late Middle French, from Latin systēma, from Ancient Greek σύστημα (‘a whole made of parts’), from σύν (‘together’) + ἵστημι (‘I set up’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>x + y = 10 and x − y = 2, together, have the unique solution x = 6, y = 4.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A system is not ‘two problems side by side that may use different unknowns’. And a pair that fits only one of the equations is not a solution of the system.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The bar model makes the same relationship visible before any letters are written, as in Day 1.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-substitution">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="substitution" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear substitution">Hear</button> substitution</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A method for solving a system: use one equation to express one unknown in terms of the other, and put that expression into the second equation.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French substitution, from Late Latin substitutio.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>From x = y + 6 and x + y = 48, replace x in the second equation: (y + 6) + y = 48.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Substitution is not guessing a number and checking, and it is not changing the unknown in only one place and leaving it in another.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Elimination (adding or subtracting equations to cancel an unknown) is a second method for the same systems. Both must give the same pair.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A **system** is two (or more) equations that must both be true. The pair you want has to satisfy every equation at once.

**Concrete and pictorial.** “Two numbers add to 48, and one is 6 more than the other.” Draw a bar for the smaller number and a bar 6 units longer for the larger. Together they make 48. The two equal shorter pieces plus 6 make 48, so the two pieces make 42, and each is 21. The numbers are 21 and 27.

**Abstract: substitution.** Let the smaller be *y* and the larger be *x*. Then *x* = *y* + 6 and *x* + *y* = 48. Replace *x* in the second equation: (*y* + 6) + *y* = 48, so 2*y* = 42 and *y* = 21, *x* = 27.

**Elimination.** Sometimes it is cleaner to add or subtract the equations so that one unknown disappears. If *x* + *y* = 15 and 2*x* − *y* = 6, adding gives 3*x* = 21, so *x* = 7 and *y* = 8. Check in both originals: 7 + 8 = 15 and 14 − 8 = 6.

A system of two different linear equations in two unknowns has one solution (two lines meet at one point). If the two equations say the same thing, there are infinitely many solutions; if they contradict, there are none.

</div>

## Writing & Rhetoric · The semicolon and the conjunctive adverb {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution; grammar of the sentence (joining two independent clauses)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-semicolon">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="semicolon" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear semicolon">Hear</button> semicolon</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The mark (;) that can join two closely related complete sentences without a coordinating conjunction. Each side must be able to stand as a sentence on its own.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From semi- (‘half’) + colon, because the mark is a colon with the lower half replaced by a comma.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The radius meets the tangent at one point; it is perpendicular there.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A semicolon is not a fancy comma, and it cannot join a sentence to a fragment. It is also not a colon: a colon often introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation after a complete clause.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 11’s sentence combining needed a legal join. The semicolon is one such join; *and/but/so* with a comma is another.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-conjunctive-adverb">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="conjunctive adverb" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear conjunctive adverb">Hear</button> conjunctive adverb</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A word such as however, therefore, moreover, meanwhile, or otherwise that shows the relation between two complete sentences. When it joins them, a semicolon stands before it and a comma after it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The locative looks like a genitive; however, it does not show possession in Rōmae habitat.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A conjunctive adverb is not a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so). You cannot legally write ‘The locative looks like a genitive, however it does not show possession’ — that is a comma splice.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The transition’s meaning (contrast, result, addition) must still be real, as on Day 11.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Two complete sentences that belong together can be joined in more than one legal way.

**Three legal joins.** (1) A period, then a new sentence. (2) A comma plus *and, but, or, so, yet.* (3) A **semicolon**, with or without a **conjunctive adverb**.

**The pattern with a conjunctive adverb.** Sentence; however, sentence. The semicolon comes *before* however/therefore/moreover/meanwhile; a comma comes after it.

**A colon is different.** A colon, in the use taught here, follows a complete sentence and introduces an explanation, a list, or a quotation: *The rule is simple: the preposition chooses the case.*

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

<p>Key terms: semicolon, conjunctive adverb.</p>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>This is not permission to swap the verbs at random, and it does not apply to every adjective in the same way. When in doubt, go back to the two jobs: characteristic versus condition or place.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Agreement still holds: the adjective matches the subject in gender and number (*estamos listos*, *está lista*).</dd></div>
</dl></article>

You already conjugate **ser** (soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son) and **estar** (estoy, estás, está, estamos, estáis, están). Today the work is **choosing**.

**Ser** for:
- identity: *Ella es estudiante.*
- origin: *Somos de la ciudad.*
- a characteristic: *El museo es grande.*
- time: *Es la una.* *Hoy es viernes.*

**Estar** for:
- place: *El libro está en la mesa.* *Madrid está en España.*
- a condition or state: *Estoy cansado.* *La puerta está abierta.*

**The same adjective, two senses.** *Es aburrido* (boring, a characteristic) versus *está aburrido* (bored, a condition). *Es listo* (clever) versus *está listo* (ready). *La manzana es verde* can describe its color; *la manzana está verde* often means it is unripe. Do not treat this as a license to swap the verbs everywhere; use it as a reason to ask what you mean.

## Art history · The Aegean: Cycladic, Minoan, Mycenaean {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: close looking at named objects, with a caution about restoration · After Day 3 (how to look) and Day 8 (Egypt)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-aegean-bronze-age">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Aegean Bronze Age" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Aegean Bronze Age">Hear</button> Aegean Bronze Age</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The Bronze Age cultures of the Aegean Sea and its islands and shores, treated here in three parts: Cycladic (the Cyclades islands), Minoan (Crete), and Mycenaean (the mainland palatial centers, such as Mycenae). <div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Aegean is from Latin Aegaeus, from Ancient Greek Αἰγαῖος; the further link to the mythical king Aegeus, or to a word for waves, is suggested rather than settled, so it is left aside here.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A Cycladic folded-arm marble figure, a fresco from the palace at Knossos, and the Lion Gate at Mycenae belong to this world, in different places and centuries.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Evans’s reconstructions and the fresco restorations (often by the Gilliéron workshop) are part of what we see in photographs of Knossos. Looking includes asking what is ancient plaster and pigment, and what is modern reconstruction.</dd></div>

Three neighboring Bronze Age cultures, not one style.

**Cycladic.** On the Cyclades, in the third millennium BCE (a conventional range for the well-known folded-arm marble figures is the Early Cycladic period, often placed in that millennium). The figures are usually highly simplified: a long head, a folded-arm pose, a flat or slightly modeled body, often originally painted. We do not have a contemporary written explanation of their use; be careful of words such as ‘idol’ if they go beyond the evidence.

**Minoan Crete.** Palaces such as **Knossos**, excavated and imaginatively reconstructed by **Arthur Evans** from 1900 onward. Wall paintings in **fresco** (pigment in plaster) survive in fragments; many textbook images include later restoration. A famous bull-leaping scene is to be looked at with that caution: describe what is actually preserved, and what is reconstructed, as far as a good modern photograph or museum note allows. The name **Minoan** is Evans’s.

**Mycenaean mainland.** Citadels such as **Mycenae**. The **Lion Gate** is a monumental entrance: a relieving triangle above the lintel holds a relief of two lionesses (the heads, probably of a different material, are lost) flanking a central column. This is Late Bronze Age palatial architecture on the mainland, later than the early Cycladic figures, and not the same culture as Knossos, though the worlds were in contact.

**Linear A and Linear B** may be named in passing: Linear B, used on the mainland and at some Cretan sites in the later period, was deciphered as an early form of Greek; Linear A is not deciphered in the same way. That is a fact about writing, not a license to invent translations.

Look, compare, and do not collapse the three into one ‘ancient Greek’ style.
