<p>You already have the present infinitive, and you can use it after <em>possum / potest</em> (“is able to see”). Today the infinitive does a different job.</p>
<p>After verbs of seeing, saying, or thinking — <em>vidēre, dīcere, putāre</em> — Latin can name a second action with an <strong>accusative and an infinitive</strong> (often abbreviated AcI). The accusative is the subject of that infinitive, not the object of a second finite verb.</p>
<p>Original sentences: <em>Puella avem volāre videt.</em> The girl sees the bird fly (she sees that the bird is flying). <em>Magister puellam cantāre dīcit.</em> The teacher says that the girl is singing. <em>Putō puerum dormīre.</em> I think that the boy is sleeping.</p>
<p>Do not write two finite verbs for this idea (*<em>Puella videt avem volat</em>). Keep the second verb in the infinitive, and keep its subject in the accusative.</p>

## Mathematics · Geometry XXVII: a missing side, from a scale factor {#math}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: write the similarity, then a proportion for corresponding sides</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-scale-factor-similarity">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="scale factor (similarity)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear scale factor (similarity)">Hear</button> scale factor (similarity)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The constant ratio of corresponding sides of two similar triangles. If △ABC ∼ △DEF and the scale factor from ABC to DEF is k, then each side of DEF is k times the corresponding side of ABC, so a missing side can be found from one known pair.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If 4 corresponds to 10 and 6 corresponds to x, and the triangles are similar, then 4/10 = 6/x, so x = 15. The scale factor from the smaller to the larger is 10/4 = 5/2.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a ratio of sides that do not correspond, and not a claim that any two sides from the two triangles may be placed in a fraction. The vertices in the similarity statement tell you which sides match.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Monday’s definition (corresponding sides proportional) is the reason a scale factor works. AA, taught yesterday, is often how we know the triangles are similar before we write the proportion.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>Once two triangles are known to be similar, one pair of corresponding sides gives the <strong>scale factor</strong>, and that same factor finds a missing side.</p>
<p>In the figure, the sides 4 and 10 correspond, and the sides 6 and <em>x</em> correspond. The proportion is 4/10 = 6/<em>x</em>, or 4 : 10 = 6 : <em>x</em>. Cross-multiplying, 4<em>x</em> = 60, so <em>x</em> = 15. The scale factor from the smaller triangle to the larger is 10/4 = 5/2; check: 6 × 5/2 = 15.</p>
<p>Write the similarity first, or at least name the corresponding sides, before you write a fraction. A ratio of sides that do not correspond will give the wrong missing length.</p>

## Writing & Rhetoric · A warrant is not a restated claim {#writing}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: the already-taught warrant, set beside a restated claim</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms recall-terms" markdown="0">
<h4>Key terms (from earlier lessons)</h4>

<li><a href="d61.html#term-toulmin-model">Toulmin model</a> — Day 61</li>
<li><a href="d61.html#term-grounds-toulmin">grounds (Toulmin)</a> — Day 61</li>
</ul>
<p>Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.</p>

<p>A warrant is the rule that makes the grounds count. A <strong>restated claim</strong> only says the claim again, sometimes with “therefore” or “so” in front of it. The two are easy to confuse, and they are not the same job.</p>
<p>Take yesterday’s set sentence. Claim: the war tests whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure. Grounds: “we are engaged in a great civil war.”</p>
<p>A warrant: a civil war of this kind tests whether such a nation can endure. That is a general rule you can agree or disagree with, and it joins the grounds to the claim.</p>
<p>A restated claim: “therefore the war is a test of whether the nation can endure.” That sentence does not add a rule. It repeats the claim. If you are asked for a warrant and you write only “therefore” plus the claim, you have not yet given a warrant.</p>
<p>The same distinction holds for an invented case. Claim: the tea is cool. Grounds: no steam rises from the cup. Warrant: a hot cup of tea in this room gives off steam. A restated claim: “so the tea is cool.”</p>


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A warrant is a general rule that joins grounds to a claim. A restated claim says the claim again (often after “therefore” or “so”) and does not do that job. You can test a warrant by asking whether it is a rule someone could dispute.

## Physics · The lever, as a case of rotational equilibrium {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: Στ = 0 about a named fulcrum, with labeled invented numbers</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-fulcrum">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="fulcrum" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear fulcrum">Hear</button> fulcrum</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The support about which a lever turns. In this lesson the fulcrum is the point about which torques are taken. A force through the fulcrum gives no torque about it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin fulcrum, “bedpost, foot of a couch,” from fulciō, “prop up, support” (English Wiktionary, entry “fulcrum”).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In the invented 3.00 m light rod, the fulcrum is 1.00 m from the left end, so a 20 N force at the left end has a moment arm of 1.00 m about the fulcrum.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the load and not the effort. The fulcrum is the turning point, not a second downward force. Moving the fulcrum changes the two distances, and so changes the forces needed for Στ = 0.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Rotational equilibrium (Στ = 0) is still the condition. The lever is the special case in which a rigid bar turns about one named support, the fulcrum.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-lever-rule">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="the lever rule" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear the lever rule">Hear</button> the lever rule</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>For a light lever in rotational equilibrium about its fulcrum, the effort times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum equals the load times its perpendicular distance from the fulcrum (opposite senses). It is Στ = 0 written for two downward forces and one support.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 20 N load, 1.00 m to the left of the fulcrum, is balanced by an effort F, 2.00 m to the right: 20 N × 1.00 m = F × 2.00 m, so F = 10 N. The fulcrum then supplies 30 N upward if the lever is at rest.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a new unit, and not an excuse to ignore ΣF = 0 when the lever is at rest. Not a measurement from a named official machine; the numbers in this lesson are invented and labeled as such.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Monday’s rotational equilibrium is the general condition. Today it is applied to a bar with a named fulcrum, often called a lever.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>A <strong>lever</strong> is a rigid bar that turns about a support called the <strong>fulcrum</strong>. The condition is the one taught on Monday: Στ = 0 about the fulcrum. If the lever stays at rest, ΣF = 0 as well, and the fulcrum can supply the upward force that balances the downward ones.</p>
<p>The figure is a labeled invented example. A light rod, 3.00 m long, has its fulcrum 1.00 m from the left end. 20 N acts downward at the left end, so its moment arm about the fulcrum is 1.00 m. An unknown downward force F at the right end has a moment arm of 2.00 m. For Στ = 0: 20 N × 1.00 m = F × 2.00 m, so F = 10 N. The downward forces add to 30 N, so the fulcrum supplies 30 N upward if the rod is at rest.</p>
<p>That two-force statement is sometimes called the <strong>lever rule</strong>: effort times effort arm equals load times load arm, when the two torques are in opposite senses and the bar is light. It is not a new law; it is Στ = 0 in this special case.</p>


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A lever turns about a fulcrum. In rotational equilibrium, load × load arm = effort × effort arm (opposite senses). If the lever is at rest, the fulcrum also supplies the upward force that makes ΣF = 0. Numbers in this lesson are invented classroom figures.


<p>Key terms: fulcrum, the lever rule.</p>
