The **vocative** is the form used for calling or addressing someone (*Salvē, puella!*). In many words it looks like the nominative; a familiar exception in the early books is masculine names and nouns in *-us*, which often take *-e* (*Mārce*, *serve*). Commands (the imperative) are used when you tell one person to do something.

A **function** assigns to each allowed input exactly one output. We write *y* = *f*(*x*), or just *y* in terms of *x*. The **graph** is the set of points (*x*, *f*(*x*)) in the plane. A vertical line should meet the graph at most once (the vertical-line test). Linear functions have graphs that are straight lines; *y* = *mx* + *c* has slope *m* and intercept *c*.

**Function.** A rule assigning to each allowed input x (the **domain**) exactly one output f(x). The set of outputs is the **range**.

**Transformations of y = f(x).**
- y = f(x) + c: shift up by c.
- y = f(x − c): shift right by c.
- y = a·f(x): stretch vertically by a (and reflect if a < 0).
- y = f(−x): reflect in the y-axis.
- y = |f(x)|: reflect the parts below the x-axis upward.

**Parabolas by completing the square.** y = x² − 4x + 1 = (x − 2)² − 3: the graph of y = x², shifted 2 right and 3 down; vertex (2, −3).

Franklin's method, third pass: after hints and order, **compare** your rewrite with the original and make a short list of what you missed — a word choice, a clause, a turn of thought. Then write once more, aiming at those misses only. This is not copying with extra steps; it is training your ear on a real passage.

**Why it works.** Verse forces you to search for words of the same meaning but different length and sound; prose forces you to make the logic explicit. Moving between them builds what Franklin called "a stock of words".

**Model** (Shakespeare, Sonnet 73, first quatrain, public domain):

> That time of year thou mayst in me behold
> When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang
> Upon those boughs which shake against the cold,
> Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang.

</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Compare, list the misses, rewrite once more. Do not inflate the list with taste ('I just prefer this word') unless the original's word does a job yours does not.

## Science · Light: polarization and the quantitative model {#science}

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="polarization" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear polarization">Hear</button> polarization</h5>
<dl class="frayer">

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="frequency" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear frequency">Hear</button> frequency <span class="symbol">f = c / λ</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The number of wave cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). For light in vacuum, frequency times wavelength equals the speed of light c.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin frequentia, from frequēns; in English, frequent + -cy.</dd></div>

**Another clue in the sky.** Light scattered by air molecules is **partly polarised**: its vibrations are concentrated in one direction. The effect is strongest in the part of the sky about 90° away from the Sun. With polarising sunglasses (or the polarising filter of a camera), look at the sky 90° from the Sun and rotate the lens: the sky darkens and brightens. Some insects, such as bees, use this pattern of polarization to navigate. *Never look at the Sun itself.*

**Useful relations.** Speed of light in vacuum c = 299,792,458 m/s (exact, by definition of the meter). For any wave, c = fλ.

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="rule of 72" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear rule of 72">Hear</button> rule of 72</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A short estimate: money at r percent a year doubles in about 72/r years, a good approximation for rates between about 2% and 15%.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>At 6% a year, doubling takes about 12 years; at 9%, about 8 years.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not exact, and it is not reliable for every rate. Use the formula when you need a precise amount.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>It is a reading of compound interest, not a different kind of interest.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Simple interest** pays only on the original principal; **compound interest** pays on the interest already earned. With principal P, annual rate r and n years, compounded yearly: **A = P(1 + r)ⁿ**.

**Rule of 72.** Money at r% a year doubles in roughly 72/r years (a good approximation for rates between about 2% and 15%).

**APR and APY.** A credit card with 24% APR charges about 2% a month; compounded monthly, that is (1.02)¹² − 1 ≈ 26.8% a year. The same mathematics that makes savings grow makes debt grow.

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Simple interest pays only on the principal P. Compound interest pays on interest already earned: A = P(1 + r)^n when compounded yearly. The rule of 72: doubling time ≈ 72/r years for ordinary rates (about 2–15%). A 24% APR compounded monthly is (1.02)^12 − 1 ≈ 26.8% effective. The same mathematics grows savings and debt.
