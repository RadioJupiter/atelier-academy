## Mathematics · Geometry VIII: rectangle, rhombus and square {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Kiselev, <em>Planimetry</em>, special parallelograms · Friday practice on this week's figures</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-rectangle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="rectangle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear rectangle">Hear</button> rectangle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A parallelogram with a right angle. Then all four angles are right angles, and the diagonals are equal and still bisect each other.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Middle French, from Old French, from Medieval Latin or Late Latin rectangulum (‘right angle’), from Latin rectus (‘right’) + angulus (‘an angle’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A rectangle 8 units by 6 units has diagonal 10, because 6² + 8² = 10².</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A rectangle is already a parallelogram; you do not need to prove the opposite sides are parallel again. It is not ‘any four-sided figure with a square corner’ if the opposite sides fail to be parallel.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday's parallelogram theorems still apply. The new facts are the right angles and the equal diagonals.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-rhombus">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="rhombus" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear rhombus">Hear</button> rhombus</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A parallelogram with all four sides equal. Its diagonals are perpendicular and bisect the vertex angles; they still bisect each other.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin rhombus, from Ancient Greek ῥόμβος (‘rhombus, spinning top’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A rhombus with side 10 and a 60° angle splits into two equilateral triangles, so the shorter diagonal is 10.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A rhombus need not be a square: the angles need not be 90°. A square is the special rhombus (and special rectangle) that has both a right angle and four equal sides.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A square is the figure that is both a rectangle and a rhombus.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A **rectangle** is a parallelogram with one right angle. Then every angle is 90° (consecutive angles are already supplementary), and the diagonals are equal.

A **rhombus** is a parallelogram with all sides equal. Its diagonals are perpendicular, and each diagonal bisects the angles at the vertices it joins.

A **square** is both: a rectangle with equal sides, or a rhombus with a right angle. All the rectangle facts and all the rhombus facts hold.

**Pythagorean checks.** In a rectangle, a diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle made of two adjacent sides. In a rhombus that is not a square, the diagonals still form four right triangles, but the diagonal lengths are not equal.

In this course a **trapezoid** is a quadrilateral with *exactly one* pair of parallel sides (the bases). It is not a parallelogram.

</div>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A shorter account, in your own words, of what a passage says, in the same order, without new examples and without your opinion of it.</dd></div>

**Rules.** No quoted clauses unless a single word is a term of art. No ‘I think’. No added examples. If the original spends most of its time on one move, so does the précis.

## Physics · Projectile motion {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-projectile">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="projectile" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear projectile">Hear</button> projectile <span class="symbol">v<sub>x</sub> constant; a<sub>y</sub> = g</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A body in free flight under gravity alone (air resistance neglected in this course), with a constant horizontal velocity and a vertical acceleration of g downward. The two components are treated separately, then combined.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Medieval Latin, from Latin prōiectus, the perfect participle of prōicere (‘to throw forth’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A body is thrown horizontally at 20 m/s from a height of 19.6 m. Taking g = 9.8 m/s², the time to the ground is 2.0 s, and the horizontal range is 40 m.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A projectile is not ‘anything that is thrown’ once it is still being pushed, and the path is not two independent objects. It is one body, described by two components. Horizontal velocity does not ‘run out’.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 5’s free fall is the vertical part. Yesterday’s components are how the two parts are written. The path is a parabola (Day 9’s shape, now as a motion).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-range">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="range (of a projectile)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear range (of a projectile)">Hear</button> range (of a projectile)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The horizontal distance traveled from the launch point to the landing point, equal to v<sub>x</sub> times the time of flight, when the landing is at the same vertical level or at a stated lower level.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If v<sub>x</sub> = 20 m/s and the time of flight is 2.0 s, the range is 40 m.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Range is not the length of the curved path (that would be the distance traveled), and it is not the greatest height.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Time of flight comes from the vertical motion alone. The range then uses the constant v<sub>x</sub>. Mixing the two is the usual error.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Once a body is in flight and gravity is the only force (air neglected), the **horizontal** velocity stays constant, and the **vertical** motion is free fall with *a<sub>y</sub> = g* downward (Day 5).

**Horizontal launch, a clean time.** From rest vertically, *y = (1/2) g t²* downward. If the height is 19.6 m and *g* = 9.8 m/s², then 19.6 = (1/2)(9.8)*t*², so *t*² = 4 and *t* = 2.0 s. If *v<sub>x</sub>* = 20 m/s, the **range** is 20 × 2.0 = 40 m.

**Independence.** Changing *v<sub>x</sub>* does not change the time to fall from a given height. Changing the height does not change *v<sub>x</sub>*.

For launches at an angle, split *v* into *v<sub>x</sub>* and *v<sub>y</sub>* (Day 13), then treat the two motions separately. The path is a parabola.

</div>

## Elective · Artistic anatomy 2: muscles and tendons {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: landmark on the living figure or on a good plate, then a short drawing · After Day 5 (the skeleton under the skin)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-origin-insertion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="origin and insertion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear origin and insertion">Hear</button> origin and insertion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The two attachments of a muscle. The origin is the usually more stable attachment; the insertion is the usually more movable one. A simple first rule: the muscle pulls the insertion toward the origin. (Some muscles can work with the two ends reversed; do not force that exception into every case.)</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Origin is from Middle English, from Latin orīgō, orīginem (‘beginning, source, birth’), from orior (‘I rise’). Insertion is from insert + -ion.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The biceps brachii lies on the front of the upper arm and flexes the elbow; you can feel its distal tendon in the fold of the elbow. In the simple story, that tendon is at the insertion end, on the forearm, and the origin is up at the shoulder.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Origin and insertion are not ‘top and bottom’, and they are not the same as the belly (the fleshy middle of the muscle).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 5’s bony landmarks (clavicle, and the other bones you mapped) are where many of these attachments can be thought, even when you cannot see the bone.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-tendon">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="tendon" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear tendon">Hear</button> tendon</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The tough, cord-like or flattened connective tissue that often joins a muscle to a bone. It is not the muscle belly, and it does not bulge in the same way when the muscle works.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from French tendon or Medieval Latin tendō, from Ancient Greek τένων (‘sinew, tendon’), later associated with the Latin verb tendere (‘to stretch’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The biceps tendon in the fold of the elbow, or the Achilles tendon at the back of the heel, can be seen or felt as a cord, not as a thick belly.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A tendon is not a bone, and it is not a ligament (which joins bone to bone). You do not need a full classification of connective tissue for today’s check; you do need not to shade a tendon as if it were a swollen muscle.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>In a drawing, a tendon is often a hard, narrow form leading into a bony landmark.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

The skeleton (Day 5) is the frame. **Muscles** change the surface.

**Three named muscles, as landmarks, not as a complete atlas.**

- **Deltoid.** Caps the shoulder, giving the upper arm its rounded start from the clavicle and scapula down to the humerus. In a standing figure it is one of the first large forms of the arm.
- **Biceps brachii.** On the front of the upper arm; it flexes the elbow. Its **tendon** can be felt in the fold of the elbow.
- **Gastrocnemius.** The large calf muscle on the back of the lower leg; it helps to point the foot (stand on the toes) and is joined to the heel by the **Achilles tendon**.

**Origin and insertion.** Learn the words with the simple pulling rule, then look: when the biceps shortens, the forearm is drawn toward the shoulder and the elbow flexes.

**What to draw today.** From a living model, a photograph of a living arm or leg, or a good anatomical plate, make a short study (20–25 minutes) of one region — shoulder and upper arm, or calf and heel. Block in the bony landmarks first, then the muscle bellies, then the tendons as narrower forms. Do not invent muscles you cannot honestly see or feel.

This is not a medical course. Names are for seeing, not for diagnosis.

**Check.**
