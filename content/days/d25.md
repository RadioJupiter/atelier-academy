## Mathematics · Algebra VII: the sum of an arithmetic progression {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: pairing first with last (a pictorial Gauss pairing), then the formula; nth term from Day 20</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sum-of-an-arithmetic-progression">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sum of an arithmetic progression" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sum of an arithmetic progression">Hear</button> sum of an arithmetic progression</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The sum of the first n terms of an arithmetic progression with first term a and last term ℓ is S<sub>n</sub> = n/2 · (a + ℓ). Equivalently, S<sub>n</sub> = n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d), where d is the common difference. Day 20 already defined the progression and the nth term; today adds the sum.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The first ten terms of 3, 7, 11, … have last term 3 + 9×4 = 39, and sum 10/2 × (3 + 39) = 210.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>This is not the nth term itself, and it is not the sum of a geometric progression (next week). Do not add n × the middle term unless n is odd and you know the middle term, or unless you are really using n times the average of the first and last, which is the same formula.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A pictorial reason: pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on. Each pair sums to a + ℓ. There are n/2 such pairs (if n is odd, the middle term is the average, and the same formula still holds).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Day 20 defined an arithmetic progression: each term after the first is found by adding the same common difference *d*. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d.

**The sum, by pairing.** Write the sum forwards and backwards:

S = a + (a+d) + … + ℓ

S = ℓ + (ℓ−d) + … + a

Adding, 2S = n(a + ℓ), so S = n/2 · (a + ℓ).

If you do not yet know ℓ, use ℓ = a + (n − 1)d, and S = n/2 · (2a + (n − 1)d).

**A picture.** Ten terms 3, 7, 11, …, 39. Five pairs each summing to 42, and 5 × 42 = 210.

Worked examples you will use: first ten of 3, 7, 11, … = 210; first eight of 5, 9, 13, … : last term 5+7×4=33, S=4×(5+33)=152; twenty terms with a=6, d=3: S=10×(12+19×3)=10×(12+57)=690.

## Physics · Friday mix: work, friction, and kinetic energy {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: add the works, then W_net = ΔKE; friction from weeks 3–4 as a force that does negative work</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-net-work">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="net work" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear net work">Hear</button> net work</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The sum of the work done by every force acting on the object along the path, equal (in this course) to the work done by the net force along the path, and equal to the change in kinetic energy.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 20 N pull along 3 m, with a 5 N friction force opposite the motion: W_pull = 60 J, W_friction = −15 J, W_net = 45 J = ΔKE.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the work of one force alone, unless that force is the only one (or the only one with a component along the path).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Monday and Wednesday already computed single-force work and ΔKE. Today you add the works and then use the theorem.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**A Friday mix, not a new law.** You already have W = F s cosθ, KE = ½ m v², and W_net = ΔKE. Today you use them together, including friction as a force that usually does negative work (it opposes the displacement).

**A worked combination.** A 4 kg block is pulled 2 m along a straight path by a 20 N force along the path. Kinetic friction is 9.8 N (for example, μ_k = 0.25 and N = 4 × 9.8, which you already know how to compute). Then:

- W_pull = 20 × 2 = 40 J
- W_friction = −9.8 × 2 = −19.6 J
- W_net = 20.4 J = ΔKE

If the block started at rest, KE_final = 20.4 J, so ½ × 4 × v² = 20.4, v² = 10.2, v = √10.2 m/s. You may leave a square root, or remark that the number is a little more than 3.2 m/s, if a decimal is asked for and labeled as approximate.

**A cleaner check you will see:** 4 kg, 2 m, friction 9.8 N only (no pull), starting from a KE of 19.6 J: W_net = −19.6 J, and the block just stops.

This is still a first look at energy, beyond the Term 1 list. Next week adds gravitational potential energy near the Earth, as a model with *m g h*.


</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

**What the tradition says.** The Romans told that, in the mid-fifth century BCE, the law was written down on twelve tables and published, so that it was not only in the keeping of a few. Later Romans appealed to that written law as a foundation.

<li>Question: How is an inscribed angle different from a central angle, and why does the same arc matter?</li>
<li>An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle; a central angle has its vertex at the center. On the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle (Euclid III.20).</li>
<li>If the vertices stand on different arcs, the half-or-double relation for that particular central angle does not apply.</li>
<li>A near-miss is to treat any angle drawn in a circle as half of any other angle in the figure, without checking the arc.</li>

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The dative marks the person to or for whom (Mārcō). The accusative marks the thing given (rosam). They are different jobs; Mārcum would make Marcus the thing given.
2. pedir is e → i: pido. pensar is e → ie: pienso. They are different families; “the vowel changes” is not enough.
3. Chlorophyll absorbs more in the red and the blue than in the green, so more leftover green light reaches the eye. The near-miss treats the seen color as the most absorbed color, which is backwards in this account.
4. The converse swaps the two sides (If Q, then P). The contrapositive swaps and negates both (If not Q, then not P). Only the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original.
5. los libros is the subject; gustan agrees with that plural. *Yo gusto los libros copies the English shape and makes yo the subject of a verb meaning “like.”
6. Livy wrote in the late first century BCE and early first century CE, centuries after the early kings. A late narrative can still show later tradition and argument; it cannot, by itself, prove a named year of the eighth century BCE.
7. Books 1–4, whose foreground is Telemachus in Ithaca, Pylos, and Sparta. The war is already over and the return is already delayed; we enter a story in motion, and Odysseus is not yet on stage.
8.
