## Mathematics · Geometry XL: a sector of a circle {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a sector as a fraction of the circle already measured yesterday</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sector-of-a-circle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sector of a circle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sector of a circle">Hear</button> sector of a circle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A sector of a circle is the region bounded by two radii and the included arc. Its area is the same fraction of the circle’s area as the central angle is of 360°, or as the arc is of the circumference: A = (θ/360) × πr², when θ is measured in degrees.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin sector (Wiktionary). The Latin word is from secō, to cut.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 90° sector of a classroom circle of radius 6 has area (90/360) × 36π = 9π.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a triangle with two radii and a chord, and not the area of the whole circle. Not a first look at a central angle, which was taught on Day 21.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The central angle, already taught, now names the fraction of the circle that the sector occupies.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>A <strong>sector</strong> is a “slice” of a circle: two radii and the arc between them. The angle at the center is a central angle, already taught on Day 21. What is new is the area of that slice.</p>

<p>The slice is the same fraction of the whole circle as its angle is of a full turn. A full turn is 360° in the degree measure this course already uses. So</p>
<p>A<sub>sector</sub> = (θ / 360) × πr².</p>
<p>The same fraction of the circumference is the arc length: ℓ = (θ / 360) × 2πr. That is yesterday’s circumference, taken in part, not a new constant.</p>
<p>A 90° sector is a quarter of the circle. For r = 6, the whole area is 36π, and the sector is 9π.</p>

<p>The body paragraphs and the other-side paragraph now exist, or exist as drafts. Today you write the two ends of the same short essay: a brief opening and a close.</p>
<p>The <strong>opening</strong> names the set text or question and states the thesis. It does not preview every heading (“first I will… then I will…”). It does not begin with a vast claim about all poetry or all history.</p>
<p>The <strong>close</strong> returns to the thesis in wording that has earned the right to be said, because the body and the other side have been heard. It does not repeat the opening sentence with “therefore” in front. It does not add a new reason that should have been a body paragraph. It stops.</p>
<p>Write both today, as paragraphs or as tight paragraph-openers, and keep them with the same outline. Tomorrow you will assemble the whole essay.</p>

## Physics · The period of a mass on a spring {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: T = 2π√(m/k), from OpenStax, College Physics 2e, 16.3, contrasted with last week’s pendulum</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-period-of-a-mass-spring">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="period of a mass-spring" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear period of a mass-spring">Hear</button> period of a mass-spring</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>For a mass m on a spring that obeys Hooke’s law, and with no damping, the period is T = 2π√(m/k). T is larger for a larger mass and smaller for a stiffer spring. It does not depend on the amplitude.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>An invented classroom mass of 0.20 kg on a spring with k = 80 N/m has T = 2π√(0.20/80) = 2π√(0.0025) = 2π × 0.050 = 0.10π seconds, about 0.31 s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not last week’s simple-pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g). A pendulum’s period, for a small swing, does not depend on mass; a mass-spring period does.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>OpenStax, College Physics 2e, section 16.3, “Simple Harmonic Motion: A Special Periodic Motion” (CC BY), gives T = 2π√(m/k) and f = 1/(2π)√(k/m), independent of amplitude.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-simple-harmonic-motion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="simple harmonic motion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear simple harmonic motion">Hear</button> simple harmonic motion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Simple harmonic motion, in OpenStax’s wording, is oscillatory motion for a system whose net force can be described by Hooke’s law. A mass on a spring, without damping, is the standard example.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A block sliding on a frictionless surface, attached to a spring and released from a displacement, performs simple harmonic motion of amplitude equal to that first displacement, as in OpenStax Figure 16.9.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not every oscillation. Last week’s pendulum is close to this for a small swing (less than about 15°), which is why a similar square-root formula appeared, but the pendulum formula is not F = −kx written in the same direct way, and mass drops out of it.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The name is from OpenStax 16.3. This lesson uses it for the mass-spring system that obeys today’s formula.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>OpenStax, <em>College Physics 2e</em>, section 16.3 (CC BY), gives the period of a simple harmonic oscillator — a system whose net force follows Hooke’s law — as</p>
<p>T = 2π√(m/k),</p>
<p>and the frequency as f = 1/T = (1/(2π))√(k/m). Neither T nor f depends on the amplitude. Both depend on the mass m and on the force constant k only.</p>

<p>Today’s history extract, Dewing I.1.43–45, describes four triangles of stonework between the arches, ending in a circle that supports the dome. A later architectural name for those triangular transitions is <strong>pendentive</strong>.</p>
<p>Dewing does not use that later word. He writes “four triangles (trigôna).” This seminar uses the later name honestly, as a school term for what he describes, not as if it had appeared in the 1940 translation.</p>
<p>A pendentive is not the dome, and not a pier. It is the concave triangle that lets a dome sit on a square of arches.</p>
<p>A useful contrast is already in this course. The Pantheon in Rome, taught on Day 38, is a circular temple with a concrete dome and a central oculus, generally treated as a rebuild under Hadrian. That dome sits on a circular plan, not on four triangles rising from a square of arches. The two buildings are not to be merged. No official height or span is invented for either one.</p>
<p>The seeming in I.1.46 — a golden dome “suspended from Heaven” that is also said to rest on the circle — belongs with this form: the support is real, and the seeming is Dewing’s.</p>
