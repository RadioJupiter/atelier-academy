<p>This is review. A wave is a disturbance that propagates from the place it was created. Wavelength λ is the distance between adjacent identical parts. Wave velocity vw = λ / T = fλ. Example 16.8, already recomputed: λ = 10.0 m, T = 5.00 s, vw = 2.00 m/s, the speed of the disturbance, not the gull’s changing vertical speed. A transverse wave has its disturbance perpendicular to propagation (a guitar string, or visible light). A longitudinal wave has its disturbance parallel to propagation (sound in air or water). Water waves are a combination. Official measured speeds of sound, light, or earthquake P- and S-waves are not invented. Superposition, section 16.10, remains reserved. Resonance was Monday’s review, not a first look today.</p>

<p class="meta">40 min · Review of already-taught single discounts, successive discounts, and a markup then a discount, invented figures · tax reserved</p>

<div class="keyterms recall-terms" markdown="0">

<p>Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.</p>
</div>
<p>This is review, on invented figures only. A single 10 percent discount on $100 leaves $90. Two successive 10 percent discounts on $100 leave $81, not $80, because the second percent is taken off the remainder. A 10 percent markup on $100 makes $110; a 10 percent discount then, off the new price, leaves $99, not $100, and not $81. Tax and compound interest remain reserved. All figures are invented and labeled invented.</p>

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A second percent is taken off the current price, not off the original again, and not as a simple subtraction of percents. Two 10 percent discounts on $100 leave $81. Ten percent up, then 10 percent down, on $100, leaves $99.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Watch the successive discounts, then the markup-then-discount, then the near-misses.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>10 percent of 100 is 10, leaving 90; 10 percent of 90 is 9, leaving $81.</li>
<li>10 percent of 100 added on is 110; 10 percent of 110 is 11, leaving $99.</li>
<li>A miss is $80, or $100 as if the two 10 percents cancelled.</li>
</ol>

</div>

### Exercises

1. How do the two $100 paths differ?
2. Why must you not treat 10 percent up and 10 percent down as a net of zero?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The final price is $81. $80 would be a single 20 percent discount off the original, not two successive 10 percent discounts.
2. The final price is $99. $100 would treat the percents as cancelling. $81 was the successive-discount result, with no markup.
3. $200 becomes $220; 25 percent of 220 is 55; 220 − 55 = $165.

**Exercises.**
1. Successive discounts take 10 percent off $100 and then 10 percent off $90, leaving $81. The markup path adds 10 percent first, then takes 10 percent off $110, leaving $99.
2. The discount is a percent of the current price, so 10 percent of 110 is larger than 10 percent of 100, and the two steps do not cancel.
