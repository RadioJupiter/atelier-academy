## Physics · Intensity of a wave, a first look, from OpenStax 16.11 {#physics}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: I = P/A and E = IAt; official Example 16.9, 700 W/m², E = 5.04 × 10^6 J</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-intensity-of-a-wave">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="intensity of a wave" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear intensity of a wave">Hear</button> intensity of a wave</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In OpenStax 16.11, intensity I is power per unit area, I = P/A, where P is the power carried by the wave through area A. The SI unit is watts per square meter, W/m². A wave’s energy is directly proportional to its amplitude squared. Because power is energy per unit time, E = IAt when I, A, and t are constant.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>OpenStax Example 16.9 uses the official average intensity of sunlight on Earth’s surface, about 700 W/m², on a collector of area 0.500 m² in 4.00 h: E = IAt = 5.04 × 10^6 J.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not last week’s amplitude itself, which is a displacement, not a power per unit area. Not an official living solar figure other than the book’s 700 W/m² and the book’s 1300 W/m² just above the atmosphere, if that second figure is named as the book’s. Decibels remain reserved.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s superposition, and the already-taught idea that a wave carries a disturbance. Example 16.10, intensity and constructive interference, waits until Wednesday.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>OpenStax, <em>College Physics 2e</em>, 16.11, “Energy in Waves: Intensity,” is this week’s physics source. Access for free at the OpenStax 16.11 page. All waves carry energy. The energy in a wave is directly proportional to its amplitude squared. Intensity I is power per unit area: I = P/A, in watts per square meter. When I, A, and t are constant, E = IAt, because P = E/t.</p>
<p>Example 16.9, official figures from the book: the average intensity of sunlight on Earth’s surface is about 700 W/m². For a collector of area 0.500 m² in 4.00 h, E = 700 × 0.500 × 4.00 × 3600 = 5.04 × 10^6 J. If the same sunlight is concentrated onto an area 200 times smaller, I′ = 200 × 700 = 1.40 × 10^5 W/m². The book also states that infrared and visible energy from the Sun impinge on Earth at an intensity of 1300 W/m² just above the atmosphere; that is the book’s figure, not a classroom invention. Decibels remain reserved. Invent no official living solar measurement beyond these book figures.</p>

## Seminar · Logic: complementary events, P(not A) = 1 − P(A), a first look {#seminar}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: P(not A) = 1 − P(A) on last week’s invented fair cube and spinner</p>

<article class="term" id="term-complementary-events">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="complementary events" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear complementary events">Hear</button> complementary events</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two events are complementary when one is exactly the cases in which the other does not happen, and one of the two must happen. If P(A) is the probability of A, then P(not A) = 1 − P(A), provided the outcomes are the same equally likely set already taught, and one of them must happen.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On an invented fair six-faced cube, P(face 4) = 1/6, so P(not 4) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6. P(an even face) = 1/2, so P(not even) = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not an official probability of a real event, such as weather. Not a lesson in gambling. Not a claim that a real cube in the room has been tested as fair. Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s equally likely outcomes, with probability 1/n for one named outcome among n equally likely ones.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>Last week’s logic lesson treated equally likely outcomes: if n outcomes are equally likely and one must happen, the probability of one named outcome is 1/n. Today a first look at complementary events. Two events are complementary when one is exactly the cases in which the other does not happen, and one of the two must happen. Then P(not A) = 1 − P(A). On an invented fair six-faced cube, P(face 4) = 1/6, so P(not 4) = 5/6. The even faces are 2, 4, and 6, so P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2, and P(not even) = 1/2. On an invented fair eight-sector spinner, P(one named sector) = 1/8, so P(not that sector) = 7/8. If two named sectors are taken together, P(that pair) = 2/8 = 1/4, and P(not that pair) = 3/4. Do not invent an official probability for the weather, or for a living person’s future. This is not a gambling lesson. Outcomes that are not equally likely remain reserved.</p>
