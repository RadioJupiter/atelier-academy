**Notice: the accusative** marks the *object* of an action. Singular endings: *-um* (masculine and neuter in *-us/-um*) and *-am* (feminine in *-a*).

| nominative (subject) | accusative (object) |
|---|---|
| serv**us** | serv**um** |
| ancill**a** | ancill**am** |
| puer | puer**um** |

*Puer puell**am** pulsat. Puella puer**um** pulsat.* The endings, not the word order, show who does what. Latin word order is free; the usual order puts the verb last.

**Verbs** in this chapter are third person singular: *-at* (vocat, pulsat), *-et* (videt, rīdet), *-it* (venit, dormit).

<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A line that crosses two or more other lines, forming corresponding, alternate interior, and same-side interior angles.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French transversal, from Medieval Latin trānsversālis, from Latin trānsversus (‘turned across’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>When the two lines are parallel, alternate interior angles are equal.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A transversal is not itself required to be perpendicular, and it is not ‘any third line in the plane’ if it does not cut the other two.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Equal alternate interior angles (or equal corresponding angles) are the test that the two lines are parallel.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-exterior-angle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="exterior angle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear exterior angle">Hear</button> exterior angle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An angle formed by one side of a triangle and the extension of an adjacent side. It equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 60°, the exterior angle at C is 110°.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not the same as an adjacent interior angle, and it is not ‘any angle outside the triangle’ with no side in common.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The corollary follows from the theorem that the angles of a triangle add to 180°, which itself uses a parallel and a transversal.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

When a line *t* crosses two other lines, it is a **transversal**. If those two lines are **parallel**, corresponding angles are equal, alternate interior angles are equal, and consecutive interior angles add to 180°. Conversely, if (for example) corresponding angles are equal, the lines are parallel. These facts are the engine of many later proofs.

**The parallel axiom.** Through a point not on a line, there is *at most one* line parallel to the given line. (Kiselev proves that at least one exists; the axiom supplies uniqueness.)

**Angles formed by a transversal.** When a line crosses two lines, it forms corresponding, alternate interior and same-side interior angles. **Theorem:** two lines are parallel if and only if alternate interior angles are equal (equivalently, if corresponding angles are equal, or same-side interior angles add to 180°).

**Theorem.** The angles of a triangle add to 180°.
*Proof.* Through vertex B of triangle ABC draw the line parallel to AC. The two angles it makes with BA and BC are equal to ∠A and ∠C (alternate interior angles), and together with ∠B they form a straight angle. ∎

**Corollary (exterior angle).** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles not adjacent to it.

</div>

A single paragraph is one unit of thought: a **topic sentence** that states the claim, then sentences that support it with reason or evidence, then (often) a close that does not merely repeat the first sentence. Hochman's method still applies: write tight sentences first, then arrange them. One paragraph, one job.

**Plan before drafting.** An SPO has: a **topic sentence** (written in full); three or four **supporting details** written as brief notes, not sentences (abbreviations and symbols welcome, so you cannot copy them straight into the draft); and a **concluding sentence** (written in full) that restates the point in a new way. The notes force you to *compose* sentences when drafting, using the strategies from Days 1–3.

**Topic sentence types:** a statement, a question, an "although" sentence, or an appositive sentence.

**Example SPO.** T.S.: *Although Franklin had little schooling, he trained himself into a fine writer by a deliberate method.*
1. Spectator vol. → read repeatedly, admired
2. short hints → put away → rebuilt from memory
3. compared w/ original → corrected faults
4. prose → verse → prose = stock of words
C.S.: *His method shows that good style can be built step by step, by anyone willing to compare work against a master.*

## Physics · Newton's first law: inertia {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Peer Instruction protocol</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-inertia">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inertia" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inertia">Hear</button> inertia</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The tendency of a body to keep its velocity — to stay at rest, or to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed — unless a net force acts on it. This is Newton’s first law.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin inertia (‘inactivity, lack of skill’), from iners, from in- (‘without’) + ars (‘skill, art’). A sense close to the modern physics one was first used in New Latin by Johannes Kepler.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A puck on smooth ice keeps sliding at nearly constant velocity because the net force is almost zero.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Inertia is not a force, and it is not ‘a force that keeps things moving’. No net force is needed to keep a constant velocity.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A constant velocity (Day 1) is what inertia maintains. Acceleration (Day 3) is what happens when a net force breaks that maintenance.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-net-force">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="net force" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear net force">Hear</button> net force <span class="symbol">F<sub>net</sub></span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The vector sum of all forces on a body. Newton’s first law says that if the net force is zero, the velocity does not change.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Force is from Old French force, from Latin fortia, from fortis (‘strong’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Two equal opposite pushes cancel: F<sub>net</sub> = 0, so the object does not accelerate.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A net force of zero does not mean ‘no forces act’. Forces can be present and cancel.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Inertia describes the motion when F<sub>net</sub> = 0. Day 8 will relate a nonzero net force to acceleration.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Newton's **first law**: a body keeps its velocity (including remaining at rest) unless a net force acts on it. 'Net' matters: several forces can cancel, and then the velocity does not change. **Inertia** is this tendency to keep the present velocity. A force is not needed to keep a body moving at a steady speed in a straight line (in this model); a force is needed to *change* that velocity.

**Pre-reading.** An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a **net** external force acts on it. Motion does not need a force; *change* of motion does. On Earth, moving things slow down because friction and air resistance act on them, which is why the law was not obvious before Galileo and Newton. **Net force** is the vector sum of all forces. Forces are measured in newtons (N).

An egg is a simple form with a single curved surface, used to learn **light on form**. One main light makes a highlight, a light family, a terminator, a shadow family, and a reflected light in the shadow, plus a cast shadow on the cloth. Keep the families simple; do not invent extra lights.

**The structure of light on a curved form** (one light source):
- **Light family:** highlight → light → halftones, getting darker as the surface turns away.
- **Terminator / core shadow:** where the form turns away from the light completely; the darkest part of the form shadow is usually just inside it.
- **Shadow family:** form shadow, softened by **reflected light** bouncing in from nearby surfaces. Reflected light is always darker than the halftones in the light. Keeping the families separate is what makes a form look solid.
- **Cast shadow:** the shadow the object throws on the surface, darkest and sharpest where the object touches it (the **occlusion** shadow).

## Logic · Validity, soundness and two classic fallacies {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Logic</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-validity">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="validity" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear validity">Hear</button> validity</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A property of an argument’s form: the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are true. Validity does not require the premises to be actually true.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From valid + -ity, from Middle French validité, from Latin validitās, from validus, from valēre (‘to be strong’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘All fish can fly; sharks are fish; so sharks can fly’ is valid (and unsound). If the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be true.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Valid does not mean ‘true’, and it does not mean ‘persuasive’. A valid argument can have a false conclusion if a premise is false.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 1’s conditionals are the forms being tested: modus ponens and modus tollens are valid; affirming the consequent is not.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-soundness">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="soundness" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear soundness">Hear</button> soundness</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The property of an argument that is valid and whose premises are actually true. Only then must the conclusion be true in the world.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From sound in the sense ‘whole, reliable’ + -ness. (Not the same word as musical sound.)</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘If a triangle is equilateral, it is isosceles. This triangle is not isosceles. So it is not equilateral’ can be sound, because the form is modus tollens and the first premise is true.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Sound is not a synonym of valid. ‘All birds can fly; penguins are birds; so penguins can fly’ is valid but unsound.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Validity is about form (Day 1’s truth tables). Soundness adds a check on the facts.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-fallacy">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="fallacy" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear fallacy">Hear</button> fallacy</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A tempting but invalid form of argument. Two classics: affirming the consequent, and denying the antecedent.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin fallācia, from fallere (‘to deceive, to be mistaken’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘If the plate is accurate, the proportions match. The proportions match. So the plate is accurate.’ That is affirming the consequent, and it is invalid.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A fallacy is not ‘any false conclusion’, and a true conclusion can still have been reached by a fallacy.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The contrapositive (Day 1) is safe. Affirming the consequent looks similar and is not.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


An argument is **valid** if its conclusion *must* be true whenever its premises are true. It is **sound** if it is valid *and* its premises are actually true. Validity depends only on form.

| form | pattern | valid? |
|---|---|---|
| modus ponens | p → q; p; therefore q | valid |
| modus tollens | p → q; ¬q; therefore ¬p | valid |
| affirming the consequent | p → q; q; therefore p | **invalid** |
| denying the antecedent | p → q; ¬p; therefore ¬q | **invalid** |

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

An argument is valid if the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are. It is sound if it is valid *and* the premises are actually true. Valid forms: modus ponens, modus tollens. Invalid: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent. Validity is about form, not about whether you like the conclusion.

<p>Key terms: validity, soundness, fallacy.</p>
