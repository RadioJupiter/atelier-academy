## Mathematics · Geometry LXXIX: the law of cosines, a first look {#math}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, in degrees; invented figures labeled invented</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="the law of cosines" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear the law of cosines">Hear</button> the law of cosines</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The law of cosines says that in any triangle, c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, where c is the side opposite angle C, and a and b are the other two sides. When C is 90°, cos 90° = 0, and the formula becomes c² = a² + b², the already-taught Pythagorean theorem. It is not the law of sines.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On an invented triangle with sides 5 and 5 and included angle 60°, c² = 25 + 25 − 2·5·5·(1/2) = 25, so c = 5. The figures 5, 5, and 60° are invented classroom numbers.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the law of sines, a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Not a claim that the formula holds only in a right triangle. Not a conversion into radians.</dd></div>

</dl></article>
</div>

## Physics · Sound intensity level, and the decibel, a first look, from OpenStax 17.3 {#physics}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: β = 10 log10(I/I0), I0 = 10^−12 W/m²; official table and definition only</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sound-intensity-level-the-decibel">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sound intensity level (the decibel)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sound intensity level (the decibel)">Hear</button> sound intensity level (the decibel)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The sound intensity level β, in decibels, is defined in OpenStax 17.3 by β = 10 log10(I/I0), where I is the sound intensity in watts per square meter and I0 = 10^−12 W/m² is the reference intensity, the threshold of hearing at 1000 Hz. It is not the intensity I itself, and it is not a sound pressure level in water.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If I = I0, then log10(1) = 0, so β = 0 dB, the threshold of hearing in Table 17.2. If I is ten times I0, β = 10 dB. These are official values from the definition and from Table 17.2, not invented official levels of a place the table does not name.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the intensity I in W/m². Not a sound pressure level, a different decibel scale used especially in water, which OpenStax 17.3 sets aside. Not an invented official decibel level of a real place not in Table 17.2.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s sound and intensity of a wave. The bel, OpenStax notes, is named for Alexander Graham Bell.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>OpenStax 17.3, “Sound Intensity and Sound Level,” at https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level, starts from intensity already taught: I is the power per unit area carried by a wave, I = P/A, in W/m². How the ear perceives sound is more accurately described by the logarithm of the intensity than by the intensity itself. The sound intensity level β in decibels is defined to be β = 10 log10(I/I0), where I0 = 10^−12 W/m² is the lowest, or threshold, intensity of sound a person with normal hearing can perceive at 1000 Hz. Because β is defined in terms of a ratio, it is a unitless quantity; the units of decibels (dB) mark that the ratio is multiplied by 10. The bel, OpenStax notes, is named for Alexander Graham Bell.</p><p>If I = I0, log10(1) = 0, so β = 0 dB: the threshold of hearing is 0 decibels. Table 17.2 gives official pairs: 0 dB with 1×10^−12 W/m², threshold of hearing at 1000 Hz; 10 dB with 1×10^−11 W/m², rustle of leaves; 60 dB with 1×10^−6 W/m², normal conversation; 80 dB with 1×10^−4 W/m², loud radio, classroom lecture. Each factor of 10 in intensity corresponds to 10 dB. Do not invent an official decibel level of a real place the table does not name. The sound pressure level used especially in water remains reserved.</p>

## Seminar · Logic: independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B), a first look {#seminar}

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="independent events" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear independent events">Hear</button> independent events</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two events are independent when the happening of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Independence is not the same as mutually exclusive events, which cannot both happen.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On two invented fair coins, tossed independently, P(heads on both) = (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4. The coins and the tosses are invented classroom figures, not an official experiment.</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s mutually exclusive events, and earlier complementary and equally likely outcomes. Inclusion-exclusion remains reserved, except as last week’s overlap miss.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<p>Last week, mutually exclusive events could not both happen, and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) when they were mutually exclusive. Today a different relation: independent events. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).</p><p>On two invented fair coins, tossed independently, P(heads on the first) = 1/2 and P(heads on the second) = 1/2, so P(both heads) = 1/4. ### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Two events are independent when the happening of one does not change the probability of the other. Then P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
2. 1/4; 1/12.
3. 4 and 5 cannot both happen on one roll; they are mutually exclusive, and P(4 and 5) = 0.
4. Model: **independent events.** Own words should match this idea: Two events are independent when the happening of one does not change the probability of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Independence is not the same as mutually exclusive events, which cannot both happen. Near-miss: Not mutually exclusive events, which cannot both happen, so P(A and B) = 0, not P(A)P(B). Not a claim that every pair of events is independent. Not an official probability of a real event. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
