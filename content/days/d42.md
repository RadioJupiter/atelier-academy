## Mathematics · Geometry XIX: the equation of a circle {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: the already-taught locus, then the coordinate equation (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-equation-of-a-circle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="equation of a circle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear equation of a circle">Hear</button> equation of a circle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The coordinate form of the locus of points at a fixed distance r from a center (h, k): (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². When the center is the origin, this is x² + y² = r².</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>x² + y² = 4 is the circle of radius 2 centered at (0, 0). The point (0, 2) satisfies it: 0 + 4 = 4. The point (3, 0) does not: 9 ≠ 4.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the graph of a function y = f(x) in general (a full circle fails the “one y for each x” test). And not a parabola, whose equation is quadratic in one variable only, in the form you have used.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The locus definition of a circle (Geometry XVI), and the distance idea already in the plane. Completing the square can move a general x² + y² + … form into (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², but today’s checks stay with the centered form.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A circle was already defined as a locus: all points at one fixed distance, the radius, from a center (Geometry XVI). Today that same locus is written in coordinates.

**The equation.** If the center is **(h, k)** and the radius is **r**, a point **(x, y)** is on the circle exactly when **(x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²**. That is the Pythagorean statement of the distance from the center (the squared distance is r²). When the center is the origin, **h = 0** and **k = 0**, so **x² + y² = r²**.

**A check.** On x² + y² = 4, the radius is 2. (0, 2) works; (2, 0) works; (0, 0) does not, because it is the center, not a point on the circle.

**A circle is not, in general, the graph of a single function y = f(x).** A vertical line through the origin meets x² + y² = 4 at (0, 2) and (0, −2): two y-values. Function graphs from Algebra IX–X stay functions; this is a locus in the plane.

**Classroom circles stay labeled invented** (a radius-2 classroom circle is not a NASA orbit).

The path from the cornea to the retina and out by the optic nerve is already taught. The National Eye Institute (NEI) page “How the eyes work” says that in the retina, **special cells called photoreceptors turn the light into electrical signals**, that those signals travel through the **optic nerve** to the brain, and that **the brain turns the signals into the images you see**.

We still **invent no count** of photoreceptors, and no count of rods or cones. Day 34 already refused those counts. Today takes the NEI’s word *photoreceptor* and the brain’s job in making the image, including color as an experience the brain constructs from signals, not as a paint already “in” the light as a little colored chip on the retina.

**Color vision deficiency.** The NEI’s page on color blindness (color vision deficiency) says that it means you see colors differently than most people; that most of the time it makes certain colors hard to tell apart; that complete color vision deficiency (seeing no color at all) is rare; that the most common types are genetic and often run in families; and that men have a much higher risk than women. The common test it describes is a circle of colored dots with a shape inside. This lesson is not a diagnosis of anyone, and it is not a demand to use a test chart as a toy.

**Source.** National Eye Institute, “How the eyes work,” and “Color blindness,” accessed for this lesson. We do not invent a receptor count, a wavelength in nanometers, or a classroom diagnosis.
