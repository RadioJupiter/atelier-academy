<p>Friday mixes the law of cosines in both directions, and uses last week’s law of sines only as a check when a side and its opposite angle are both known. Recovering the Pythagorean theorem when C is 90° is part of the mix, not a new first look. Radians and the ambiguous case remain reserved. Invented figures stay labeled invented.</p>

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C, and cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab). When C is 90°, the first formula is the Pythagorean theorem.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>On an invented 30°-30°-120° triangle with equal sides 4, find the side opposite 120° by the law of cosines, and check it with the law of sines.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>By cosines: c² = 16 + 16 − 2·4·4·(−1/2) = 48, so c = 4√3.</li>
<li>By sines: 4 / sin 30° = c / sin 120°. Sin 30° = 1/2 and sin 120° = √3/2, so 4 / (1/2) = c / (√3/2), hence c = 4√3.</li>
<li>The two laws agree. The triangle is invented.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>On an invented triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, what is the angle opposite 5, and why is that the Pythagorean theorem read in the other direction?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The cosine</summary>

cos C = (9 + 16 − 25)/24 = 0, so C = 90°.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The special case</summary>

c² = a² + b² is the same statement as a 90° included or opposite angle.

</details>

</div>

### Exercises

3. What remains reserved?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C. When C is 90°, c² = a² + b².
2. 4√3, because c² = 48. The law of sines gives the same 4√3, because 4 / sin 30° = (4√3) / sin 120°.
