<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="distance from the center" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear distance from the center">Hear</button> distance from the center</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The perpendicular distance from the center of a circle to a chord. A radius to an endpoint, that perpendicular, and a half-chord form a right triangle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If the radius is 10 and the chord is 6 from the center, the half-chord is √(10² − 6²) = 8, so the chord is 16.</dd></div>

A **chord** joins two points of a circle. A **diameter** is a chord through the center.

**Theorem.** The perpendicular from the center to a chord bisects the chord. Conversely, the segment from the center to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord.

**The right triangle.** If *O* is the center, *AB* a chord, and *M* the foot of the perpendicular from *O* to *AB*, then *M* is the midpoint of *AB*, and △OMA is right-angled at *M*. So

*OM*² + *AM*² = *OA*².

Equal chords lie at equal distances from the center; a longer chord lies closer to the center. The longest chord is a diameter, at distance 0.

</div>

## Writing & Rhetoric · Quotations and attribution {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: introduce, quote, and explain; no quotation left to ‘speak for itself’</p>

<article class="term" id="term-quotation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="quotation" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear quotation">Hear</button> quotation</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Someone else’s exact words, placed in quotation marks (or indented as a block, for a longer extract), introduced by a sentence of your own, and followed by an explanation in your own words.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Lincoln writes that the nation was ‘dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.’ The quoted phrase is the standard he then says the war is testing.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A quotation is not a substitute for a topic sentence, and a sentence that is only a quotation is not a paragraph. Do not let a quoted clause do the arguing for you.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 9’s analytical essay already required that every quotation be introduced and explained. Today that rule is practiced as a skill of its own.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-attribution">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="attribution" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear attribution">Hear</button> attribution</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The naming of who said or wrote the words, and in what work or on what occasion, so that a reader can find the source. In this course a short prose quotation is attributed in the sentence that introduces it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French attribution, from Latin attribūtiō.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘In the first paragraph of the Gettysburg Address, Lincoln writes that…’ is an attribution. A bare pair of quotation marks with no name is not.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Attribution is not a bibliography entry alone, and it is not ‘someone once said’. If the writer or the work is missing, the quotation is unearned.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Public-domain sources still need attribution. The fact that a text is free to quote does not make it anonymous.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A quotation has three parts: an **introduction** (who, where, and why the words are being used), the **words themselves** in quotation marks, and an **explanation** of what the words do in your argument.

**How much to quote.** Quote a phrase or a sentence you will actually use. Do not quote a whole paragraph and then paraphrase it. If you can say it just as well in your own words, summarize instead (Day 15).

**Punctuation, for this course.** The introducing clause, then a comma or a colon, then the quotation marks. The explanation is a new sentence, not a trailing ‘which means that’ tacked on without a verb of your own.

**Today’s practice.** Using Lincoln or Johnson (already in your notes), write three introduced-and-explained quotations that could sit in the two-paragraph essay outlined on Day 16.

## Physics · Momentum {#physics}

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-momentum">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="momentum" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear momentum">Hear</button> momentum <span class="symbol">p = mv</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The product of mass and velocity, p = m v, a vector in the same direction as the velocity. The SI unit is kg·m/s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin mōmentum; a doublet of moment and movement.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 3 kg body moving at 4 m/s has momentum 12 kg·m/s in the direction of the velocity.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Momentum is not the same as inertia (a property of mass alone), and it is not the same as kinetic energy (which uses v² and is not a vector). A heavy body at rest has zero momentum.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Newton’s second law can be read as: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum. Day 10’s third law will give conservation in the next paragraph.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-conservation-of-momentum">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="conservation of momentum" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear conservation of momentum">Hear</button> conservation of momentum</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>If two (or more) bodies interact and the net external force on the system is zero (or is negligible for a short collision), the total momentum of the system does not change. Internal action–reaction pairs (Day 10) cancel in the total.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 3 kg cart at 4 m/s catches up to a 1 kg cart at rest, and they stick: 12 = (3+1)v, so v = 3 m/s in the original direction.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Conservation of momentum is not ‘every body keeps its own momentum’, and it is not a claim that energy is always conserved in a collision (a sticking collision loses kinetic energy).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 10: forces between two bodies are equal and opposite. Over the same time, the momentum changes are equal and opposite, so the total is constant when no outside force matters.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Momentum** is *p = m v*, a vector. A net force changes momentum: *F<sub>net</sub> = Δp / Δt* (this is Newton’s second law, written for a change in *mv*).

**A closed system.** For two carts on a level track, the internal pushes during a collision are an action–reaction pair (Day 10). If friction and other outside horizontal forces are negligible for that short time, the total momentum *p<sub>1</sub> + p<sub>2</sub>* is the same before and after.

**A sticking collision (inelastic).** 3 kg at 4 m/s, 1 kg at rest, they stick: total *p* before is 12 kg·m/s, total mass after is 4 kg, so *v* = 3 m/s. Kinetic energy is not conserved in this collision (check: before, (1/2)(3)(16) = 24 J; after, (1/2)(4)(9) = 18 J). Momentum still is.

Always pick a direction as positive, and let a velocity the other way be negative.

## Atelier · Lost-and-found edges {#atelier}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: continue the plaster feature or the still life; study the quality of edges · After terminators (Day 8) and today’s preceding solids</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-lost-and-found-edge">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="lost-and-found edge" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear lost-and-found edge">Hear</button> lost-and-found edge</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A contour or an inner edge that is sharp in one passage (found) and soft or gone in another (lost), according to the light and the turn of the form. The drawing’s outline is not a wire of even thickness all the way around.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-edge-quality">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="edge quality" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear edge quality">Hear</button> edge quality</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>How sudden or gradual a change of value is at a boundary: razor-sharp, firm, soft, or lost. Edge quality is decided after the large values are in, or along with them, not as a decorative outline drawn first and never revised.</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>If you find every edge, the form can look cut out. If you lose every edge, the form can fall apart. The work is to vary them on purpose.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

A drawing is not a coloring-book outline filled in. **Edges** change.

**Found.** A firm, clear boundary: a cube’s plane break, a cast-shadow edge close to the object in a hard light, a ridge of plaster turned against a darker ground.

**Lost.** A boundary that almost disappears: two values that nearly match (a shadow side against a dark cloth), or a slow turn (the shadow contour of a sphere). You still know the form is there, because the construction and the neighboring values hold it.

**Order.** Keep yesterday’s (or the still life’s) construction. Then, on a pass of edge quality, walk around every contour and every inner edge and ask: is this found, firm, soft, or lost, in the set-up I am looking at? Change the drawing to match, rather than leaving a wire.

**Background.** A lost edge often needs the background to be brought up in value so that the contour can disappear into it. Do not leave a white halo around the object ‘to keep it safe’.

**A common error.** A single, even outline; or a smear that loses a sharp ridge you can clearly see.

Continue one study (plaster feature or the group of solids) and make the edges deliberate.

## Art history · The kouros and contrapposto {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: close looking, then a comparison across two periods · After Day 13 (Aegean) and Day 8 (Egypt)</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>

Two moments in the Greek standing figure, not a story of ‘progress’ as a moral, but a change you can see.

**The kouros (Archaic).** A typical kouros stands frontal, left foot forward, weight even, fists close to the thighs, hair often patterned in a bead-like or wig-like convention, the face with the **Archaic smile**. Dates for the type run through the seventh and sixth centuries BCE; do not treat a single year as exact unless an inscription or a secure context forces it. Function: some are funerary, some votive; argue from evidence.

**Comparison with Egypt.** The shared ‘one foot forward, frontal, compact arms’ is real and often taught as a connection. The differences are also real: nudity as a convention for the male youth, a different treatment of the body in the round, and no Egyptian composite view (profile head with a frontal eye, and so on).

**Contrapposto and the Kritios Boy.** In the early fifth century BCE, after the Archaic period, the **Kritios Boy** (found on the Acropolis of Athens; the name comes from a later attribution, not from a signature on the statue that we must take as certain) shows **weight on one leg**, a relaxed other leg, a tilt of the pelvis, a slight turn of the head, and no Archaic smile. That counter-tilt is **contrapposto**.

Look at the two stances as problems of balance. What has to happen at the hip, the knee, and the shoulder for the weight to shift and the figure still to stand?
