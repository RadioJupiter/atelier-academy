<article class="term" id="term-inradius">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inradius" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inradius">Hear</button> inradius</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The radius of the incircle: the perpendicular distance from the incenter to any side. In a triangle, A = r s, where A is the area and s is the semiperimeter (half the perimeter).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>For the invented 6-8-10 right triangle, A = (6 · 8)/2 = 24, s = 12, and r = 24 / 12 = 2.</dd></div>

</dl></article>

Yesterday the incircle was found by **equal tangents**. Today is its center and its radius.

The **incenter** I is the point inside the triangle that is equally far from all three sides. Each angle bisector is the set of points equally far from two sides (a use of the already-taught angle bisector). So the angle bisectors meet at I, and a perpendicular from I to a side is the **inradius** r. You already have a construction for an angle bisector (Day 12); today names what their meeting point is for.

**Why A = r s.** The three small triangles formed by I and the sides have the same height r. Their areas add to the area A of the triangle: A = (1/2) a r + (1/2) b r + (1/2) c r = r · (a + b + c)/2 = r s, where s is the semiperimeter.

**A worked triangle.** The invented 6-8-10 triangle from Day 47 is a right triangle, so A = (6 · 8)/2 = 24. The perimeter is 24, so s = 12. Then r = A / s = 24 / 12 = 2.

The tangent lengths from Day 47 (4, 2, and 6) are not needed for this computation of r, but they describe the same incircle.
