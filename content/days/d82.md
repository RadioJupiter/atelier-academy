## Mathematics · Geometry XXXIX: the area of a circle {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a limiting rearrangement of equal sectors into a near-parallelogram, then A = πr²</p>

<p>Yesterday gave the length once around a circle: C = 2πr. Today is the area inside it.</p>

<p>The area of that near-parallelogram is base × height ≈ (πr) × r = πr². In the limit of thinner and thinner sectors, this is the area of the circle:</p>
<p>A = πr².</p>
<p>This course treats that as the working school formula. Euclid XII.2 says, in other words, that circles are to one another as the squares on their diameters, which is the same ratio as the squares of the radii. The formula A = πr² is the school way of writing that fact with yesterday’s π.</p>
<p>A classroom circle of radius 6 has A = 36π. Leave exact answers in terms of π.</p>

<p>An analytical essay that never meets a difficulty looks stronger than it is. Today you write the other-side paragraph: a fair statement of an objection or of another reading, and then a reply that still defends the thesis.</p>
<p>If last week’s outline already has an other-side heading, use it. If it does not, add a heading now, honestly, rather than pretending the outline had one.</p>
<p>The paragraph should do three things, in order:</p>
<p>1. State the other side in a form that side could accept. A straw man, already taught, is a miss.</p>
<p>2. Concede what is genuinely right in it, if something is.</p>
<p>3. Answer it, and return to the thesis, without sneering and without dropping the claim.</p>
<p>An although-sentence, taught on Day 9, is a useful way to open or to turn: it concedes a real point, then states the judgment you can still defend. It is not a first look today.</p>

## Science · Water: density, and why ice floats {#science}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: density as mass per unit volume, then sourced figures for ice and liquid water at 0 °C</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-density">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="density" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear density">Hear</button> density</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Density is mass per unit volume: ρ = m / V. In this lesson the usual classroom units are grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³), which are the same size as grams per milliliter.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not mass alone, and not volume alone. A large piece and a small piece of the same uniform material have the same density.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is the physical sense of density (mass density), not a looser everyday use of the word.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-why-ice-floats">

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Ice floats on liquid water because the ice is less dense than the liquid. At 0 °C and standard atmospheric pressure, ice Ih has a density of 0.9167 g/cm³, and liquid water has a density of 0.99984 g/cm³, in the NIST table by Allan H. Harvey used here.</dd></div>

<p>Water’s states were already taught, including the ice point at one atmosphere. Today is a new question: why does ice float?</p>
<p><strong>Density</strong> is mass per unit volume, ρ = m / V. An invented classroom sample of 20 g in 10 cm³ has density 2 g/cm³. The same material in a larger piece would have a larger mass and a larger volume, and the same density if it is uniform.</p>

<p>The U.S. Geological Survey states the qualitative fact plainly: ice is less dense than liquid water, which is why ice cubes float.</p>
<p>For numbers, this lesson uses a NIST table by Allan H. Harvey (an update to a <em>CRC Handbook</em> table), at standard atmospheric pressure, 101.325 kPa:</p>
<p>Ice Ih at 0 °C: 0.9167 g/cm³. Liquid water at 0 °C: 0.99984 g/cm³.</p>
<p>Those two figures are not to be blended into a single official percentage, and they are not to be mixed with a different table’s slightly different liquid-water value. The comparison is enough: at 0 °C, ice is less dense than the liquid, so it floats.</p>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="antecedent and consequent, music" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear antecedent and consequent, music">Hear</button> antecedent and consequent (music)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The antecedent is the first phrase of a period, often ending on V (a half cadence). The consequent is the answering phrase, often ending on I (an authentic cadence). The two are heard as a question and an answer, not as two unrelated starts.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If the antecedent begins with a short motif and ends on V, the consequent may begin like the antecedent and then turn to close on I.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the logical terms antecedent and consequent already used in conditionals (Day 21 and Day 41). The names are the same; the job here is musical.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Because the cadences themselves are already known, today’s Check can ask you to name which cadence ends each phrase, without reteaching those cadences as first looks.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>A cadence can end a phrase. A <strong>period</strong> is two phrases that belong together as a pair.</p>
<p>The first phrase is the <strong>antecedent</strong>. It often ends with a weaker close, especially a half cadence on V, already taught on Day 52. The second phrase is the <strong>consequent</strong>. It often answers with a stronger close, especially an authentic cadence to I, already taught in week 15.</p>
<p>The pairing is the new work. This is not a first look at those cadences, and not a first look at last week’s deceptive cadence (V–vi), which would be an unusual, disappointing end for a consequent if you wrote one by habit.</p>
<p>A classroom skeleton in C major (invented, not a quoted score): antecedent ends on G (V); consequent begins like the antecedent and closes V–I on C. You may sing or play that skeleton on any instrument in the room, or speak the scale degrees, if a keyboard is not ready.</p>
<p>The word period here is not last week’s physics T, and not a historical era.</p>
