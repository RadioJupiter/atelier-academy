## Mathematics · Algebra XIII: composition of functions {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: a machine picture (first g, then f), then the writing (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), then a linear example</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-composition-of-functions">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="composition of functions" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear composition of functions">Hear</button> composition of functions</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The function (f ∘ g) that sends x to f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f to that result.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English composicioun, from Old French composicion, from Latin compositiō (English Wiktionary).</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Composition is not multiplication of f and g, and f ∘ g is not generally the same as g ∘ f. The little circle is not a decimal point.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Function notation f(x) from Algebra VIII, and linear functions from Algebra IX. The inverse of a linear function (Algebra XII) will return tomorrow’s neighbour, Day 43.</dd></div>

A function still assigns one output to each allowed input (Algebra VIII). Today we make a new function by using one function’s output as the next function’s input.

**The writing.** **(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x))** . The circle is read “composed with.” It is not a decimal point, and it is not multiplication. The inner function, g, is applied first.

**A linear example.** Let g(x) = x − 3 and f(x) = 2x + 1. Then (f ∘ g)(x) = f(x − 3) = 2(x − 3) + 1 = 2x − 6 + 1 = **2x − 5**. So (f ∘ g)(4) = 2(4) − 5 = 3, which matches f(g(4)) = f(1) = 3.

**Order matters.** (g ∘ f)(x) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1) − 3 = **2x − 2**. That is not the same rule as 2x − 5. So f ∘ g and g ∘ f are different functions here.

**Classroom numbers stay classroom numbers.** These linear rules are invented practice functions, labeled as such; they are not NASA measurements.

## Physics · A first look at circular motion, and a center-seeking force {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: a tangent direction, then why a change of direction needs a center-seeking force; no invented speeds</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-circular-motion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="circular motion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear circular motion">Hear</button> circular motion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Motion along a circular path. Even if the speed (the distance along the path in each unit of time) is constant, the direction is still changing, so the velocity is changing.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A point moving once around a fixed circle at a steady speed is still changing direction at every moment: the direction is along the tangent.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “constant speed means no change of any kind.” Speed can be constant while direction changes. And not an invented orbital speed for a planet or a spacecraft.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Level and week 8’s qualitative look at a curved path, now named as circular motion, still without an invented speed.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-centripetal">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="centripetal" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear centripetal">Hear</button> centripetal</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Center-seeking: a force (or an acceleration) directed toward the center of the circle, needed if the motion is to stay on that circle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>For a ball on a string, swung in a horizontal circle at a steady speed (an invented classroom demo), the string pulls toward the center. That pull is centripetal.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a second outward force that “balances” it in this first look, and not a license to compute a satellite’s speed. We do not invent a number of newtons for the string, and we do not yet treat a formula as today’s law.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Newton’s first law, already in this course: if no net force acted, the motion would not keep turning. A center-seeking force is what turns it.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A path can be curved even when the **speed** is steady, because **direction** can still change. Motion around a circle is the clean first case.

**Velocity is along the tangent.** At each point of the circle, the direction of motion is along the tangent, not along the radius. (You already used a tangent to a circle in geometry; here it is the direction of the motion.)

**A change of direction is a change of velocity.** So there is an acceleration even when the speed is constant. For motion that stays on the circle, that acceleration is toward the **center**. A force toward the center is required. The word for that center-seeking direction is **centripetal**.

**A classroom string.** A ball on a string, swung in a circle, is an invented demonstration: the string pulls inward. If the string is let go, the ball does not fly radially outward from the center along a radius; it goes off along the tangent it already had. We will not invent a speed, a length of string, or a number of newtons for this picture.

**No invented orbital speed.** A later, sourced lesson may compute with a named formula. Today is the direction, the name, and the first-law reason.

<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-affirming-the-consequent">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="affirming the consequent" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear affirming the consequent">Hear</button> affirming the consequent</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An invalid move: from “If P, then Q” and “Q,” to conclude “P.” The consequent Q is affirmed, but that is not enough to give the antecedent P.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle. This shape is a rectangle. Therefore it is a square. — Invalid: a non-square rectangle is still a rectangle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not modus ponens (from “If P, then Q” and “P,” to “Q”), which is valid. And not the undistributed middle (Day 36), which is a different syllogism error. This is a conditional error.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 21’s converse, inverse, and contrapositive, and Day 16’s necessary and sufficient conditions. Affirming the consequent treats a necessary result as if it were a sufficient test for the start.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-denying-the-antecedent">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="denying the antecedent" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear denying the antecedent">Hear</button> denying the antecedent</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An invalid move: from “If P, then Q” and “not P,” to conclude “not Q.” The antecedent is denied, but Q may still hold for another reason.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle. This shape is not a square. Therefore it is not a rectangle. — Invalid: it may still be a non-square rectangle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not modus tollens (from “If P, then Q” and “not Q,” to “not P”), which is valid. The two are easy to mix because both use a “not.”</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The same conditional, “If P, then Q.” Valid paths: assume P, get Q; or assume not Q, get not P. The two invalid paths are today’s named errors.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Conditionals are already in this course (converse, inverse, contrapositive; necessary and sufficient). Today names two **invalid** moves that look like reasoning.

**Affirming the consequent.** From “If P, then Q” and “Q,” it does **not** follow that P. Example: *If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle. This shape is a rectangle. Therefore it is a square.* A non-square rectangle shows the move is invalid.

**Denying the antecedent.** From “If P, then Q” and “not P,” it does **not** follow that not Q. Example: *If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle. This shape is not a square. Therefore it is not a rectangle.* Again a non-square rectangle is a counterexample.

**Valid neighbours, already available.** From “If P, then Q” and P, you may conclude Q (a valid path). From “If P, then Q” and not Q, you may conclude not P (modus tollens, the useful “not”). Today’s work is to refuse the two invalid look-alikes, not to invent a new formal calculus.

Classroom shapes stay invented examples, labeled as such.

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

From “If P, then Q,” you may not argue from Q to P (affirming the consequent), or from not-P to not-Q (denying the antecedent). A non-square rectangle is a counterexample. These are not modus ponens and not modus tollens.


<p>Key terms: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent.</p>

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Show that “If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is a rectangle. Therefore it is a square” is invalid.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The form is: If P then Q; Q; therefore P.</li>
<li>That affirms the consequent.</li>
<li>A non-square rectangle is Q without P, so the conclusion need not hold.</li>
