<p>Today is a short mix of this week’s similarity, not a new test. You may be asked to decide whether two triangles are similar, to name the scale factor, or to find a missing side or a height from a shadow. Keep corresponding parts together, and use AA when two pairs of angles are what you are given.</p>
<p>Congruence is still available when the sides are actually equal. If they are only proportional, the right word is similarity.</p>


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Similar triangles: corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides proportional. AA is enough to establish similarity. A scale factor from one corresponding pair finds a missing side. Congruence is same size as well as same shape.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>△ABC has angles 40° and 70°. △DEF has angles 70° and 70°. Are they similar? If not, say why; if they are, find the third angles and match the vertices.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>In △ABC the third angle is 70°. In △DEF the third angle is 40°. So both are 40-70-70 triangles.</li>
<li>Yes, they are similar. Match 40° with 40° and each 70° with a 70°: △ABC ∼ △FDE, for example, if ∠A = 40°, ∠B = 70°, ∠C = 70°, ∠D = 70°, ∠E = 70°, and ∠F = 40°.</li>
<li>The sides need not be equal; this is similarity, not automatically congruence.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>A 1.2 m stick casts a 1.8 m shadow. A wall in the same light casts a 6.0 m shadow. Find the height of the wall.</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: AA and a proportion</summary>

1.2/1.8 = h/6.0.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The height</summary>

h = 4.0 m.

</details>


</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>Use only this week’s methods. Show the proportion or the angle match.</p>

2. △PQR ∼ △XYZ, PQ = 10, XY = 4, QR = 15. Find YZ.
3. △ABC has ∠A = 55° and ∠B = 55°. △DEF has ∠D = 70° and ∠E = 55°. Are they similar?
4. A 4 m pole casts a 5 m shadow. A tree’s shadow is 15 m in the same light. Find the height of the tree.

</div>

### Exercises

1. Why is AA enough, without first measuring the sides?
2. Sides 3, 5, 7 and 6, 10, 16: similar or not? Explain.
3. The scale factor from △ABC to △DEF is 5/2, and AC = 6. Find DF.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Yes. 9/6 = 12/8 = 13.5/9 = 3/2. The scale factor from the first to the second is 3/2.
2. 10/4 = 15/YZ, so YZ = 6.
3. Yes. In △ABC, ∠C = 70°. In △DEF, ∠F = 55°. Both are 55-55-70. They are similar (isosceles in the same angle pattern).
4. 4/5 = h/15, so h = 12 m.

1. Two equal pairs of angles force the third pair, by the angle sum, so all corresponding angles are equal and the triangles are similar. The sides will then be proportional; you do not have to measure them first in order to know the triangles are similar.
2. Not similar. 6/3 = 2, 10/5 = 2, but 16/7 is not 2. The sides are not proportional.
3. DF corresponds to AC, so DF = 6 × 5/2 = 15.

<p>A Friday mix of this week’s rotation work, not a new condition. </div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

τ = F × d⊥, in N·m. Rotational equilibrium means Στ = 0 about the chosen point. A lever is that condition about a fulcrum; if the body is at rest, ΣF = 0 as well, and a force through the fulcrum gives no torque.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<ol class="steps">
<li>Each arm is 1.00 m, so the right-hand force is 18 N downward.</li>
<li>The support supplies 36 N upward.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>A light lever has a 50 N load, 0.40 m from the fulcrum. The effort is 1.00 m from the fulcrum, on the other side. Find the effort for Στ = 0.</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The lever rule</summary>

50 N × 0.40 m = E × 1.00 m.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The effort</summary>

E = 20 N.

</details>


</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>Take torques about the named point. Use N·m, not joules.</p>

1. A 12 N force acts with d⊥ = 0.25 m. Find the torque, and name the unit.
2. A 4.00 m light rod is supported at its center. 16 N downward at the left end: find the right-hand downward force for Στ = 0, and the support at rest.
3. Why does a force through the fulcrum not appear in the lever rule, even when ΣF = 0 needs that force?
4. Why is “equilibrium price” the wrong meaning of equilibrium in this lesson?

</div>

### Exercises

1. A 36 N load sits 0.25 m to the left of a fulcrum. Find the effort 0.75 m to the right for Στ = 0, and the support at rest.
2. If 10 N acts downward 0.50 m to the left of a support, and 10 N downward 0.50 m to the right, is Στ = 0? Is ΣF = 0 without a support force?
3. A student reports a torque of 4 J. What should be written instead, and why?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. τ = 12 N × 0.25 m = 3.0 N·m. The unit is the newton-meter, named as a torque.
2. The right-hand force is 16 N downward (equal arms). The support supplies 32 N upward.
3. Because that force’s perpendicular distance from the fulcrum is zero, so its torque about the fulcrum is zero. It still matters for ΣF = 0.
4. That is the already-taught market meaning: a price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Today’s word is rotational equilibrium of a rigid body, Στ = 0.

1. 36 N × 0.25 m = E × 0.75 m, so E = 12 N downward. Support: 36 N + 12 N = 48 N upward.
2. Yes, Στ = 0 about the support (equal opposite torques of 5.0 N·m). No, ΣF is not zero without a support: the two downward forces add to 20 N, so a 20 N upward support is needed for rest.
3. 4 N·m, named as a torque. A joule is the unit of work or energy, not of a turning effect, even though both involve a force and a distance.

## Elective · Anatomy: the leg — femur, tibia, and fibula {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: the three long bones of the leg, named and distinguished, without invented counts</p>

<p>Friday electives in odd-numbered weeks have been anatomy. Today is the <strong>leg</strong>: three long bones, named accurately, without invented muscle counts or a classroom diagnosis.</p>
<p>The <strong>femur</strong> is the thigh bone, the single long bone of the upper leg, from hip to knee. Below the knee the lower leg has two long bones. The <strong>tibia</strong> is the shinbone, the inner and usually the larger of the two, and the one you can feel along the front of the shin. The <strong>fibula</strong> is the thinner, outer bone, beside the tibia, down to the ankle. A common mix-up is to treat the lower leg as one bone, as the thigh is; it is two.</p>
<p>The word <em>fibula</em> also names an ancient clasp. Wiktionary records that the bone was so called because the pair resembled a clasp, with the fibula as the pin. That is a name, not a drawing you have to force onto every view of the leg.</p>
