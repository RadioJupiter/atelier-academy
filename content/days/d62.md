<p>The same neuter rule covers more of the body’s nouns. <em>Pectus, pectoris</em>; <em>ōs, ōris</em> (mouth or face, with a long <em>ō</em>); <em>crūs, crūris</em>; and <em>cor, cordis</em> all belong to the third family, and all are neuter. ## Mathematics · Geometry XXVI: AA similarity {#math}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: the angle sum, then two equal angles as a test for similarity</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-aa-similarity">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="AA similarity" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear AA similarity">Hear</button> AA similarity</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>If two angles of one triangle are equal to two angles of another triangle, the third angles are equal as well (because the angle sum is 180°), and the triangles are similar. The name AA records that two pairs of equal angles are enough.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If ∠P = ∠S = 50° and ∠Q = ∠T = 70°, then ∠R = ∠U = 60°, and △PQR ∼ △STU.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a claim that two equal sides, without equal angles, already give similarity. Not SAS or SSS congruence from Term 1, which decide same size as well as same shape. AA is about similarity, not congruence, unless you have more information.</dd></div>

<p>Once two pairs of angles match, the third pair is forced. Each triangle’s angles add to 180°, so the remaining angle is 180° minus the two you already know. If those two are the same in both triangles, the third is the same as well. All three corresponding angles are equal, and the triangles are similar.</p>
<p>That test is called <strong>AA similarity</strong> (angle-angle). Two equal angles are enough; you do not first have to show the third pair, and you do not first have to measure the sides. The sides will then be proportional, by the definition of similarity.</p>
<p>AA does not, by itself, make the triangles congruent. They may still differ in size.</p>

## Science · Water: melting and boiling at one atmosphere {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: the familiar 0 °C and 100 °C, set beside sourced modern figures</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-melting-point-1-atm">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="melting point (at 1 atm)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear melting point (at 1 atm)">Hear</button> melting point (at 1 atm)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The temperature at which a solid and its liquid are in equilibrium at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. <div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Ice and liquid water together, at one atmosphere, sit at about 0 °C in ordinary laboratory language. This course uses that familiar figure as an approximation, beside the sourced modern scale described in the lesson.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the triple point, which is a different condition (solid, liquid, and vapor together at a unique pressure and temperature). Not a claim that every substance melts at 0 °C. Evaporation, already taught, is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and is not the same as melting.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Evaporation was taught in the oil-paint module. Today begins the water module with the familiar ice point and boiling point, and with the sourced modern figures for those points.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The temperature at which a liquid boils at a stated pressure, here one atmosphere. <div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The IAPWS FAQ is the source for both the historical definition and the ITS-90 atmospheric boiling temperature. Familiar “about 100 °C” and the sourced 99.974 °C are kept side by side.</dd></div>

<p>Water is found as a solid (ice), a liquid, and a gas (water vapor). Melting is the change from solid to liquid; boiling is the change from liquid to gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point. Evaporation, already taught, is a liquid becoming a gas at its surface, and it can happen below the boiling point. That earlier idea is not retaught as new; it is only kept distinct from boiling.</p>
<p>Ordinary laboratory language still says that, at one atmosphere, ice melts at about 0 °C and water boils at about 100 °C. Those figures have a history. According to the IAPWS FAQ, the Celsius scale was originally defined so that the freezing point and the boiling point of pure water, both at one atmosphere, were 0 and 100 degrees. That definition ceased to be valid when the International Temperature Scale of 1990 (ITS-90) was adopted.</p>
<p>On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 99.974 °C (373.124 K). The familiar “about 100 °C” is still a good ordinary approximation. This course does not blend those two figures into a fake official exact 100. The International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS), Frequently Asked Questions, FAQ 1 (https://iapws.org/faqs/faq1.html). The Celsius scale was originally defined so that the freezing point and boiling point of pure water, both at one atmosphere, were 0 and 100 degrees. That definition ceased to be valid with the International Temperature Scale of 1990 (ITS-90). On ITS-90, the atmospheric boiling temperature of water is approximately 373.124 K (99.974 °C). The triple point of water — the unique pressure and temperature where solid, liquid, and vapor coexist — is exactly 273.16 K on that scale, which is 0.01 °C.</p>

<article class="term" id="term-triad-inversion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="triad inversion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear triad inversion">Hear</button> triad inversion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A change of which triad tone is in the bass: root position has the root in the bass; first inversion has the third in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The three tones of the triad are the same; only the lowest note changes.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A C-major triad is C–E–G in root position, E–G–C in first inversion, and G–C–E in second inversion. The bass is C, then E, then G.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not interval inversion, already taught on Day 37, which turns a single interval by moving one of its two notes by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth, and so on). Triad inversion is about which chord tone is in the bass, not about inverting one interval.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Days 17 and 42 taught the triad and the major and minor triad. Day 37 taught interval inversion, with the octave as the frame. Today’s inversion is of a whole triad, and those two uses of the word must be kept apart.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>You already know a <strong>triad</strong>: three tones, a root, a third, and a fifth (a major or a minor triad, as Day 42 taught). You also already know <strong>interval inversion</strong>: one interval, turned so that the old top note is below, or the old bottom note is above, inside an octave. Today’s word is different.</p>
<p><strong>Triad inversion</strong> asks which of the three triad tones is in the bass — the lowest note. In <strong>root position</strong>, the root is in the bass (C–E–G for a C-major triad). In <strong>first inversion</strong>, the third is in the bass (E–G–C). In <strong>second inversion</strong>, the fifth is in the bass (G–C–E). The three tones remain the same; they are only stacked in a new order above a new bass.</p>
<p>Do not name this as interval inversion. A fifth inverting to a fourth is a two-note job. A triad inversion is a three-note job, named from the bass.</p>

### Exercises

1. Write the first and second inversions of a D-major triad (D, F♯, A).
2. Which inversion of C major has G in the bass?
3. Why is “the triad has been inverted, so it is now a fourth” the wrong sentence for today’s job?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. First inversion has the third of the triad in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The other two tones are stacked above.
2. Root position, F; first inversion, A; second inversion, C.
3. Interval inversion turns a single two-note interval by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth). Triad inversion keeps all three tones of the triad and changes which of them is in the bass.
4. The three tones are still C, E, and G: a C-major triad, now in first inversion (E in the bass). It has not become a different triad.
5. Model: **triad inversion.** Own words should match this idea: A change of which triad tone is in the bass: root position has the root in the bass; first inversion has the third in the bass; second inversion has the fifth in the bass. The three tones of the triad are the same; only the lowest note changes. Near-miss: Not interval inversion, already taught on Day 37, which turns a single interval by moving one of its two notes by an octave (a fifth becomes a fourth, and so on). Triad inversion is about which chord tone is in the bass, not about inverting one interval. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

1. First inversion: F♯–A–D (F♯ in the bass). Second inversion: A–D–F♯ (A in the bass).
2. Second inversion.
3. “A fourth” is interval-inversion language for two notes. Today’s job is to name which triad tone is in the bass, not to rename the whole chord as a single interval.
