## Latin · Capitulum XIII: the named day, and prīdiē {#latin}

<p class="meta">25 minutes · Method: ablative on the named day itself; prīdiē with the accusative</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-ablative-named-day">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="the ablative of the named day" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear the ablative of the named day">Hear</button> the ablative of the named day</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The Kalends, Nones, or Ides themselves are expressed in the ablative, with the month adjective agreeing: Kalendīs Mārtiīs, Nonīs Aprīlibus, Īdibus Octōbribus.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Īdibus Mārtiīs means “on the Ides of March,” that is, on 15 March.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the accusative, which is used after prīdiē and in ante-diem phrases, and not a singular ablative *Kalendā. These words stay plural.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Allen and Greenough §631 and the usual dating construction: the named day itself is ablative. Prīdiē is taught in the same sitting.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-pridie">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="PREE-di-ay" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear PREE-di-ay">Hear</button> prīdiē</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>“On the day before,” used with the accusative of Kalendae, Nonae, or Īdūs (and the agreeing month): prīdiē Īdūs Mārtiās is 14 March.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>prīdiē Kalendās Maiās is 30 April, the day before 1 May.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the named day itself (that is ablative), and not “two days before,” which is a later inclusive count. Prīdiē is only the day immediately before.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Tomorrow treats days counted inclusively backward (ante diem). Today stops at the named day and the day before it.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


When you mean the named day itself, Latin uses the **ablative**, and the month agrees as an adjective:

- **Kalendīs Mārtiīs** — on the Kalends of March (1 March)
- **Nonīs Mārtiīs** — on the Nones of March (7 March)
- **Īdibus Mārtiīs** — on the Ides of March (15 March)

The day immediately before a named day is **prīdiē**, used with the **accusative**:

- **prīdiē Īdūs Mārtiās** — the day before the Ides of March, which is 14 March
- **prīdiē Nonās Aprīlēs** — the day before the Nones of April, which is 4 April (the Nonae of April are the 5th)
- **prīdiē Kalendās Maiās** — the day before the Kalends of May, which is 30 April

Prīdiē is only that one day immediately before. “Two days before,” counted the Roman way, is tomorrow’s job, not today’s.

## Mathematics · Geometry XXXII: the area of a right triangle {#math}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: A = ½ab when a and b are the legs</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-area-of-a-right-triangle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="area of a right triangle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear area of a right triangle">Hear</button> area of a right triangle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In a right triangle the two legs are perpendicular, so either leg may be taken as the base and the other as the height. The area is therefore ½ab, where a and b are the legs.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 5-12-13 triangle has area ½ × 5 × 12 = 30.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not ½ times a leg times the hypotenuse, and not the product of the two legs without the one-half.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is yesterday’s method, stated cleanly for the right-triangle case. It does not replace ½bh for other triangles.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


In a right triangle the two legs meet at a right angle. So if one leg is taken as the base, the other leg is already the perpendicular height. The area is

**A = ½ab**,

where **a** and **b** are the legs. The hypotenuse is not used as a factor in this product.

A 3-4-5 triangle has area ½ × 3 × 4 = 6. A 6-8-10 triangle, similar to it and already used yesterday, has area ½ × 6 × 8 = 24, which is 4 times 6, as the scale factor 2 would lead you to expect for area (2² = 4). That check uses similarity from week 13 and the theorem from week 14; it is not a new theorem today.

The formula ½bh is still the general rule. ½ab is that rule in the special case of a right triangle, with the legs as b and h.

## Writing & Rhetoric · The false dilemma {#writing}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: do not treat two options as the only ones when a third remains</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-false-dilemma">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="false dilemma" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear false dilemma">Hear</button> false dilemma</h5>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A fallacy in which two options are presented as if they were the only ones, when at least one other genuine option has been left out.</dd></div>

A **false dilemma** (sometimes called a false either–or) is a fallacy in which two options are presented as if they were the **only** ones, when at least one other genuine option has been left out.

An invented example, labeled invented:

> “Either this triangle is a 3-4-5 triangle, or it is not a right triangle.”

Those are not the only possibilities. A 5-12-13 triangle is right-angled and is not 3-4-5. Last week already treated 3-4-5 as one integer triple, not the only right triangle. The invented sentence hides that third case.

A genuine either–or is not a fallacy. “A triangle either is right-angled or it is not” really does exhaust the cases (for that one property). The flaw is the hidden extra option, not the word “or.”

## Physics · Finding a center of mass by two suspensions {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: two plumb lines from two hanging-points; the crossing is the center of mass</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-center-of-mass-by-suspension">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="locating a center of mass by suspension" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear locating a center of mass by suspension">Hear</button> locating a center of mass by suspension</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A method for finding the center of mass of a flat object: hang it from one point, mark the vertical plumb line through that point; hang it from a second point, mark that second plumb line; the center of mass lies where the two lines meet.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>An invented card of uneven shape, hung from a pin at one corner and then from another, has two plumb lines drawn on it; their crossing is treated as the center of mass of the card.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a single hanging, which only gives one line that the center of mass lies on, and not a claim that the crossing is an official laboratory measurement of a named object.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week the center of mass was a balance point, with mass × distance on a light bar. Today is a method for locating that point on a flat card. The idea of a vertical line is the same family as last week’s line from the center of mass.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Last week the center of mass of a light bar was the balance point at which the mass × distance products agreed. Today is a method for **locating** that point on a **flat** object, such as a card.

Hang the object from a pin, so that it can swing freely, and let it come to rest. A **plumb line** (a vertical line from the pin, in an invented classroom picture a thread with a small weight) marks one line that the center of mass lies on. The card in rest hangs so that its center of mass is vertically below the pin.

One line is not enough to name the point. Hang the same object from a **second** point, and mark a second plumb line. The center of mass lies on that line too, so it lies at the **crossing**.
