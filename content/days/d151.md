<p>Sequor already has a present indicative sequor, a future sequar, sequēris, sequētur, and the perfect tenses already taught. Today the present subjunctive: sequar, sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur. The first-person singular is spelled sequar, the same as the future; the other persons split. The future has ē: sequēris, sequētur, sequēmur, sequēminī, sequentur. The present subjunctive has ā: sequāris, sequātur, sequāmur, sequāminī, sequantur. Context, and the vowel of the ending, tell them apart. The sentences are original classroom Latin, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the English gloss, not with an unquoted English “boy” or “girl.” A purpose clause with ut, and the present subjunctive of ēgredior, wait until they are taught; ēgredior’s forms wait until tomorrow.</p>

## Writing · A commonplace, a first look, after last week’s confirmation {#writing}

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A commonplace, in this course, is an amplification of a general, already-acknowledged fault of a type, not a trial of a living person, and not a new claim that still has to be confirmed or refuted. It takes a fault already shown to fail and enlarges the reasons why it fails.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>The English name commonplace is a calque of Latin locus commūnis, itself a calque of Ancient Greek κοινὸς τόπος (koinòs tópos), “common place,” as given on Wiktionary.</dd></div>

It does not try a new claim, and it does not try a living person. It takes a general fault of a type that has already been acknowledged in this course, and it amplifies the reasons why that fault fails. The invented type used this week is the fault of treating a fable as if it were a record of a historical event. That has already been taught: “The Crow and the Pitcher” can be told often, and it is still not a historical record. A commonplace states the type of fault, enlarges the already-taught reasons, and may contrast the fault with a claim that does hold, such as last week’s invented confirmation that if two sides of a triangle are equal, the angles opposite those sides are equal. No living person is named. The English name commonplace is a calque of Latin locus commūnis, itself a calque of Ancient Greek κοινὸς τόπος, as given on Wiktionary.</p>
