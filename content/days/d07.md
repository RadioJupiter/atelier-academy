<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-inequality">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="inequality" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear inequality">Hear</button> inequality</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A statement that one quantity is greater or less than another. Adding the same number, or multiplying by a positive number, preserves the inequality; multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the sign.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From inequality as in- + equality, from Latin aequālitās, from aequālis (‘equal’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>2x + 3 > 11 becomes x > 4. From −3x ≥ 12, dividing by −3 gives x ≤ −4.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>You must not keep the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative. A quadratic inequality is also not solved by ‘just doing the same to both sides’ without using the roots and the shape of the graph.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A linear inequality is like an equation with one extra rule. Quadratic inequalities in this lesson use the graph between or outside the roots.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-am-gm">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="A M G M inequality" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear A M G M inequality">Hear</button> AM–GM inequality</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>For a, b ≥ 0, (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b. It is a tool for maxima and minima.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Among rectangles of area 36, a + b ≥ 12, so the smallest perimeter is 24, when the rectangle is a 6 × 6 square.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>AM–GM does not apply to a negative number, and equality is not ‘almost when a is near b’ — it holds only when a = b.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The proof is (√a − √b)² ≥ 0, an inequality that comes from a square never being negative.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

An **inequality** such as 2*x* + 3 > 11 is solved much like an equation, with one extra rule: if you multiply or divide both sides by a **negative** number, you must reverse the inequality sign. You can also reason on a number line, or with bars when the problem is a comparison of quantities.

**Rules.** You may add the same number to both sides of an inequality, or multiply both sides by a *positive* number. Multiplying by a negative number reverses the sign.

**Quadratic inequalities** are solved from the roots and the shape of the graph: x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) < 0 exactly when x lies *between* the roots.

**The arithmetic mean–geometric mean inequality (two numbers).** For a, b ≥ 0: (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality only when a = b.
*Proof.* (√a − √b)² ≥ 0, since a square is never negative. Expand: a − 2√(ab) + b ≥ 0. ∎

**Use it to optimise.** Among rectangles of perimeter 40, which has the largest area? Sides a and b with a + b = 20: √(ab) ≤ 10, so ab ≤ 100, with equality when a = b = 10. The square wins.

## Science · Phenomenon: why is the sky blue and the sunset red? {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Phenomenon-based investigation</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-rayleigh-scattering">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Rayleigh scattering" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Rayleigh scattering">Hear</button> Rayleigh scattering <span class="symbol">intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength. Short wavelengths are scattered much more strongly than long ones (the 1/λ⁴ law). Named after John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Blue light at 450 nm is scattered about 5.9 times as strongly as red light at 700 nm. That is why a clear sky looks blue.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not the reason clouds are white: cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength and scatter all colors about equally.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The same law, applied to a long path through air at sunset, takes blue out of the direct beam and leaves orange and red.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-wavelength">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="wavelength" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear wavelength">Hear</button> wavelength <span class="symbol">λ</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The distance from one crest of a wave to the next. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to about 700 nm (red).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>A transparent English compound of wave + length; no classical root is claimed here.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Violet light has a shorter wavelength than red, so in Rayleigh scattering it is scattered more strongly.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Wavelength is not the same as the speed of light, and it is not ‘how strong the light is’.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The 1/λ⁴ law in Rayleigh scattering is a statement about wavelength. Day 9 will also use λ in c = fλ.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The phenomenon.** On a clear day the sky is blue overhead; near sunset the Sun turns orange-red. Clouds are white. On the Moon the daytime sky is black.

**Model.** Sunlight contains all visible wavelengths (about 400 nm violet to 700 nm red). Air molecules are much smaller than these wavelengths, and they scatter light with an intensity proportional to 1/λ⁴ (**Rayleigh scattering**): short wavelengths are scattered far more than long ones. Light scattered toward you from all over the sky is therefore rich in short wavelengths. At sunset, sunlight crosses far more atmosphere to reach you, so most of the blue is scattered *out* of the direct beam, leaving orange and red. Cloud droplets are much *larger* than the wavelength and scatter all colors about equally, so clouds look white.

**Experiment (15 min).** Fill a clear glass or jar with water and stir in a few drops of milk. In a dark room, shine a flashlight through the side. Look at the glass from the side, then look through it toward the flashlight. Add milk drop by drop and repeat.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Sunlight contains all visible wavelengths (about 400–700 nm). Air molecules are much smaller than those wavelengths and Rayleigh-scatter with intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴, so short wavelengths are scattered much more. The sky looks blue; at sunset the direct beam is drained of blue and looks orange-red. Cloud droplets are larger and scatter all colors about equally (clouds look white). The Moon's daytime sky is black because it has almost no atmosphere. Never look at the Sun itself.

<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>G major has one sharp, F♯. A major has three: F♯, C♯, G♯. A signature of three flats is E♭ major (the second-to-last flat names the major key).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A key signature is not a time signature, and the sharps are not added in random order.</dd></div>

**The major scale** follows the step pattern **W W H W W W H** (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half). From C on the white keys it needs no sharps or flats: C D E F G A B C. Starting on any other note, sharps (♯, raise a half step) or flats (♭, lower a half step) are needed to keep the pattern.

**Key signatures.** Sharps are added in the order **F C G D A E B**; flats in the order **B E A D G C F**. G major has one sharp (F♯); D major two (F♯, C♯). F major has one flat (B♭); B♭ major two (B♭, E♭). Each letter name appears exactly once in a major scale.

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Writing G major, as a model, then reading a signature.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Start on G and keep W W H W W W H: G A B C D E F♯ G. The half steps are B–C and F♯–G. One sharp is needed, and it is F♯ (the first sharp in the order F C G D A E B).</li>
<li>A signature of three flats is B♭, E♭, A♭. The second-to-last is E♭, so the major key is E♭ major.</li>
<li>If you flatten the wrong letter, a letter will be repeated or missed, and the pattern will break.</li>
