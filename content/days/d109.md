### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The final amount paid is $81. $80 would be a single 20 percent discount off the original, not two successive 10 percent discounts.
2. The second 10 percent is a part of the remainder, $90, not of the original $100.
3. 25 percent of 80 is 20, leaving 60. 10 percent of 60 is 6, leaving 54. 35 percent of 80 would be a different single discount, not these two successive discounts.
4. So that the method can be practiced without claiming a real store sale. Last week’s job was a single percent off the original; today a second percent is taken off the remainder.
5. Model: **successive discounts.** Own words should match this idea: A successive discount, in this first look, takes one percent off an invented price, then takes a second percent off the amount that remains, not off the original price again. If 10 percent is taken off an invented $100, the first amount paid is $90. A second 10 percent off that $90 is $9, and the final amount paid is $81, not $80. Two discounts of 10 percent are not one discount of 20 percent off the original. Last week’s single discount, 25 percent off an invented $80, remains a different job, already taught. The figures are invented, not a real store sale. Tax and compound interest remain reserved. Near-miss: Not a first look at last week’s single percent-off discount, or at percent itself, already taught. Not a real store’s price, and not a first look at tax or at compound interest. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.

1. 10 percent of 50 is 5, leaving 45. 10 percent of 45 is 4.50, leaving $40.50.
2. 25 percent of 120 is 30, leaving 90. 10 percent of 90 is 9, leaving $81.
3. The second 10 percent was taken of the original 100, instead of of the remainder 90. That is a single 20 percent discount, not two successive 10 percent discounts.
4. Those jobs have not been taught, and a real advertised sale is not a classroom invented figure.
