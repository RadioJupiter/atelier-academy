<article class="term" id="term-completing-the-square">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="completing the square" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear completing the square">Hear</button> completing the square</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Rewriting x² + px as a square plus or minus a constant, so a quadratic can be solved or a parabola’s vertex read off.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>x² − 8x + 12 = 0 becomes (x − 4)² = 4, so x = 6 or x = 2.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-discriminant">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="discriminant" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear discriminant">Hear</button> discriminant <span class="symbol">D = b² − 4ac</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The quantity that decides how many real roots a quadratic has: two if D > 0, one if D = 0, none if D < 0.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin discrīmināns, from discriminare (‘to distinguish’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>For x² − 4x + 5 = 0, D = 16 − 20 = −4, so there are no real roots.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The discriminant is not the same as the vertex, and a negative D does not mean ‘the equation is illegal’; it means the roots are not real.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Completing the square produces D under the square root, which is why its sign matters.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-vietas-formulas">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Vieta&#x27;s formulas" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Vieta&#x27;s formulas">Hear</button> Vieta's formulas <span class="symbol">r + s = −p, rs = q</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>For the roots r and s of x² + px + q = 0, the sum of the roots is −p and the product is q. (Named after François Viète.)</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If the roots of x² − 8x + 12 = 0 are 6 and 2, then 6 + 2 = 8 and 6 · 2 = 12.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Vieta does not find the roots by itself if you do not already have them or an equivalent fact; it checks them and can build an equation from known roots.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>After completing the square, use Vieta as a check, not as a substitute for the method.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A **quadratic** equation can be written *ax*² + *bx* + *c* = 0 with *a* ≠ 0. Some quadratics factor: *x*² − 5*x* + 6 = (*x*−2)(*x*−3) = 0, so *x* = 2 or *x* = 3. Others need completing the square, or the quadratic formula, which comes from completing the square on the general equation. Always check roots in the original equation.

**Completing the square** is the method; the formula is its result. For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0:

x² + (b/a)x = −c/a ⇒ (x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/4a² ⇒ x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a.

The **discriminant** D = b² − 4ac decides the number of real roots: two if D > 0, one (double) if D = 0, none if D < 0.

**Vieta's formulas.** If r and s are the roots of x² + px + q = 0, then x² + px + q = (x − r)(x − s), so **r + s = −p** and **rs = q**. Use them to check answers and to work with roots without finding them.

**Worked example.** Solve x² − 6x + 5 = 0 by completing the square: (x − 3)² = 4, so x − 3 = ±2, x = 5 or 1. Check with Vieta: 5 + 1 = 6 = −(−6) ✓; 5 · 1 = 5 ✓.

Grammar, in the trivium, is the craft of accurate sentences: parts of speech, agreement, and punctuation that makes the structure visible. Today's work names what a sentence is doing, so later logic and rhetoric have something solid to stand on. Mark the subject and the verb before you polish anything else.

**The three arts.** In the medieval liberal arts, the *trivium* was grammar (the structure of language), logic or dialectic (valid reasoning) and rhetoric (effective persuasion). This course teaches them in that order and then keeps all three running together.

**Sentence anatomy.**
- **Subject** (who or what) and **predicate** (what is said about it).
- **Direct object** (receives the action: *Franklin bought **a volume***); **indirect object** (*gave **me** the book*); **subject complement** after a linking verb (*the writing was **excellent***).
- **Phrase**: a group of words without its own subject–verb pair (*in the morning*). **Clause**: has a subject and a verb. **Independent** clauses can stand alone; **dependent** clauses cannot.
- **Simple** sentence: one independent clause. **Compound**: two or more independent clauses. **Complex**: an independent clause plus at least one dependent clause.

</div>

## Physics · Free fall {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Peer Instruction protocol</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-free-fall">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="free fall" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear free fall">Hear</button> free fall <span class="symbol">g ≈ 9.8 m/s², downward</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Motion under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. Near Earth, the acceleration has size g and is directed downward, the same for all objects.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A stone dropped from rest falls with a ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward. After 2 s its speed is about 19.6 m/s, if air is ignored.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Free fall is not ‘only motion going down’: a ball thrown upward is still in free fall after it leaves the hand, with the same downward acceleration.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is a constant acceleration (Day 3), with a special value and direction given by gravity.</dd></div>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="gravity" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear gravity">Hear</button> gravity <span class="symbol">g</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The attraction that gives a free-falling object its downward acceleration g near Earth. In these lessons g is taken as about 9.8 m/s², downward.</dd></div>

Near Earth's surface, if air resistance is small enough to ignore, all bodies fall with the same downward acceleration, about **g = 9.8 m/s²** (often taken as 10 m/s² in a quick estimate). The motion is uniformly accelerated: *v* = *u* + *at*, and *s* = *ut* + ½*at*², with *a* = *g* downward. Mass does not appear in these equations, which is the point of the famous (if simplified) falling-bodies claim.

**Pre-reading.** Without air resistance, all objects near Earth's surface fall with the same acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward, regardless of mass. Galileo argued this from experiments with inclined planes and thought experiments; on the Moon in 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together, and they landed at the same moment. Falling from rest: d = ½gt², v = gt.

**Modeling** means turning the flat contour into a form that looks solid, by shading in values you have already trained on the scale. Start with the big light and the big shadow, then the terminator (the edge where light turns to shadow), then only a few darker accents. Do not polish a cheek before the large light is in the right place.

**Modeling the form.** With the shadow shapes in place as a flat tone, model in the light side: the **halftones**, which darken gradually as the surface turns away from the light, reaching their darkest just at the terminator. Keep the shadow side simple and unified: darks in the shadow are fewer and quieter than they look. Reserve the lightest lights (the paper) for the planes facing the light most directly.

**Order of work:** (1) darken the core of the shadow near the terminator; (2) lay the darkest halftones; (3) work outward toward the light; (4) soften edges where the form turns gradually, and keep them sharp where it turns abruptly.

**Why artists study bones.** Where bone lies just under the skin, it fixes the form: these **subcutaneous landmarks** do not change shape with fat or muscle, so they are the reliable measuring points of figure drawing.

**Key landmarks.** The *clavicles* (collarbones) and the *jugular notch* between them; the *acromion* at the point of the shoulder; the spinous process of the *seventh cervical vertebra* (the prominent bump at the base of the neck); the *sternum*; the *olecranon* (point of the elbow); the *iliac crest* and the *anterior superior iliac spine* at the front of the hip; the *patella*; the *malleoli* (ankle bones, the inner one higher than the outer).

**Proportion of the head.** In an adult head seen from the front, the eyes lie at about half the height of the head (chin to top of the skull), lower than beginners usually place them.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The remainder is what is left after a part has been taken, and the next fraction is *of that leftover*, not of the original whole. A simple two-part whole is just two parts that add to the total, with no second cut of what was left.
2. Velocity is displacement over time and has a direction; speed is distance over time and does not. A lap that returns to the start has speed &gt; 0 and average velocity 0.
3. An axiom is a starting statement accepted without proof; a theorem is a statement proved from axioms, definitions, and earlier theorems. 'It looks right in the figure' is neither.
4. An epithet is a fixed, reusable descriptive phrase ('swift-footed Achilles'). A one-off metaphor is invented for a single moment and is not a standing name.
5. *Ser* for identity, origin, characteristics, and time: *La ciudad es antigua.* *Estar* for place or a temporary condition: *El libro está en la mesa* / *Estamos cansados.*
6. Sight-size sets the drawing so that, from a fixed viewing point, the drawing and the subject appear the same size, and lengths can be compared at arm's length. If the viewing point moves, yesterday's proportions will not match.
