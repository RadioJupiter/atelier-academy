## Mathematics · Algebra II: quadratic equations {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Gelfand & Shen, <em>Algebra</em></p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A **quadratic** equation can be written *ax*² + *bx* + *c* = 0 with *a* ≠ 0. Some quadratics factor: *x*² − 5*x* + 6 = (*x*−2)(*x*−3) = 0, so *x* = 2 or *x* = 3. Others need completing the square, or the quadratic formula, which comes from completing the square on the general equation. Always check roots in the original equation.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Completing the square** is the method; the formula is its result. For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0:

x² + (b/a)x = −c/a ⇒ (x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/4a² ⇒ x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a.

The **discriminant** D = b² − 4ac decides the number of real roots: two if D > 0, one (double) if D = 0, none if D < 0.

**Vieta's formulas.** If r and s are the roots of x² + px + q = 0, then x² + px + q = (x − r)(x − s), so **r + s = −p** and **rs = q**. Use them to check answers and to work with roots without finding them.

**Worked example.** Solve x² − 6x + 5 = 0 by completing the square: (x − 3)² = 4, so x − 3 = ±2, x = 5 or 1. Check with Vieta: 5 + 1 = 6 = −(−6) ✓; 5 · 1 = 5 ✓.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Bring to *ax*² + *bx* + *c* = 0. Factor if you can. Otherwise complete the square or use *x* = (−*b* ± √(*b*²−4*ac*)) / (2*a*). Check by substitution.

</div>

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. *x* = 2 or *x* = 3.
2. A product (or a square) can be zero in more than one way; the graph can cross the axis twice.

1. (x + 3)² = 16 ⇒ x + 3 = ±4 ⇒ **x = 1 or x = −7**.
2. x = (3 ± √(9 + 16))/4 = (3 ± 5)/4 ⇒ **x = 2 or x = −1/2**. Vieta on x² − 1.5x − 1 = 0: sum 1.5 ✓, product −1 ✓.
3. D = k² − 36 = 0 ⇒ **k = ±6**.
4. r + s = 5, rs = 3. r² + s² = 25 − 6 = **19**; 1/r + 1/s = (r + s)/rs = **5/3**.
5. Sum 4, product (2 + √3)(2 − √3) = 4 − 3 = 1 ⇒ **x² − 4x + 1 = 0**.
6. ac < 0 ⇒ −4ac > 0 ⇒ D = b² − 4ac > 0. ∎
7. Let y = x²: y² − 5y + 4 = 0 ⇒ y = 1 or 4 ⇒ **x = ±1, ±2**.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium I, grammar: the anatomy of a sentence {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: the Trivium (grammar → logic → rhetoric). Grammar here means knowing precisely how sentences are built.</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Grammar, in the trivium, is the craft of accurate sentences: parts of speech, agreement, and punctuation that makes the structure visible. Today's work names what a sentence is doing, so later logic and rhetoric have something solid to stand on. Mark the subject and the verb before you polish anything else.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The three arts.** In the medieval liberal arts, the *trivium* was grammar (the structure of language), logic or dialectic (valid reasoning) and rhetoric (effective persuasion). This course teaches them in that order and then keeps all three running together.

**Sentence anatomy.**
- **Subject** (who or what) and **predicate** (what is said about it).
- **Direct object** (receives the action: *Franklin bought **a volume***); **indirect object** (*gave **me** the book*); **subject complement** after a linking verb (*the writing was **excellent***).
- **Phrase**: a group of words without its own subject–verb pair (*in the morning*). **Clause**: has a subject and a verb. **Independent** clauses can stand alone; **dependent** clauses cannot.
- **Simple** sentence: one independent clause. **Compound**: two or more independent clauses. **Complex**: an independent clause plus at least one dependent clause.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Find the subject and the finite verb first. Check agreement. Fix punctuation so that clauses are visible. Do not start with 'style'.

</div>

<h3>Watch me</h3>

## Physics · Free fall {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Peer Instruction protocol</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Near Earth's surface, if air resistance is small enough to ignore, all bodies fall with the same downward acceleration, about **g = 9.8 m/s²** (often taken as 10 m/s² in a quick estimate). The motion is uniformly accelerated: *v* = *u* + *at*, and *s* = *ut* + ½*at*², with *a* = *g* downward. Mass does not appear in these equations, which is the point of the famous (if simplified) falling-bodies claim.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Pre-reading.** Without air resistance, all objects near Earth's surface fall with the same acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward, regardless of mass. Galileo argued this from experiments with inclined planes and thought experiments; on the Moon in 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather together, and they landed at the same moment. Falling from rest: d = ½gt², v = gt.

### ConcepTests

1. A heavy ball and a light ball of the same size are dropped together from the same height; air resistance is negligible. (A) The heavy one lands first (B) The light one lands first (C) They land together.
2. One ball is dropped and another is thrown horizontally from the same height at the same moment. (A) The dropped ball lands first (B) The thrown ball lands first (C) They land together.
3. A ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s (no air resistance). When it returns to the thrower's hand its speed is: (A) less than 12 m/s (B) 12 m/s (C) more than 12 m/s.

### Problems

4. A stone is dropped from a 20 m bridge. How long does it fall, and how fast is it moving when it hits the water?
5. A ball is thrown up at 15 m/s. Find its maximum height and the time to reach it.
6. **Experiment: reaction time.** Have someone hold a ruler vertically with its zero end between your open fingers, then drop it without warning. Catch it, and read the distance d it fell. Do it five times. Your reaction time is t = √(2d/g). If d = 15 cm, what is t?

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

*g* ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward (≈ 10 m/s² for estimates). *v* = *u* + *gt* (downward taken positive if you choose that). Mass cancels: heavy and light bodies fall together in this model.

## History · Greece: Herodotus and the idea of "inquiry" {#history}

<p class="meta">45 min · Primary source: Herodotus, <em>Histories</em>, opening sentence (G. C. Macaulay translation, 1890, public domain)</p>

**Context.** Herodotus of Halicarnassus (born in the 480s BCE, died after about 430 BCE) wrote an account of the wars between the Greeks and the Persian Empire (490 BCE and 480–479 BCE) and of the peoples involved. The Greek word *historiē* means *inquiry*. Cicero later called him "the father of history". He travelled, interviewed people and reported what he was told, often saying when he did not believe it.

**The source.**

> This is the Showing forth of the Inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassos, to the end that neither the deeds of men may be forgotten by lapse of time, nor the works great and marvellous, which have been produced some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians, may lose their renown; and especially that the causes may be remembered for which these waged war with one another.

### Exercises

1. Herodotus states three purposes. List them in your own words.
2. "Barbarians" here translates *barbaroi*, the Greek word for all non-Greek speakers. Does Herodotus's sentence treat their works as inferior? Quote the evidence.
3. Compare this purpose with that of Hammurabi's stele (Day 3). How is a history different from a royal inscription?
4. Why is the last purpose (the causes of the war) the beginning of history as an analytical discipline rather than a chronicle?

### Answer key

1. So that human deeds are not forgotten; so that great achievements of both Greeks and non-Greeks keep their fame; and, above all, so that the *causes* of the war are remembered.
2. No: "works great and marvellous … some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians" gives both equal credit. (*Barbaroi* was not yet as pejorative as "barbarian" is in English.)
3. The stele glorifies one ruler and commands; Herodotus writes as a private inquirer, explains causes, and gives credit to both sides. The stele is a primary source for Hammurabi's self-image; Herodotus is a secondary source for earlier events and a primary source for 5th-century Greek thought.
4. A chronicle lists what happened; history asks *why*. Seeking causes means weighing evidence and explaining, which is what historians still do.

## Atelier · Bargue plate: modeling and completion {#atelier}

<p class="meta">90 min</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Modeling** means turning the flat contour into a form that looks solid, by shading in values you have already trained on the scale. Start with the big light and the big shadow, then the terminator (the edge where light turns to shadow), then only a few darker accents. Do not polish a cheek before the large light is in the right place.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Modeling the form.** With the shadow shapes in place as a flat tone, model in the light side: the **halftones**, which darken gradually as the surface turns away from the light, reaching their darkest just at the terminator. Keep the shadow side simple and unified: darks in the shadow are fewer and quieter than they look. Reserve the lightest lights (the paper) for the planes facing the light most directly.

**Order of work:** (1) darken the core of the shadow near the terminator; (2) lay the darkest halftones; (3) work outward toward the light; (4) soften edges where the form turns gradually, and keep them sharp where it turns abruptly.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Big light, big shadow, then the terminator. Match values to the scale by squinting. Leave the smallest darks until last.

</div>

## Elective · Artistic anatomy 1: the skeleton under the skin {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Elective (Fridays). See the Electives page for the full list; this term's Friday sessions begin with artistic anatomy and poetry.</p>

**Why artists study bones.** Where bone lies just under the skin, it fixes the form: these **subcutaneous landmarks** do not change shape with fat or muscle, so they are the reliable measuring points of figure drawing.

**Key landmarks.** The *clavicles* (collarbones) and the *jugular notch* between them; the *acromion* at the point of the shoulder; the spinous process of the *seventh cervical vertebra* (the prominent bump at the base of the neck); the *sternum*; the *olecranon* (point of the elbow); the *iliac crest* and the *anterior superior iliac spine* at the front of the hip; the *patella*; the *malleoli* (ankle bones, the inner one higher than the outer).

**Proportion of the head.** In an adult head seen from the front, the eyes lie at about half the height of the head (chin to top of the skull), lower than beginners usually place them.

### Exercises

1. On your own body, find and touch each landmark listed. On a sheet, draw a simple standing figure and mark each landmark with a dot and label.
2. Look at a photograph of an adult face from the front. Measure the chin-to-crown height and the eye level. What fraction is it?
3. Why are the malleoli useful when drawing a standing figure?

### Answer key

1. Check positions against an anatomy reference (see SOURCES.md for recommended books).
2. Close to 1/2 for most adults.
3. They show the direction of the foot and the axis of the ankle: the inner malleolus is higher than the outer, so the line joining them slopes down toward the outside.
