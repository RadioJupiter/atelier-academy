## Latin · Capitulum II, part 1: gender and the genitive {#latin}

<p class="meta">30 min · Ørberg, <em>Familia Romana</em>, Cap. II</p>

**Read aloud twice** the first third of Capitulum II. Use the pictures and margin notes.

**Notice: three genders.** Nouns are masculine (most in *-us*, some in *-r*, such as *puer*, *vir*), feminine (most in *-a*) or neuter (*-um*).

**Notice: the genitive** answers *cuius?* (whose? of what?) and links one noun to another:

| | singular | plural |
|---|---|---|
| masc. / neut. | -ī (fluvi**ī**, oppid**ī**) | -ōrum (fluvi**ōrum**, oppid**ōrum**) |
| feminine | -ae (insul**ae**) | -ārum (insul**ārum**) |

*Rōma oppidum Itali**ae** est.* Rome is a town of Italy. *Nīlus fluvius Aegypt**ī** est.*

### Exercises

1. Give the genitive singular and plural: *fluvius, insula, oppidum, prōvincia, servus.*
2. Complete: (a) *Sicilia est insula Itali__.* (b) *Nīlus est fluvius Aegypt__.* (c) *Numerus insul__ magnus est* (plural). (d) *Numerus oppid__ parvus est* (plural).
3. Answer with the genitive: (a) *Cuius prōvincia est Sicilia?* (Sicily is a province of the Roman Empire: *imperium Rōmānum*.) (b) *Cuius fluvius est Nīlus?*

### Answer key

1. fluviī / fluviōrum; insulae / insulārum; oppidī / oppidōrum; prōvinciae / prōvinciārum; servī / servōrum.
2. (a) Itali**ae** (b) Aegypt**ī** (c) insul**ārum** (d) oppid**ōrum**.
3. (a) *Sicilia prōvincia imperiī Rōmānī est.* (b) *Nīlus fluvius Aegyptī est.*

## Mathematics · Geometry II: congruent triangles and the isosceles triangle {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Kiselev, <em>Planimetry</em>, triangles</p>

**Congruence** means one figure can be placed exactly on the other. Kiselev proves the first criterion by superposition; the others follow:

- **SAS**: two sides and the included angle equal ⇒ triangles congruent.
- **ASA**: a side and the two angles adjacent to it equal ⇒ congruent.
- **SSS**: three sides equal ⇒ congruent.

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal; this is how congruence proves other facts.

**Theorem.** In an isosceles triangle the angles at the base are equal.
*Proof.* Let AB = AC. Compare triangle ABC with triangle ACB (the same triangle, read with B and C swapped): AB = AC, AC = AB, and the included angle A is common. By SAS they are congruent, so ∠B (in the first) = ∠C (in the second). ∎

**Write proofs in two columns** until the habit is automatic: *statement | reason*.

### Exercises

1. Segments AB and CD bisect each other at O. Prove that AC = BD.
2. On one side of an angle with vertex O, mark points A and C; on the other, B and D, so that OA = OB and OC = OD. Prove AD = BC.
3. In isosceles triangle ABC (AB = AC), M is the midpoint of BC. Prove that AM bisects angle A and is perpendicular to BC.
4. Prove the converse of the base-angle theorem: if ∠B = ∠C in triangle ABC, then AB = AC.
5. Is "side–side–angle" (two sides and a *non-included* angle) a valid congruence criterion? Investigate with an angle of 30°, an adjacent side of 10 and an opposite side of 6.
6. Prove that all three angles of an equilateral triangle are equal.

### Answer key

1. In triangles AOC and BOD: AO = OB, CO = OD (bisected), ∠AOC = ∠BOD (vertical). SAS ⇒ congruent ⇒ AC = BD. ∎
2. Triangles OAD and OBC: OA = OB, OD = OC, angle O common. SAS ⇒ AD = BC. ∎
3. Triangles ABM and ACM: AB = AC, BM = MC, AM common. SSS ⇒ congruent, so ∠BAM = ∠CAM (bisector) and ∠AMB = ∠AMC; these are adjacent and supplementary, so each is 90°. ∎
4. Compare triangle ABC with ACB: ∠B = ∠C, BC = CB, ∠C = ∠B. ASA ⇒ congruent ⇒ AB = AC. ∎
5. **Not valid.** With ∠A = 30°, AC = 10 and BC = 6 (opposite A): the height from C to the line of AB is 10 · sin 30° = 5 < 6 < 10, so a circle of radius 6 around C cuts that line at two points on the same side of A, giving two different triangles (∠B ≈ 56.4° or ≈ 123.6°).
6. AB = AC ⇒ ∠B = ∠C; AB = BC ⇒ ∠A = ∠C. So all three are equal. ∎

## Writing & Rhetoric · Franklin's method, part 1: making hints {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Benjamin Franklin's reconstruction exercise (Day 2's passage describes it); model text: Addison, <em>The Spectator</em> No. 10, 12 March 1711 (public domain)</p>

Franklin's method has four steps: (1) read a model paragraph closely; (2) write a short **hint** for each sentence (a few words recording the idea, not the wording); (3) put it away for several days; (4) rebuild the paragraph from the hints alone, then compare with the original and correct. You will do steps 1–2 today and step 4 on **Day 7**.

**The model** (*The Spectator*, No. 10; the original capitals and spelling are kept):

> It is with much Satisfaction that I hear this great City inquiring Day
 by Day after these my Papers, and receiving my Morning Lectures with a
 becoming Seriousness and Attention. My Publisher tells me, that there
 are already Three Thousand of them distributed every Day: So that if I
 allow Twenty Readers to every Paper, which I look upon as a modest
 Computation, I may reckon about Threescore thousand Disciples in
 *London* and *Westminster*, who I hope will take care to distinguish
 themselves from the thoughtless Herd of their ignorant and unattentive
 Brethren. Since I have raised to myself so great an Audience, I shall
 spare no Pains to make their Instruction agreeable, and their Diversion
 useful. For which Reasons I shall endeavour to enliven Morality with
 Wit, and to temper Wit with Morality, that my Readers may, if possible,
 both Ways find their account in the Speculation of the Day. And to the
 End that their Virtue and Discretion may not be short transient
 intermitting Starts of Thought, I have resolved to refresh their
 Memories from Day to Day, till I have recovered them out of that
 desperate State of Vice and Folly, into which the Age is fallen. The
 Mind that lies fallow but a single Day, sprouts up in Follies that are
 only to be killed by a constant and assiduous Culture. It was said of
 *Socrates*, that he brought Philosophy down from Heaven, to inhabit
 among Men; and I shall be ambitious to have it said of me, that I have
 brought Philosophy out of Closets and Libraries, Schools and Colleges,
 to dwell in Clubs and Assemblies, at Tea-tables, and in Coffee-houses.
 

### Exercises

1. Read the paragraph aloud twice. Look up any word you cannot define exactly (for example *Computation, Threescore, Speculation, fallow, assiduous*; note that *Culture* here keeps its older sense of cultivating soil).
2. Count the sentences. For each one, write a hint of **no more than six words** on a separate sheet. Number them.
3. Put the hints in an envelope dated today. Do not reread the paragraph before Day 7.
4. In two sentences: what is Addison's purpose in this paragraph, and who is his imagined reader?

### Answer key

1. *Computation*: calculation or estimate. *Threescore*: sixty (so "threescore thousand" = 60,000). *Speculation*: here, an essay or reflection (the paper itself). *Fallow*: ploughed but left unsown. *Assiduous*: persistent, diligent.
2. The paragraph has seven sentences (the second runs on past a colon). Example hints: (1) City eager for my papers. (2) 3,000 daily × 20 readers = 60,000. (3) Big audience: agreeable, useful. (4) Wit and morality combined. (5) Refresh memories daily against folly. (6) Idle mind sprouts follies. (7) Socrates: heaven to men; me: clubs, coffee-houses.
3. —
4. To announce the paper's aim (moral improvement made agreeable) and flatter its readers into continuing; the imagined reader is the educated London public at tea-tables and coffee-houses.

## Science · Combustion and gases: the quantitative model {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Phenomenon continued; olympiad-style problems</p>

**Combustion of wax.** Paraffin wax is a mixture of alkanes, roughly C₂₅H₅₂. Complete combustion: C₂₅H₅₂ + 38 O₂ → 25 CO₂ + 26 H₂O. (Balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen: 25 × 2 + 26 = 76 O atoms = 38 O₂.)

**Ideal gas law.** pV = nRT, with T in kelvin (K = °C + 273.15). At constant pressure, V ∝ T; at constant volume, p ∝ T.

**Olympiad habit:** estimate before calculating, keep units in every line, and check the order of magnitude at the end.

### Problems

1. Balance the combustion of methane (CH₄) and of propane (C₃H₈).
2. Per molecule of O₂ consumed, how many molecules of CO₂ does burning C₂₅H₅₂ release? If all the water condensed, by what fraction would the number of gas molecules from the reaction fall, per O₂ used?
3. A 1.0 L jar of air at 20 °C is sealed by water. About how many moles of gas does it hold? (Use 24 L/mol at 20 °C and 1 atm.) What volume does 21% of that gas occupy?
4. The trapped air is heated from 20 °C to 80 °C at constant pressure. What fraction of it escapes as bubbles? (Hint: volume at 80 °C compared with the jar's volume.)
5. Estimate: how much does a typical candle's mass decrease in one hour? Explain your assumptions, then check it with a kitchen scale over an hour (with supervision).

### Answer key

1. CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O.
2. 25/38 ≈ **0.66 CO₂ per O₂**. Each O₂ removed is replaced by about 0.66 CO₂, so the gas count falls by about **one third** of the oxygen used, not all of it.
3. 1.0/24 ≈ **0.042 mol**; 21% of 1.0 L ≈ **0.21 L** of oxygen.
4. V₈₀/V₂₀ = 353/293 ≈ 1.20. The jar holds only its own volume, so 0.20/1.20 ≈ **17%** of the heated gas escapes.
5. Typical household candles burn several grams per hour (often roughly 5–10 g/h, depending on size and wick). Any reasoned estimate with stated assumptions (wick size, flame size) is acceptable; the measurement is the real check.

## Literature · Iliad, Books 2–3 {#literature}

<p class="meta">45 min · Read Books 2–3 before this lesson (the Catalogue of Ships in Book 2 may be skimmed)</p>

**Book 2.** Zeus sends Agamemnon a false dream promising victory. Agamemnon tests the army by proposing to sail home; the army rushes to the ships. Odysseus restores order and strikes Thersites, the one common soldier who speaks in the assembly. The book ends with the long Catalogue of Ships, a roll-call of the Greek contingents.

**Book 3.** Paris (Alexandros) offers to settle the war in single combat with Menelaus. On the wall of Troy, Helen names the Greek leaders for Priam. Menelaus wins the duel, but Aphrodite snatches Paris away to safety in his bedroom and summons Helen to him.

### Questions

1. Why might the poet show the whole army nearly running home in Book 2? What does it reveal about Agamemnon's leadership?
2. Thersites says many of the same things Achilles said in Book 1. Why is his speech treated so differently? What does this suggest about the poem's view of rank?
3. In the scene on the wall, how does Homer make Helen sympathetic, or not? Quote one line.
4. Write one TWR-style sentence beginning with *Although* that states a judgement about Paris.

### Answer key

1. It shows that the army is exhausted after nine years and that Agamemnon misjudges his men; only Odysseus's intervention saves the situation.
2. He is a commoner and is presented as ugly and insolent; the poem treats speech against the leaders as a privilege of rank, though readers may notice that his complaints are not wrong.
3. Answers vary; strong ones note her self-reproach and Priam's gentleness toward her, with a quotation and line number.
4. Model: *Although Paris issues the challenge himself, he leaves the duel in the arms of a goddess rather than finishing it.*

## Atelier · Value: the nine-step scale {#atelier}

<p class="meta">90 min</p>

**Value** is lightness or darkness, independent of colour. Before modelling form, the eye must judge value accurately. (The Munsell colour system, a standard reference, measures value on a scale from 0 for pure black to 10 for pure white.)

**Build a nine-step scale.** Draw nine 2 cm squares in a row. Square 1 is the white of the paper; square 9 is the darkest graphite you can achieve (layered 2B or softer). Square 5 is the visual middle. Then fill 3 and 7 halfway between, then 2, 4, 6 and 8. Steps must look **equally spaced**; that is a judgement for the eye, not for the pencil pressure.

**Technique.** Apply graphite in even layers of light, overlapping strokes in several directions; don't press hard early (it polishes the paper and stops later layers). Squint to compare values: squinting reduces detail and makes values easier to judge.

### Exercises

1. *(45 min)* Make the nine-step scale. Punch a small hole in a grey card; use it to isolate each square and compare it with its neighbours.
2. *(30 min)* Make a smooth **gradation** 15 cm long from 9 to 1 with no visible steps.
3. *(15 min)* Hold your scale against objects in the room and identify the value number of five surfaces.

### Answer key

The steps should appear equally spaced when squinting; the most common fault is too many light steps and a sudden jump to dark. A good scale makes step 5 a convincing middle grey. In the gradation, no bands or blotches should be visible at arm's length.

## Economics & personal finance · Scarcity and opportunity cost {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Economics (Thursdays)</p>

**Scarcity**: resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up. The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the *best alternative given up*, not the sum of every alternative.

**Thinking at the margin**: decisions are about *one more* unit: one more hour of practice, one more item. Compare the extra benefit with the extra cost.

**Sunk costs** are costs already paid that cannot be recovered. They should not affect a forward-looking decision.

### Exercises

1. You can spend Saturday afternoon (a) working a paid shift for $60, (b) attending a free museum lecture, or (c) painting. You choose painting. Which is the opportunity cost? Why not "$60 plus the lecture"?
2. You paid $40 for a workshop ticket, but on the day you would rather draw at home, which you value more than the workshop. Should the $40 matter? Explain.
3. In one hour you can either sketch 6 studies or write 3 pages. What is the opportunity cost of one page, in sketches?

### Answer key

1. The single best alternative given up: whichever of the shift or the lecture you value more. You could only have done one of them, so the cost is one, not both.
2. No. The $40 is spent whether you go or not (a sunk cost). Choose the option you value more from now on.
3. One page takes 1/3 hour, which could produce 2 sketches: **2 sketches per page.**
