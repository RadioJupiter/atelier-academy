## Mathematics · Geometry II: congruent triangles and the isosceles triangle {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Kiselev, <em>Planimetry</em>, triangles</p>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="congruence" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear congruence">Hear</button> congruence <span class="symbol">△ABC ≅ △DEF</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A matching of two figures so that corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal; one can be placed exactly on the other.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-isosceles">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="isosceles" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear isosceles">Hear</button> isosceles</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A triangle with two sides equal. The angles at the base (opposite those sides) are equal.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin īsoscelēs, from Ancient Greek ἰσοσκελής (‘equal-legged’), from ἴσος (‘equal’) + σκέλος (‘leg’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If AB = AC and ∠A = 40°, each base angle is 70°.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Isosceles does not mean ‘all three sides equal’ (that is equilateral, which is a special isosceles triangle).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The base-angle theorem is proved by congruence: the triangle is compared with itself with the equal sides swapped.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Two triangles are **congruent** when one can be fitted exactly onto the other: corresponding sides and corresponding angles are equal. Standard tests (taught as axioms or early theorems, depending on the book) include SSS, SAS, ASA, and often RHS for right triangles. You must name the correspondence (A to D, B to E, C to F) or the equalities are meaningless.

**Congruence** means one figure can be placed exactly on the other. Kiselev proves the first criterion by superposition; the others follow:

- **SAS**: two sides and the included angle equal ⇒ triangles congruent.
- **ASA**: a side and the two angles adjacent to it equal ⇒ congruent.
- **SSS**: three sides equal ⇒ congruent.

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal; this is how congruence proves other facts.

**Theorem.** In an isosceles triangle the angles at the base are equal.
*Proof.* Let AB = AC. Compare triangle ABC with triangle ACB (the same triangle, read with B and C swapped): AB = AC, AC = AB, and the included angle A is common. By SAS they are congruent, so ∠B (in the first) = ∠C (in the second). ∎

**Write proofs in two columns** until the habit is automatic: *statement | reason*.

Franklin's method, as he describes it in his *Autobiography*: take a passage, jot brief notes (hints) of the ideas in order, put the passage away, then rewrite from the hints and compare. A later step is to scramble the hints and practice finding a good order. Today you make the hints and rewrite.

Franklin's method has four steps: (1) read a model paragraph closely; (2) write a short **hint** for each sentence (a few words recording the idea, not the wording); (3) put it away for several days; (4) rebuild the paragraph from the hints alone, then compare with the original and correct. You will do steps 1–2 today and step 4 on **Day 7**.

**The model** (*The Spectator*, No. 10; the original capitals and spelling are kept):

> It is with much Satisfaction that I hear this great City inquiring Day
 by Day after these my Papers, and receiving my Morning Lectures with a
 becoming Seriousness and Attention. My Publisher tells me, that there
 are already Three Thousand of them distributed every Day: So that if I
 allow Twenty Readers to every Paper, which I look upon as a modest
 Computation, I may reckon about Threescore thousand Disciples in
 *London* and *Westminster*, who I hope will take care to distinguish
 themselves from the thoughtless Herd of their ignorant and unattentive
 Brethren. Since I have raised to myself so great an Audience, I shall

 Wit, and to temper Wit with Morality, that my Readers may, if possible,

 End that their Virtue and Discretion may not be short transient

**Combustion of wax.** Paraffin wax is a mixture of alkanes, roughly C₂₅H₅₂. Complete combustion: C₂₅H₅₂ + 38 O₂ → 25 CO₂ + 26 H₂O. (Balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen: 25 × 2 + 26 = 76 O atoms = 38 O₂.)

**Ideal gas law.** pV = nRT, with T in kelvin (K = °C + 273.15). At constant pressure, V ∝ T; at constant volume, p ∝ T.

**Olympiad habit:** estimate before calculating, keep units in every line, and check the order of magnitude at the end.

A **nine-step value scale** is a row of grays from white to black, each step visually even. Value is how light or dark a surface is, separate from its color. Even steps matter: if step 5 is too dark, every later judgment of mid-gray will be off. Squint to compare the steps at arm's length.

**Value** is lightness or darkness, independent of color. Before modeling form, the eye must judge value accurately. (The Munsell color system, a standard reference, measures value on a scale from 0 for pure black to 10 for pure white.)

**Build a nine-step scale.** Draw nine 2 cm squares in a row. Square 1 is the white of the paper; square 9 is the darkest graphite you can achieve (layered 2B or softer). Square 5 is the visual middle. Then fill 3 and 7 halfway between, then 2, 4, 6 and 8. Steps must look **equally spaced**; that is a judgment for the eye, not for the pencil pressure.

**Technique.** Apply graphite in even layers of light, overlapping strokes in several directions; don't press hard early (it polishes the paper and stops later layers). Squint to compare values: squinting reduces detail and makes values easier to judge.

## Economics & personal finance · Scarcity and opportunity cost {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Economics (Thursdays)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-scarcity">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="scarcity" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear scarcity">Hear</button> scarcity</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The fact that resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English scarsite, from Norman French escarsete; in English, scarce + -ity.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A Saturday afternoon can be spent on a paid shift, a lecture, or painting, but not on all three.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Scarcity is not ‘poverty only’, and it is not ‘having zero of something’. A person with money still has scarce time.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Because of scarcity, each choice has an opportunity cost: the best alternative given up.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-opportunity-cost">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="opportunity cost" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear opportunity cost">Hear</button> opportunity cost</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The value of the single best alternative given up when a choice is made, not the sum of every alternative.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Opportunity is from Latin opportūnitās (via French); cost is the ordinary English word. The pair is a technical term in economics.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If you paint, the opportunity cost is whichever of the paid shift or the lecture you valued more — one of them, not both.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not ‘$60 plus the lecture’, because you could not have done both of the things you gave up at the same time.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Scarcity forces a choice. Opportunity cost names what that choice actually costs.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-sunk-cost">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sunk cost" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sunk cost">Hear</button> sunk cost</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A cost already paid that cannot be recovered, and that should not affect a forward-looking decision.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A $40 workshop ticket is spent whether you go or stay home. If you would rather draw at home, the $40 should not drag you to the workshop.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A sunk cost is not an opportunity cost, and ‘I already paid’ is not a good reason to continue.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Opportunity cost looks forward to the best alternative still open. A sunk cost looks backward and should be ignored.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Scarcity**: resources (time, money, materials, attention) are limited relative to what we want, so every choice gives something up. The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the *best alternative given up*, not the sum of every alternative.

**Thinking at the margin**: decisions are about *one more* unit: one more hour of practice, one more item. Compare the extra benefit with the extra cost.

**Sunk costs** are costs already paid that cannot be recovered. They should not affect a forward-looking decision.
