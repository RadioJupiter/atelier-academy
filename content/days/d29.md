## Latin · Capitulum VIII, part 4: the ablative of price, and tantus {#latin}

<p class="meta">30 min · Text: Ørberg, <em>Familia Romana</em>, Cap. VIII · Method: two frames for price; original sentences; invented sums</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-ablative-of-price">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ablative of price" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ablative of price">Hear</button> ablative of price</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>With verbs of buying, selling, and costing (<em>emere</em>, <em>vēndere</em>, <em>cōnstāre</em>), the price is put in the ablative, with no preposition: you pay <em>with</em> that money.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd><em>Hic ānulus decem sēstertiīs cōnstat.</em> This ring costs ten sesterces.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>When the price is the predicate of <em>pretium est</em>, it is nominative: <em>Pretium est decem sēstertiī.</em> Do not mix the two frames. And this is not the dative (‘to/for someone’).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Numbers such as <em>decem</em> (10) and <em>vīgintī</em> (20) are indeclinable in this lesson. Invented prices only; do not copy a textbook shop scene word for word.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-tantus">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="tantus, -a, -um" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear tantus, -a, -um">Hear</button> tantus, -a, -um</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An adjective meaning ‘so great, of such a size (or price).’ It often pairs with <em>quantus</em> (‘how great, as great as’).</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a demonstrative like <em>hic</em> or <em>ille</em>, though it points to a degree. It agrees like a first-and-second-declension adjective.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A first working use is enough: so great a price, such a ring. Fine distinctions of <em>quantī</em> as a genitive of price can wait if they are not in today’s reading.</dd></div>

## Mathematics · Geometry XV: the circumcircle, revisited with these two weeks’ theorems {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: week-4 perpendicular bisectors, plus the semicircle and cyclic-quad facts as tests</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-circle-through-three-points">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="circle through three points (review)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear circle through three points (review)">Hear</button> circle through three points (review)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A unique circle passes through three points that are not on a single line (the circumcircle of the triangle they form). Its center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides — a construction you already have from week 4.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The circumcircle of a right triangle has the hypotenuse as diameter (the converse of the angle in a semicircle, used honestly as such).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a circle through four points in general: four points have to satisfy an extra condition (for example, a cyclic quadrilateral) to lie on one circle.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Today reviews that construction and ties it to these two weeks’ theorems, without a new construction script.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**What you already can do.** To find the center of the circle through A, B, and C (not collinear), construct the perpendicular bisectors of AB and of AC; they meet at the center O, and OA = OB = OC is the radius. That was Day 12’s perpendicular bisector as a locus, used as a circumcenter.

**What these two weeks adds.** If angle C is right, O is the midpoint of AB, because AB is a diameter. You can still construct bisectors, but you can also know where O must be before you draw.

**A check on four points.** If you want a circle through A, B, C, and D, first put a circle through A, B, and C, then test whether D lies on it — or test a cyclic-quadrilateral condition (opposite angles supplementary) when ABCD is already a quadrilateral.

**No new compass step is taught today.** The sitting is a synthesis: construct or reason, then name a theorem.

Three non-collinear points determine a circle; the center is where perpendicular bisectors meet. In a right triangle, the hypotenuse is a diameter, so the center is the midpoint of that side. Four points need an extra condition.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Concession and return in a three-paragraph close {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a real limit in the third paragraph, then a return in new words; Friday’s essay is next</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-concession-three">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="concession (three-paragraph close)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear concession (three-paragraph close)">Hear</button> concession (three-paragraph close)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A real limit or opposing point, granted in the closing paragraph, after which you return to the thesis in new words. The concession must change what a careful reader would have to admit; it is not a decoration.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Thesis: the school photosynthesis equation is a useful net summary. Concession: it does not show intermediate steps, and C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub> is a representative sugar. Return: as a net bookkeeping of atoms and of the overall change, the equation still earns its place, if it is labeled as a summary.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “some people might disagree, but they are wrong,” and not a second support paragraph that never grants a limit.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Days 19–20 already required a concession in two paragraphs. Today the close of a three-paragraph essay is the home of that job, unless a better place is earned.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Grant something real.** A late source is still late. A net equation is still a summary. A copy is still a copy. A converse is still a different implication. Then say, in new words, what remains of the thesis after that limit.

**Order in the close.** One or two sentences of concession; one or two sentences of return. Do not end on the concession without a return, and do not pretend the concession was never granted.

**A test.** If you can delete the concession and the essay is unchanged, it was not a concession.


</div>

## Science · Autumn color: unmasking, and a caution about new reds {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: two mechanisms, stated carefully; no invented percentages; chromatography as a hint, not a proof of every leaf</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-autumn-color">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="autumn color (careful account)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear autumn color (careful account)">Hear</button> autumn color (careful account)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A first account of why many deciduous leaves change color: chlorophyll often breaks down in autumn, so yellow and orange carotenoids already present can become visible. In some species, red or purple anthocyanins are made in the leaf in autumn; those colors are not always a mere unmasking of a pigment that was waiting in midsummer.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A yellow maple or aspen leaf can often be told as an unmasking story at this lesson’s level; a deep red leaf may involve new anthocyanin, not only a leftover carotenoid.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “all red leaves were secretly red all summer,” and not invented percentages of trees, pigments, or days of frost. Weather, species, and the leaf’s own chemistry all matter, and this course will not reduce them to a single number.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Chromatography already showed that a green leaf can hide a yellow pigment. Today that fact is used, with the anthocyanin caution attached.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-anthocyanin">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="anthocyanin" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear anthocyanin">Hear</button> anthocyanin</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A class of red and purple pigments that, in many autumn leaves, are produced in the season itself, rather than merely unmasked. This course names them and keeps the caution; it does not invent a biosynthetic pathway or a percentage of species.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A bright red autumn leaf is often an anthocyanin story in part, not only a carotenoid story.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not another name for carotenoids, and not a required ingredient in every autumn leaf (many yellow leaves can be told without them).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The etymology in the dump we checked was not full enough to quote beyond the obvious antho- (‘flower’) element, so a fuller origin is omitted here.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Two mechanisms, not one slogan.**

1. **Unmasking.** Chlorophyll, which dominated the midsummer leaf, often breaks down in autumn. Carotenoids already present can then be seen as yellow or orange.
2. **New reds.** In some species, anthocyanins are made in the leaf in autumn, giving reds and purples that were not simply waiting as the leaf’s midsummer color.

**What we will not do.** No invented fraction of “how much color is unmasking.” No claim that a frost is the one cause in every climate. No studio or kitchen experiment that asks you to cook leaves in a way this page has not made safe.

**A connection.** A chromatogram of a summer green leaf can already show a yellow band: the unmasking story has a laboratory hint before autumn. A chromatogram of a red autumn leaf is a different sample and is not required today.


</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Yellow and orange often come from carotenoids unmasked as chlorophyll breaks down. Reds and purples, in many cases, involve anthocyanins made in autumn, not only unmasked. No invented percentages. Species differ.


<p>Key terms: autumn color (careful account), anthocyanin.</p>

<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin <em>monēta</em>, from the temple of Juno Moneta in Rome, where a mint stood (Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A widely accepted coin or a modern currency typically does all three jobs in its own setting. A ticket to a play does a limited medium-of-exchange job at the door and is a poor general store of value or unit of account for a whole economy.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “a piece of paper with a number on it” as a complete definition (a page of doodles is paper with marks). And not a history of the first coin with a fixed year; early coinage in Asia Minor is often discussed by scholars for the first millennium BCE, and this course will not assign an exact year.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Specialization and trade (last week) already asked how people exchange. Money, when it works, lowers the need for a double coincidence of wants — you need not find a person who both has bread and wants your particular labor at the same moment.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-medium-of-exchange">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="medium of exchange" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear medium of exchange">Hear</button> medium of exchange</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The job of money as a thing commonly accepted in payment, so that trade does not have to be a direct swap of two particular goods.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a baker will take a standard coin (or a standard currency) for bread, that coin is working as a medium of exchange in that setting.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a unit of account by itself (you can write prices in a unit you rarely hand over), and not automatically a good store of value in a time of rapid inflation.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The three jobs can come apart, which is why they are taught as a list, not as one word.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Three jobs, tested with near-misses.** A gift card for one shop is a limited medium of exchange. A unit on a blackboard (“class points”) might be a unit of account inside a game and still not be money in the town. A house can store value and still be a clumsy medium of exchange. Money, when it is working, does all three well enough in its setting.

**A connection to last week.** In the invented Ana-and-Beau table, they swapped pages and staves directly. If they had a classroom token that both trusted, they could sell and buy without finding an exact match of wants. The token would be a model of a medium of exchange — still invented, still labeled.

**What we will not do.** No invented inflation percentage, no lecture on a particular central bank, and no exact year for “the first coin.” The Latin *sēstertius* of this week’s language lesson is a named historical coin; it is not a complete history of money.
