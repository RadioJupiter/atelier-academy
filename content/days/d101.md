## Mathematics · Geometry LV: the volume of a frustum of a right cone {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a frustum is what remains when a smaller similar cone is cut from a larger one; its volume is the difference of the two cone volumes, which is also V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²)</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-volume-of-a-frustum-of-a-right-cone">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="volume of a frustum of a right cone" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear volume of a frustum of a right cone">Hear</button> volume of a frustum of a right cone</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A frustum of a right cone is the solid that remains when a smaller cone, similar to the original, is cut away by a plane parallel to the base. Its volume is the large cone’s volume minus the small cone’s volume, both already taught as V = (1/3)πr²h. If the frustum has lower radius R, upper radius r, and height h, that difference is also V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²). The small cone is similar to the large one, so their heights and radii are in the same ratio, from the already-taught scale factor.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A classroom large cone has radius 6 and height 8, invented lengths. A similar small cone of height 4 then has radius 3. The frustum between them has height 4, volume (1/3)π×36×8 minus (1/3)π×9×4, which is 96π − 12π = 84π cubic units. The formula gives the same: (1/3)π×4×(36 + 18 + 9) = 84π.</dd></div>

<p>A classroom large cone has radius R = 6 and height 8, invented lengths. A similar cone has sides in the same ratio, already taught as a scale factor. If the small cone has height 4, half of 8, its radius is half of 6, so r = 3. The frustum has height 4.</p>
<p>Volume of the large cone: (1/3)π × 36 × 8 = 96π. Volume of the small cone: (1/3)π × 9 × 4 = 12π. The frustum is the difference: 84π cubic units, left in terms of π.</p>
<p>The same volume is V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²) = (1/3)π × 4 × (36 + 18 + 9) = 84π. Use either the difference of two cones, or this formula, once the similar heights and radii are in place. Do not invent an official monument’s volume.</p>

## Seminar · Logic: Felapton, a first look at a third-figure particular negative {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: Felapton (EAO-3). No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. Last week’s Darapti is a contrast, not a first look</p>

<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Felapton is a valid third-figure mood, EAO-3: No M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are not P. The middle term is the subject of both premises, as in last week’s Darapti. The conclusion is particular and negative: some S are not P, not “no S are P,” and not “all S are P.” The premises together require that some S exist, because all M are S and there is at least one M in the universal negative, in the traditional reading used here.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>No island-built temples are wooden; all island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden. The sentence is invented, so that the figure can be seen; it is not a historical claim about a named building.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not last week’s Darapti (AAI-3: all M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P), which has a particular affirmative conclusion. Not last week’s second-figure Baroco. Other third-figure moods remain reserved. Invented terms are classroom terms, not official classifications.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The third figure, already taught, and last week’s Darapti, as a contrast.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>Last week’s Darapti, still in the third figure, had two universal affirmatives and a particular affirmative conclusion: some S are P. Today a second third-figure mood begins, Felapton, whose conclusion is particular and negative.</p>
<p>No M are P. All M are S. Therefore some S are not P. The middle term is still the subject of both premises. The conclusion is not “no S are P,” because the premises do not say that every S is among the M, only that the M, which are not P, are among the S. So some of the S, at least those that are M, are not P.</p>
<p>An invented classroom example, not a historical claim: no island-built temples are wooden; all island-built temples are coastal works; therefore some coastal works are not wooden.</p>
