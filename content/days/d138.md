<p>The same formula can be rearranged when all three sides are known and an angle is wanted: cos C = (a² + b² − c²)/(2ab), where c is the side opposite the angle being found. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, the cosine of the angle opposite 5 is (9 + 16 − 25)/(2·3·4) = 0/24 = 0, so that angle is 90°. On an invented equilateral triangle with sides 5, 5, and 5, cos C = (25 + 25 − 25)/50 = 1/2, so C = 60°. On an invented triangle with sides 3, 5, and 7, the cosine of the angle opposite 7 is (9 + 25 − 49)/(2·3·5) = (−15)/30 = −1/2, so that angle is 120°. The side lengths are invented classroom numbers, chosen so the cosine is a value already known from the unit circle. Do not invent a degree measure that the cosine does not give exactly, and do not switch to radians.</p>

## Physics · Official Examples 17.2 and 17.3, and a factor of two in intensity {#physics}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: official OpenStax arithmetic; Table 17.3; invented frequencies stay labeled invented if used</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<div class="keyterms recall-terms" markdown="0">

<li><a href="d136.html#term-sound-intensity-level-the-decibel">sound intensity level (the decibel)</a> — Day 136</li>

<p>Open a card if you need a reminder; then close it and use the term in your own words.</p>
</div>
<p>Example 17.2 in OpenStax 17.3 calculates the sound intensity level of a sound wave in air at 0°C with pressure amplitude 0.656 Pa. The official speed of sound in air at 0°C is 331 m/s, and the official density of air at atmospheric pressure and 0°C is 1.29 kg/m³. The intensity is I = (Δp)² / (2 ρ vw) = (0.656 Pa)² / (2 × 1.29 kg/m³ × 331 m/s) = 5.04×10^−4 W/m². Then β = 10 log10(I/I0) = 10 log10(5.04×10^8) = 10 × 8.70 = 87 dB. These are official Example 17.2 figures, not invented official measurements of a classroom sound.</p><p>Example 17.3 shows that if one sound is twice as intense as another, the levels differ by 10 log10(2.00) = 3.01 dB, or about 3 dB. Table 17.3 lists the official correspondences: an intensity ratio of 2.0 with 3.0 dB; 5.0 with 7.0 dB; 10.0 with 10.0 dB. OpenStax notes that a 56.0 dB sound is twice as intense as a 53.0 dB sound, and that a 97.0 dB sound is half as intense as a 100 dB sound. Do not invent official dB values for real places not in the table. The sound pressure level remains reserved.</p>

</div>

<p>Last week, in Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, <em>Buildings</em> I.3.14–15, Justinian found a shrine of the Archangel Michael “small and very badly lighted,” “quite like a tiny bedroom,” and “tore this down, even to the lowest foundations.” Today the same translation continues, I.3.16–18, still as posted on LacusCurtius. “And increasing its size to the proportions which it now displays, he transformed it into a marvellously beautiful building.” “Marvellously” is Dewing’s wording and stays inside the quotation. Teaching prose uses US spelling.</p><p>“For the church is in the form of a rectangle (tetrapleuron), and the length appears not much greater than the width.” “And at either end of the side which faces the east a thick wall was perfectly constructed of many fitted stones, but in the middle it is drawn back so as to form a recess.” “On either side of this rise columns of naturally variegated hues which support the church. The opposite wall, which faces approximately the west, is pierced by the doors which lead into the church.” Dewing does not give an official length or an official count of those columns, and the classroom invents none.
