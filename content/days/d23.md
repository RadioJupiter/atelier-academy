## Latin · Capitulum VII, part 3: reflexive sē, versus eum and eam {#latin}

<p class="meta">30 min · Text: Ørberg, <em>Familia Romana</em>, Cap. VII · Method: the natural method · Original practice sentences</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-reflexive-se">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="reflexive sē" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear reflexive sē">Hear</button> reflexive sē</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The pronoun <em>sē</em> (accusative; also used as ablative) refers back to the subject of its own clause: the subject acts on himself, herself, or themselves.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd><em>Mārcus sē in aquā videt.</em> Marcus sees himself in the water. The one seen is the same as the one who sees.</dd></div>

**The test.** Ask: is the object the same person as the subject? If yes, *sē*. If no, *eum* or *eam* (or a name).

Original sentences: *Mārcus sē in aquā videt.* *Iūlia sē lavat.* *Mārcus Iūliam videt; Iūlia eum nōn videt.* *Puerī sē lavant.*

*sē* does not change for masculine, feminine, or plural in this form. The verb tells you the number: *videt* against *vident*, *lavat* against *lavant*.

## Mathematics · Geometry XII: cyclic quadrilaterals {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Euclid III.22; pictorial first (inscribed angles on complementary arcs), then the supplement rule</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-cyclic-quadrilateral">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="cyclic quadrilateral" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear cyclic quadrilateral">Hear</button> cyclic quadrilateral</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A quadrilateral whose four vertices all lie on a single circle. It is inscribed in that circle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin <em>cyclicus</em>, from Greek κυκλικός, ‘circular’ (Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If A, B, C, and D lie on a circle, in that order around the circle, then ABCD is cyclic.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not every quadrilateral is cyclic. A square is; a non-rectangular parallelogram, in general, is not (its opposite angles are equal, but they need not be supplementary).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Euclid III.22: the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary — they add to 180°.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-opposite-angles-supplementary">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="opposite angles supplementary" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear opposite angles supplementary">Hear</button> opposite angles supplementary</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In a cyclic quadrilateral, each pair of opposite angles sums to 180°. That is the usable form of Euclid III.22 in this course.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If ∠A = 70°, then ∠C = 110°. If ∠B = 95°, then ∠D = 85°.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Adjacent angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are not forced to be supplementary by this theorem (they may or may not be). Do not apply ‘interior angles on the same side’ from parallels unless you have parallels.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A reason in outline: an opposite pair of inscribed angles stand on arcs that together make the whole circle, so their measures add to half of 360°, which is 180°.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**The fact.** If ABCD is cyclic, then ∠A + ∠C = 180°, and ∠B + ∠D = 180°.

**A check that is also a test.** If you are *told* a quadrilateral is cyclic, you may use the theorem. If you are asked whether a given quadrilateral *can* be cyclic, the opposite angles must be able to add to 180°. A parallelogram that is not a rectangle has opposite angles equal but generally not 90°, so a pair of equal obtuse angles cannot be supplementary, and that parallelogram is not cyclic.

**Numbers.** ∠A = 70° ⇒ ∠C = 110°. ∠B = 95° ⇒ ∠D = 85°. The four angles of any quadrilateral still sum to 360°, which is consistent: 70 + 95 + 110 + 85 = 360.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Participial phrases, and dangling modifiers {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: explicit teaching of the attachment rule, with repairs; no new political content</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-participial-phrase">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="participial phrase" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear participial phrase">Hear</button> participial phrase</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A phrase headed by a participle (often an -ing or a past form used as a modifier) that adds a circumstance or a description to a noun or to a clause, without itself being the main verb.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>“Standing on the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle.” Or, tighter: “The inscribed angle, standing on the same arc, is half the central angle.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a finite clause (which has a subject doing a tensed verb: “The angle stands on the same arc”). And not a second independent sentence spliced with a comma.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 13’s semicolon joins two independent clauses. A participle lets you downgrade one action into a modifier, so one clause stays in charge.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-dangling-modifier">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="dangling modifier" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear dangling modifier">Hear</button> dangling modifier</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A participial phrase that is not clearly attached to the noun it is meant to describe, so the sentence seems to say the wrong thing.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Broken: “Standing on the same arc, we measure half the central angle.” (It is the inscribed angle that stands on the arc, not “we.”) Repaired: “Standing on the same arc, the inscribed angle is half the central angle.” Or: “When the inscribed angle stands on the same arc, we measure half the central angle.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not every opening -ing phrase is a mistake — only those whose implied subject is not the subject (or the noun) that follows.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The repair is to name the right subject next to the phrase, or to rewrite the phrase as a finite clause with because, when, or who.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Yesterday you held two infinitives in one slot. Today you attach a modifier without starting a new main clause.

**A participle is a form of a verb used as a modifier.** *Standing*, *written*, *given*. The phrase includes its objects and adverbs: *standing on the same arc*, *written from the hints*, *given two sides of a right triangle*.

**The attachment rule.** The thing the phrase describes must be the noun the phrase is actually next to — usually the subject of the main clause.

Broken: *Written in 1776, we read the sentence as a model of parallelism.* (We were not written in 1776.)

Repaired: *Written in 1776, the sentence is a public-domain model of parallelism.* Or: *Because the sentence was written in 1776, we may use it as a public-domain model.*

Use the tool when it earns its keep: to tuck a circumstance beside a claim, not to decorate every sentence.


</div>

## Physics · Kinetic energy, and the work–energy theorem {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: KE = ½mv² from the constant-force case of W = Fs and v² = u² + 2as; then W_net = ΔKE</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-kinetic-energy">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="kinetic energy" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear kinetic energy">Hear</button> kinetic energy</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The energy an object has because it is moving. For a single object treated as a particle, KE = ½ m v², in joules when m is in kilograms and v is in meters per second.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Greek κινητικός, from κινέω, ‘I move’ (Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 4 kg object moving at 3 m/s has KE = ½ × 4 × 3² = 2 × 9 = 18 J.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the same as momentum (p = m v). Momentum is a vector and is linear in v; kinetic energy is a scalar and depends on v². A sign on v is squared away: speed is what enters KE.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Mass is the same quantity as in Newton’s second law. Doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, other things equal.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-work-energy-theorem">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="work–energy theorem" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear work–energy theorem">Hear</button> work–energy theorem</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy: W<sub>net</sub> = ΔKE = KE<sub>final</sub> − KE<sub>initial</sub>. This is the usable form in this course for a single particle, or an object that can be treated as one.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If 18 J of net work is done on a 4 kg object that starts at rest, its KE becomes 18 J, so ½ × 4 × v² = 18, v² = 9, v = 3 m/s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “all forces always increase speed.” Negative net work decreases KE. And the theorem uses *net* work: the sum of work by every force, or the work of the net force along the path.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Today’s proofs are computational: you will go from work to ΔKE, or from a change in speed back to the net work required.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The formula.** KE = ½ *m v*². Units: kg · (m/s)² = N·m = J, the same joule as yesterday.

**Why v².** If you push with a constant net force F along a distance s, W_net = F s. From the second law, F = m a, and from kinematics you already have v² = u² + 2 a s. Then W_net = m a s = ½ m (v² − u²) = KE_final − KE_initial. That is the work–energy theorem, in the constant-force case that this course starts with.

**Numbers.** 4 kg at 3 m/s: 18 J. 2 kg at 5 m/s: ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 J. From rest, 18 J of net work on 4 kg: v = 3 m/s.

**A signed check.** If a 2 kg object slows from 5 m/s to rest, ΔKE = 0 − 25 = −25 J, so the net work was −25 J (a braking force did that much negative work).


</div>

**A working picture, with its source dated.** After the kingship, Roman tradition describes a Republic: two consuls each year, other magistrates, assemblies of the people, and a Senate. That picture is clearest for the middle Republic, when writers such as Polybius (second century BCE) could watch Rome’s government at work. It is less well documented for the first decades after the traditional end of the kings.

**Polybius, Book 6, as a class of source.** Polybius was a Greek hostage and later a friend of leading Romans. He wrote to explain Rome’s rise to a Greek audience. His account of a “mixed” constitution — magistrates, Senate, and people each holding a share — is invaluable and is also an argument. Use it as a second-century analysis, not as a transcript of the year 509 BCE (a traditional date for the Republic’s beginning, to be treated as tradition).

**What to hold.** Two consuls, annual office, a Senate of weight, assemblies that could vote; no king. What not to hold yet: a detailed map of every assembly, or a claim that the early Republic already looked exactly like Polybius’s mid-Republican picture.

**The job.** Who receives the thing or the words? That person can be a pronoun: *me, te, le, nos, os, les*.

**The place.** Before a conjugated verb: *Le doy el libro.* Not *Doy le el libro.*

**Naming the person.** Spanish often adds *a Ana*, *a ellos* even when *le* or *les* is already there: *Le doy el libro a Ana.* The *a* here is the personal *a* you have already met, now with an indirect object. It does not replace *le*.

**Original sentences.** *Te doy un lápiz.* *Le pido un libro a don Carlos.* *Nos sirven la comida.* *Os nombro las palabras.* (or a simpler *Les digo la frase.*)

## Art history · High Classical Greece: the Parthenon and the Doryphoros {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: two type examples, looking first; dates conventional; copies and later damage named</p>

**High Classical Greece, in two objects.** The Parthenon (a temple on the Athenian Acropolis, mid-fifth century BCE, often dated about 447–432 BCE) and the Doryphoros of Polykleitos (a fifth-century bronze type, known from later copies).

**What to look at, in the Parthenon.** A Doric order: columns with flutes, simple capitals, a frieze of triglyphs and metopes; a sculpted pediment; a building designed to be walked around and looked up at. Then the later history: explosion, ruin, the removal of sculptures (the “Elgin Marbles” / Parthenon sculptures, a modern controversy this course will name without settling in a slogan), and restoration. A photograph of the building today is not a photograph of 440 BCE.

**What to look at, in the Doryphoros.** Contrapposto more fully developed than in the Kritios Boy: a clear weight leg and a clear free leg, hips and shoulders in a counter-tilt, the head slightly turned. Then the copy problem: most of what we see are later marble versions of a lost bronze. Describe the copy as a copy.

**Dates, said honestly.** “About 447–432 BCE” for the Parthenon’s main construction is a conventional scholarly dating, not a claim that this course has re-dated the building. Polykleitos belongs to the fifth century BCE; exact years of the bronze are not required.
