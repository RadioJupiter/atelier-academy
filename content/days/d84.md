## Latin · Capitulum XV: sī, nisi, at, and antequam {#latin}

<p class="meta">30 min · Latin · Capitulum XV continues. Original sentences only.</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-si-and-nisi">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="see and NIH-see" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear see and NIH-see">Hear</button> sī and nisi</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Sī means if. Nisi means if not, unless, or except. Nisi is ne + sī in origin as a compound; in this course it is learned as a conjunction that sets the negative case of an if-clause.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Sī tacētis, magister nōn īrātus est. — If you are silent, the schoolmaster is not angry. Nisi tacētis, magister īrātus est. — Unless you are silent, the schoolmaster is angry.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at nōn, and not a relative clause (quī, quae, quod were taught in Capitulum VIII).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>These words hook a clause onto another clause, so the Latin sentence can now hold a condition.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-at-and-antequam">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="at and ahn-TEH-kwahm" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear at and ahn-TEH-kwahm">Hear</button> at and antequam</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>At means but on the other hand; it stands first in its clause and sets two sides or two people against each other. Antequam means before, and introduces a clause.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Titus recitat, at Mārcus tacet. — Titus recites, but Marcus (on the other side) is silent. Antequam magister venit, puerī clāmant. — Before the schoolmaster comes, the boys shout.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at sed, which corrects or adds a limitation to what has just been said. At opposes two parties or two sides. Antequam is not the preposition ante used alone (already met as ‘before’ a noun).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Ante and quam were both met earlier; together they introduce a clause of time, ‘before …’.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<p><strong>Sī tacēs, recitō.</strong> — If you are silent, I recite. <strong>Nisi tacēs, nōn recitō.</strong> — Unless you are silent, I do not recite.</p>
<p><strong>At</strong> is not a first look at sed. Sed often corrects or limits the sentence just made. At stands at the front of its clause and sets two sides against each other: Ego recitō, at tū tacēs.</p>
<p><strong>Antequam magister venit, discipulī adsunt.</strong> — Before the schoolmaster comes, the pupils are present. Antequam takes a verb; it is not only the preposition ante with a noun.</p>

## Mathematics · Geometry XLI: mixed circle measure, and a ring {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: circumference, area, and sector together, then a ring as a difference of two areas</p>

<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-annulus">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ANN-yuh-lus" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ANN-yuh-lus">Hear</button> annulus</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An annulus, or ring, is the region between two circles that share a center. Its area is the difference of the two disk areas: A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²), where R is the outer radius and r is the inner radius.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A classroom ring with outer radius 5 and inner radius 3 has area π(25 − 9) = 16π.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the area of the outer circle alone, and not π(R − r)², which would be a much smaller circle of radius R − r, not the ring.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s area formula is used twice, then subtracted. The shared center matters: this lesson does not treat two off-center circles.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<p>The ring is what is left when the inner disk is removed from the outer disk:</p>
<p>A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²).</p>
<p>It is not π(R − r)². That expression is the area of a single circle whose radius is the difference of the two radii, and that is a different figure.</p>
<p>For a classroom outer radius 5 and inner radius 3, A = π(25 − 9) = 16π.</p>

## Science · Water: the liquid is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C {#science}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: a sourced comparison of liquid water at 0 °C and near 4 °C, and why ice forms at a lake surface</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-maximum-density-of-liquid-water-near-4-c">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="maximum density of liquid water near 4 degrees C" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear maximum density of liquid water near 4 degrees C">Hear</button> maximum density of liquid water near 4 °C</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Liquid water is denser near 4 °C than at 0 °C. The U.S. Geological Survey, using a Bureau of Reclamation table (1977), lists 0.99987 g/cm³ at 0 °C and 1.00000 g/cm³ at 4.0 °C for the figures used in that table. Those values are not mixed with yesterday’s NIST liquid-water figure.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In a quiet lake, water near 4 °C can sit lower than colder water near 0 °C, because it is denser. Ice, still less dense than either, forms and floats at the surface.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at density or at why ice floats, which were taught yesterday. Not a claim that every body of water on Earth is a still lake, and not a blended official number taken from two tables at once.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>USGS Water Science School, “Water Density,” https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-density , table credited to U.S. Department of the Interior, Bureau of Reclamation, 1977, Ground Water Manual, as cited there.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<p>Yesterday: ice at 0 °C is less dense than liquid water at 0 °C, so ice floats. Today: the liquid itself is not densest at the ice point.</p>
<p>The USGS water-density page, citing a Bureau of Reclamation table from 1977, gives these liquid-water figures (grams per cubic centimeter):</p>
<p>0 °C (32 °F): 0.99987. 4.0 °C (39.2 °F): 1.00000.</p>
<p>In that table, the liquid at 4.0 °C is denser than the liquid at 0 °C. This course does not mix those figures with yesterday’s NIST value of 0.99984 g/cm³ for liquid water at 0 °C. The two tables were built in different places; each is used with its own label.</p>
<p>A school consequence, also drawn on that USGS page, is this. As a still lake cools, water near 4 °C is denser than colder water closer to 0 °C, so the colder water can remain nearer the surface. Ice, less dense than the liquid, then forms at the surface rather than from the bottom up. That is a density argument, not a certificate about every lake, current, or winter.</p>

<p>Invented figures, labeled invented: P = 1,000, r = 0.10 a year, for three years, with compounding once a year in the compound case.</p>
<p>Simple: A = 1,000 × (1 + 0.10 × 3) = 1,000 × 1.30 = 1,300.</p>
<p>Compound: A = 1,000 × (1.10)^3. 1.10² = 1.21; 1.21 × 1.10 = 1.331; so A = 1,331.</p>
<p>The compound amount is 31 larger, in this invented example, because the second and third years pay interest on interest already added. That difference is not a universal official “always 31,” and the rate is not a bank’s published rate.</p>
<p>The rule of 72, already taught, is not required today, and must not be mixed into this comparison as if it replaced either formula.</p>
