## Mathematics · Geometry I: definitions, axioms and the first proofs {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Kiselev, <em>Planimetry</em> (Givental's English translation), Chapter 1</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

In Kiselev's geometry, a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (**axioms**), and every other statement (**theorem**) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each with a reason (a definition, an axiom, or a theorem already proved).

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**What geometry is doing.** Kiselev's geometry is a chain: a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (**axioms**), and every other statement (**theorem**) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each justified by a definition, an axiom or a proved theorem. "It looks right in the figure" is never a justification.

**Definitions to learn exactly.**
- Two angles are **adjacent** if they share a vertex and one side, and their other sides lie on opposite sides of the common side.
- Two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line are **supplementary**: they add to 180°.
- **Vertical angles** are two angles where the sides of one are the extensions of the sides of the other.

**Theorem.** Vertical angles are equal.
*Proof.* Let lines AC and BD meet at O. Angles AOB and BOC are adjacent with OA and OC forming a straight line, so ∠AOB + ∠BOC = 180°. Likewise ∠BOC + ∠COD = 180°, since OB and OD form a straight line. Hence ∠AOB = 180° − ∠BOC = ∠COD. ∎

**Converse.** The converse of "if P then Q" is "if Q then P". It must be proved separately and is often false. Write the converse of every theorem you learn and decide whether it is true.

</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

### Exercises

1. One of the four angles formed by two intersecting lines is 40° greater than another. Find all four angles.
2. Prove that the bisectors of two adjacent supplementary angles are perpendicular.
3. Prove that the bisectors of two vertical angles lie on one straight line.
4. Three lines pass through one point. How many pairs of vertical angles are formed? (Count only angles smaller than 180°.)

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. A definition, an axiom, or a theorem already proved (or a given).
2. No. The drawing can guide the proof, but the reasons must be written.

1. Two different angles cannot be vertical (vertical angles are equal), so they are adjacent: x + (x + 40°) = 180°, x = 70°. **70°, 110°, 70°, 110°.**
2. Let the angles be α and β with α + β = 180°. Each bisector makes α/2 and β/2 with the common side, so the angle between the bisectors is α/2 + β/2 = 90°. ∎
3. Let ∠AOB and ∠COD be vertical, each equal to α (OA opposite OC, OB opposite OD). Let OM bisect ∠AOB and ON bisect ∠COD. Then ∠MOB = α/2, ∠BOC = 180° − α (supplementary to ∠AOB), ∠CON = α/2. Sum: α/2 + (180° − α) + α/2 = 180°, so OM and ON form a straight angle: M, O, N are collinear. ∎
4. Six rays leave the point, forming C(6,2) = 15 pairs of rays; 3 pairs are opposite rays (straight angles), leaving 12 angles. Each has exactly one vertical partner, so there are **6 pairs**.
5. Converse: "Equal angles are vertical." **False**: e.g., two separate right angles in different places are equal but not vertical.
6. If ∠A = ∠B, then 180° − ∠A = 180° − ∠B. ∎

## Writing & Rhetoric · Imitatio: copywork and copia {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Renaissance grammar-school imitation (copying, analysing and varying model sentences); Erasmus, <em>De Copia</em></p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: copy an excellent passage exactly, notice how it is built, then write a new version that keeps the structure but changes the content. Erasmus's *De Copia* (1512) trains **copia**: many ways to say one idea. Today you copy, then vary.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Background.** Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: students copied excellent passages exactly, analysed how they worked, and then produced their own versions. Erasmus's textbook *De Copia* (first published in 1512) shows the method at its most extreme: in Book I, chapter 33, he varies one Latin sentence, *Tuae literae me magnopere delectarunt* ("Your letter pleased me greatly"), in 150 different ways. The goal is *copia*, a ready abundance of ways to say a thing, so that the writer can choose the best one.

**Today's model.** Benjamin Franklin describing how he taught himself to write (public domain, from his *Autobiography*):

> About this time I met with an odd volume of the *Spectator*. It was the third. I had never before seen any of them. I bought it, read it over and over, and was much delighted with it. I thought the writing excellent, and wished, if possible, to imitate it. With this view I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again, by expressing each hinted sentiment at length, and as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that should come to hand. Then I compared my *Spectator* with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Copy exactly first (spelling, punctuation, line breaks if asked). Then vary: same skeleton, new nouns and verbs. Do not paraphrase loosely until the copy is accurate.

## Science · Phenomenon: the candle under the jar {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Finnish phenomenon-based learning (start from a real phenomenon, cross subject lines) with Russian-olympiad-style estimation</p>

**The phenomenon.** Stand a short candle in a shallow dish of water, light it, and lower a glass jar over it until the rim is under water. The flame goes out and water rises into the jar. *Safety: do this with an adult present, on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can catch fire.*

**The common explanation** is "the flame uses up the oxygen, which is about a fifth of the air, so water rises to replace it." Today you will test that claim.

**Facts you need.** Dry air is about 21% oxygen by volume. Burning wax (a hydrocarbon) consumes oxygen but produces carbon dioxide and water vapour. For a typical wax molecule, roughly two molecules of CO₂ are produced for every three of O₂ consumed, and much of the water vapour condenses. A candle also goes out long before all the oxygen is gone.

**Procedure.**
1. Do the experiment three times with one candle. Mark the water level each time with tape. Watch closely: *when* does the water rise: while the flame burns, or after it goes out? Are there bubbles as the jar goes down?
2. Repeat with two or three candles together (same jar). If the rise were caused only by oxygen use, would more candles make a larger rise or the same rise?
3. Repeat once with the jar first warmed in hot tap water, then dried.

### Questions

1. Record your three heights as a fraction of the jar's height. Compare with 21%.
2. Using the observations from steps 1–3, decide which explanation the evidence supports: (a) oxygen is removed and nothing replaces it; (b) the gas is heated and expands (some escapes as bubbles), then cools and contracts after the flame dies. Give two observations that support your choice.
3. *(Estimation, olympiad style.)* Air at 20 °C is 293 K. If the trapped air were at an average of 350 K when the jar sealed, and then cooled back to 293 K at constant pressure, by what fraction would its volume shrink? (Use V ∝ T at constant pressure.)
4. Write one paragraph, using *because*, *but* and *so* at least once, explaining the phenomenon to someone who believes the oxygen explanation.

### Answer key

1. Results vary with the jar and timing; rises of roughly 10–20% are typical. The value is not reliably 21%.
2. **(b) is supported.** Typical evidence: the water rises mostly *after* the flame goes out, as the gas cools; bubbles escape while the jar is lowered over the hot flame; more candles usually give a *larger* rise even though the jar holds the same oxygen; the oxygen explanation also ignores the CO₂ produced. Careful studies (Vera, Rivera & Núñez, 2011; see SOURCES.md) reach the same conclusion: heating, escape of gas and cooling dominate, and oxygen consumption is not the main cause.
3. V₂/V₁ = 293/350 ≈ 0.837, so the volume shrinks by about **16%**: the same size as the observed rise, from cooling alone.
4. Check for correct physics and the three conjunctions used accurately.

## Literature · Homer, Iliad, Book 1 {#literature}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: great books read whole, with close reading and written answers · Read Book 1 before this lesson</p>

**Which translation.** Use one verse translation consistently: Richmond Lattimore (1951) is the most line-by-line; Robert Fagles (1990) is the most fluent; Emily Wilson (2023) is the most recent and compressed. Samuel Butler's prose translation (1898) is in the public domain and free online.

**Epic conventions to watch for.**
- The **invocation**: the poet asks the Muse (goddess) to sing, and names the subject in the first line: the *wrath* of Achilles.
- **In medias res**: the story begins in the middle, in the tenth year of the war.
- **Epithets**: fixed descriptive phrases ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn") that help an oral poet compose and help a listener follow.
- **Similes**: extended comparisons, often taken from nature or ordinary work.

**The plot of Book 1.** Chryses, a priest of Apollo, offers ransom for his captured daughter; Agamemnon refuses him; Apollo sends a plague on the Greek camp. Forced to return the girl, Agamemnon takes Briseis, Achilles' prize, instead. Achilles withdraws from the fighting and asks his mother, the goddess Thetis, to persuade Zeus to let the Greeks suffer until they honour him. Zeus agrees.

### Questions (answer in full paragraphs; cite line numbers from your translation)

1. What is the subject announced in the first line, and why is it a surprising subject for a war poem?
2. Who is more at fault in the quarrel, Agamemnon or Achilles? Argue one side, then state the strongest point for the other.
3. Find one simile or one repeated epithet in Book 1. Copy it, and explain what it adds.
4. How does Book 1 connect the world of the gods with the world of the humans? Give two examples.

### Answer key

Model points (your answer should use your own words and line references):
1. The wrath (anger) of Achilles. The poem is about one man's anger and its cost, not about the whole war or its victory; the first lines stress the deaths his anger causes among his own side.
2. Agamemnon: he dishonours a priest, causes the plague, and takes Briseis to save face. Achilles: he nearly draws his sword on his commander, and asks for his own army to suffer. A good answer weighs honour (*timē*) on both sides.
3. Any accurate example; the explanation should say what the comparison makes vivid or what the epithet stresses.
4. Examples: Apollo answers Chryses' prayer with the plague; Athena stops Achilles from drawing his sword; Thetis petitions Zeus; the gods quarrel on Olympus, mirroring the human quarrel.

## Atelier · Bargue plate: the block-in {#atelier}

<p class="meta">90 min · Method: Bargue–Gérôme <em>Drawing Course</em>, Part I (plates after casts)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

The **block-in** is the first stage of a Bargue copy: the largest shapes, the big angles, and the main proportions, all in light line. You are not drawing features yet. Charles Bargue's *Cours de dessin* (with Jean-Léon Gérôme, published by Goupil from the late 1860s) trains this by giving you a design to copy sight-size.

</div>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The plates.** Charles Bargue's *Cours de dessin*, made with Jean-Léon Gérôme and published by Goupil in Paris from the late 1860s, has 197 lithographs in three parts: plates after plaster casts, plates after master drawings, and figure studies in charcoal. Students copied them before drawing from casts. The plates are in the public domain; scans are available from the Bibliothèque nationale de France (Gallica), and the complete course is reprinted in Gerald Ackerman's 2003 edition.

**Choose** one of the first plates of Part I: a single feature (an eye, nose, ear or mouth) shown in outline beside a finished version. Print or display it at a known size and fix it upright next to your paper at the same size (sight-size).

**The block-in, in order.**
1. Mark the top, bottom and the two widest points. Check the overall height-to-width ratio with your measuring stick before anything else.
2. Enclose the shape in an **envelope** of a few long straight lines.
3. Use the **plumb line**: what lies directly below what? Mark these alignments.
4. Break the envelope into smaller straight lines. Check each **angle** against the vertical or horizontal.
5. Check **negative shapes** (the spaces around and between parts); they are often easier to judge than the forms themselves.
6. Draw lightly. Step back to your marked spot after every few lines.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Find the tallest height and the widest width first. Block large shapes. Check angles with a pencil. No small features until the big ones sit right.

</div>

## Music theory · Pitch, staff and clefs {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Music theory (Tuesdays)</p>

**Pitch names.** Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in higher and lower **octaves**. On a keyboard, the white keys carry these names; C is the white key just left of each group of two black keys.

**Half and whole steps.** A **half step** (semitone) is the distance between two adjacent keys, black or white. A **whole step** is two half steps. Between the white keys E–F and B–C there is no black key, so those are half steps; every other pair of adjacent white keys is a whole step.

**The staff and clefs.** Five lines and four spaces. The **treble clef** (G clef) curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F; spaces: F A C E. The **bass clef** (F clef) places F below middle C on the fourth line, between its two dots. Bass lines from the bottom: G B D F A; spaces: A C E G. **Middle C** sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff, or just above the bass staff.

### Exercises

1. Name the treble-clef note on: line 1, line 3, line 5, space 2, space 4.
2. Name the bass-clef note on: line 1, line 4, space 1, space 3.
3. Whole step or half step? E–F, F–G, B–C, A–B, C–D.
4. Starting on D, go up a whole step, then a half step. Which white keys do you land on?

### Answer key

1. E, B, F, A, E.
2. G, F, A, E.
3. half, whole, half, whole, whole.
4. D → E (whole step) → F (half step).
