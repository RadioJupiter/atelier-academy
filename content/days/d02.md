**What geometry is doing.** Kiselev's geometry is a chain: a few terms are left undefined (point, line, plane), a few statements are accepted without proof (**axioms**), and every other statement (**theorem**) is proved from those and from earlier theorems. A proof is a sequence of claims, each justified by a definition, an axiom or a proved theorem. "It looks right in the figure" is never a justification.

**Definitions to learn exactly.**
- Two angles are **adjacent** if they share a vertex and one side, and their other sides lie on opposite sides of the common side.
- Two adjacent angles whose outer sides form a straight line are **supplementary**: they add to 180°.
- **Vertical angles** are two angles where the sides of one are the extensions of the sides of the other.

**Theorem.** Vertical angles are equal.
*Proof.* Let lines AC and BD meet at O. Angles AOB and BOC are adjacent with OA and OC forming a straight line, so ∠AOB + ∠BOC = 180°. Likewise ∠BOC + ∠COD = 180°, since OB and OD form a straight line. Hence ∠AOB = 180° − ∠BOC = ∠COD. ∎

**Converse.** The converse of "if P then Q" is "if Q then P". It must be proved separately and is often false. Write the converse of every theorem you learn and decide whether it is true.

Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: copy an excellent passage exactly, notice how it is built, then write a new version that keeps the structure but changes the content. Erasmus's *De Copia* (1512) trains **copia**: many ways to say one idea. Today you copy, then vary.

**Background.** Renaissance schools taught writing by *imitatio*: students copied excellent passages exactly, analyzed how they worked, and then produced their own versions. Erasmus's textbook *De Copia* (first published in 1512) shows the method at its most extreme: in Book I, chapter 33, he varies one Latin sentence, *Tuae literae me magnopere delectarunt* ("Your letter pleased me greatly"), in 150 different ways. The goal is *copia*, a ready abundance of ways to say a thing, so that the writer can choose the best one.

**Today's model.** Benjamin Franklin describing how he taught himself to write (public domain, from his *Autobiography*):

> About this time I met with an odd volume of the *Spectator*. It was the third. I had never before seen any of them. I bought it, read it over and over, and was much delighted with it. I thought the writing excellent, and wished, if possible, to imitate it. With this view I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again, by expressing each hinted sentiment at length, and as fully as it had been expressed before, in any suitable words that should come to hand. Then I compared my *Spectator* with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them.

**The phenomenon.** Stand a short candle in a shallow dish of water, light it, and lower a glass jar over it until the rim is under water. The flame goes out and water rises into the jar. *Safety: do this with an adult present, on a non-flammable surface, away from anything that can catch fire.*

**The common explanation** is "the flame uses up the oxygen, which is about a fifth of the air, so water rises to replace it." Today you will test that claim.

**Facts you need.** Dry air is about 21% oxygen by volume. Burning wax (a hydrocarbon) consumes oxygen but produces carbon dioxide and water vapour. For a typical wax molecule, roughly two molecules of CO₂ are produced for every three of O₂ consumed, and much of the water vapour condenses. A candle also goes out long before all the oxygen is gone.

**Procedure.**
1. Do the experiment three times with one candle. Mark the water level each time with tape. Watch closely: *when* does the water rise: while the flame burns, or after it goes out? Are there bubbles as the jar goes down?
2. Repeat with two or three candles together (same jar). If the rise were caused only by oxygen use, would more candles make a larger rise or the same rise?
3. Repeat once with the jar first warmed in hot tap water, then dried.

Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine. Most nouns ending in *-o* are masculine (*el libro*), and most ending in *-a* are feminine (*la mesa*). Nouns ending in *-ción*, *-sión* and *-dad* are feminine (*la canción, la ciudad*). Learn every noun **with its article**, because there are exceptions: *el día, el mapa, el problema, la mano*, and *la foto* (short for *la fotografía*).

| | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| the (singular) | el | la |
| the (plural) | los | las |
| a / some | un / unos | una / unas |

**Plurals:** add *-s* after a vowel (*libros*); add *-es* after a consonant (*ciudades*); *-z* becomes *-ces* (*el lápiz → los lápices*).

## Music theory · Pitch, staff and clefs {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Music theory (Tuesdays)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-pitch">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="pitch" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear pitch">Hear</button> pitch</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>How high or low a note sounds: the perceived frequency of the sound.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Middle C has a familiar pitch to many musicians; the next C above it is an octave higher.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Pitch is not the same as volume (how loud the note is), and it is not the name of a clef.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The staff and the clefs are written pictures of pitch. Letter names A to G repeat in each octave.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-clef">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="clef" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear clef">Hear</button> clef</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The sign at the start of a staff that fixes which line is a named pitch: the treble (G) clef or the bass (F) clef in this lesson.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French clef, from Latin clāvis (‘a key’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The treble clef curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A clef is not a note, and the bass clef’s two dots do not sit around the same line as the treble clef’s curl.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Once the clef is set, the staff’s lines and spaces have names. Middle C sits on a ledger line between the two staves.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-staff">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="staff" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear staff">Hear</button> staff</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The five lines and four spaces on which notes are written.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Old English stæf (‘staff, letter of the alphabet’); the musical sense is a later use of the same word.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Without a clef, a staff is only five lines. With a treble clef, the bottom line is E.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A staff is not a scale, and it is not a bar in a bar model.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Pitch is what you hear; the staff, with a clef, is how you write it.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**Pitch names.** Western music uses seven letter names, A to G, repeating in higher and lower **octaves**. On a keyboard, the white keys carry these names; C is the white key just left of each group of two black keys.

**Half and whole steps.** A **half step** (semitone) is the distance between two adjacent keys, black or white. A **whole step** is two half steps. Between the white keys E–F and B–C there is no black key, so those are half steps; every other pair of adjacent white keys is a whole step.

**The staff and clefs.** Five lines and four spaces. The **treble clef** (G clef) curls around the second line from the bottom, which is G above middle C. Treble lines from the bottom: E G B D F; spaces: F A C E. The **bass clef** (F clef) places F below middle C on the fourth line, between its two dots. Bass lines from the bottom: G B D F A; spaces: A C E G. **Middle C** sits on a ledger line just below the treble staff, or just above the bass staff.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Letter names A–G repeat in each octave. A half step is the distance between adjacent keys; a whole step is two half steps. E–F and B–C are half steps on the white keys. Treble lines E G B D F, spaces F A C E. Bass lines G B D F A, spaces A C E G. Middle C sits on a ledger line between the staves.
