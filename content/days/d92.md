## Science · The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, from the Harvey/NIST table {#science}

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-enthalpy-of-sublimation-of-ice">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="enthalpy of sublimation of ice" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear enthalpy of sublimation of ice">Hear</button> enthalpy of sublimation of ice</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The enthalpy of sublimation of ice, written ΔsublH in the Harvey/NIST table, is the energy needed to turn ice directly into vapor at the transition, per gram, at the stated temperature. At 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, the table gives ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>An invented 10 g of ice, already at 0 °C, would need 10 × 2834 = 2.834 × 10⁴ J, or 28.34 kJ, to sublime at 0 °C. The mass is invented; the 2834 J/g is Harvey’s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g, and not a first look at melting or at density. Not an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C, which is not taken from a fetched table row this week and is not invented. Not a claim that 2834 − 333.4 is a 100 °C boiling figure.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Harvey’s heading is “Phase Transition Properties,” and the two figures ΔfusH and ΔsublH stand side by side at 0 °C. Sublimation here is ice to vapor, not ice to liquid.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


<p>Last week’s ΔfusH(0 °C) = 333.4 J/g is the energy to melt one gram of ice at 0 °C. The same Harvey/NIST table, under the same phase-transition heading, also gives</p>
<p>ΔsublH(0 °C) = 2834 J/g</p>
<p>at 101.325 kPa. That is the energy needed to turn one gram of ice directly into vapor at 0 °C. It is not the melting figure, and it is not a boiling figure at 100 °C.</p>
<p>For an invented 10 g of ice already at 0 °C, the energy to sublime it at 0 °C is 10 × 2834 = 28,340 J, or 28.34 kJ. The mass is invented; the 2834 J/g is Harvey’s.</p>
<p>2834 / 333.4 ≈ 8.50, so, at 0 °C in this table, subliming 1 g takes about eight and a half times the energy of melting 1 g. That comparison uses two 0 °C figures from the same table. It is not a license to treat 2834 − 333.4 = 2500.6 J/g as an official enthalpy of vaporization at 100 °C. That 100 °C figure was not opened as a definite fetched table row for this week, and it is not invented.</p>
<p class="note">Numerical figures are from A. H. Harvey, “Properties of Ice and Supercooled Water,” a NIST update to a <em>CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics</em> table, at standard atmospheric pressure 101.325 kPa: https://www.nist.gov/publications/properties-ice-and-supercooled-water (PDF: https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=926353).
