## Mathematics · Geometry XLVI: the volume of a right pyramid {#math}

<p>Last week’s right prism had V = B × h. Today is a <strong>right pyramid</strong>, which comes to a point. The height h is perpendicular from the apex to the base.</p>
<p>A classroom cube of edge 6 has volume 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 cubic units. That cube can be divided into three square pyramids, each with a face of the cube as base (B = 36) and height 6. If the three pyramids fill the cube without gaps, each has volume 216 ÷ 3 = 72, which is (1/3) × 36 × 6. So</p>
<p>V = (1/3)Bh.</p>
<p>The same formula is then used for other right pyramids, with B taken from an already-taught area. For a square base 6 by 6 and height 9:</p>

## Physics · Energy in simple harmonic motion: ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX² {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: conservation of mechanical energy for a Hooke’s-law oscillator, from OpenStax 16.5, with last week’s PE_el not retaught as a first look</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-energy-in-simple-harmonic-motion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="energy in simple harmonic motion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear energy in simple harmonic motion">Hear</button> energy in simple harmonic motion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In undamped simple harmonic motion, OpenStax 16.5 writes the conservation of energy as ½mv² + ½kx² = constant. If the motion starts from rest at maximum displacement X, that constant is ½kX², so ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX². At the ends, all the energy is elastic potential; at equilibrium (x = 0), all of it is kinetic.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Last week’s invented classroom spring, k = 200 N/m and X = 0.10 m, stores ½kX² = 1.0 J at the end. At x = 0, if damping is neglected, the kinetic energy is also 1.0 J. Those classroom figures are invented; the conservation statement is OpenStax’s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at PE_el = ½kx², or at ½mv², or at conservation of mechanical energy (Days 21–28 and last week). Not last week’s period T = 2π√(m/k) used as an energy, and not a claim that real springs have no damping.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>OpenStax 16.5: “the energy oscillates back and forth between kinetic and potential, going completely from one to the other as the system oscillates,” if dissipative forces are absent.</dd></div>

<p>½mv² + ½kx² = ½kX²</p>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Baroco is a second-figure syllogism of the form AOO: a universal affirmative premise, a particular negative premise, and a particular negative conclusion, with the middle term as the predicate of both premises. In letters: All P are M; Some S are not M; therefore Some S are not P.</dd></div>

<p>Last week’s Festino was second-figure EIO: No P are M; Some S are M; therefore Some S are not P. Today is the next second-figure mood taught in this course, <strong>Baroco</strong>, whose vowels are A, O, O:</p>
<p>All P are M.</p>
<p>Some S are not M.</p>
<p>Therefore Some S are not P.</p>
<p>The middle term M is still the predicate of both premises. What is new is the universal affirmative about P, and the particular negative about S.</p>
<p>A clean classroom example: All birds have feathers. Some pets do not have feathers. Therefore some pets are not birds.</p>
<p>A near-miss is to write Festino by habit (No P are M first) and still call it Baroco. If the first premise is a universal negative about P, you have Festino, not Baroco. Another near-miss is to write a universal conclusion, “No S are P.” The premises only give that some S are not M, and all P are M, so those S are not P. They do not place every S there.</p>
