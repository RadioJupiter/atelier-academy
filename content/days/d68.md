A **comparative adjective** compares. In Latin, a first look is the pair **-ior** (masculine or feminine nominative singular) and **-ius** (neuter nominative or accusative singular). From *fortis* you form *fortior, fortius*: braver, or stronger.

So *Mīles fortis est* describes one soldier as brave. *Mīles fortior est* describes a soldier as braver (than some other person or thing already in view). *Scūtum fortius est* uses the neuter *fortius* to match *scūtum*.

Original sentences: *Mīles fortior est.* *Scūtum fortius est.* *Mīles nōn fortior est.* *Scūtum nōn fortius est.*

The comparative uses third-declension endings, but it is **not** an i-stem in the way *fortis, -e* is. You do not need the full set of cases today; the job is to recognize -ior and -ius in the nominative singular and to keep agreement.

## Mathematics · Geometry XXXI: a missing side, from a² + b² = c² {#math}

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-missing-side-right-triangle">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="a missing side in a right triangle" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear a missing side in a right triangle">Hear</button> a missing side in a right triangle</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A leg or the hypotenuse of a right triangle, found from the Pythagorean theorem when the other two sides are known. To find a hypotenuse, add the squares of the legs and take the positive square root. To find a leg, subtract the known leg’s square from the hypotenuse’s square, then take the positive square root.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Legs 6 and 8 give hypotenuse 10, because 36 + 64 = 100. Hypotenuse 13 and leg 5 give the other leg 12, because 169 − 25 = 144.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not adding the legs to get the hypotenuse (6 + 8 is not 10’s reason), and not using the theorem on a triangle that has not been shown to be right.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The length is the positive root; a side is not negative. A 6-8-10 triangle is a scaled 3-4-5 triangle, which you may notice after the calculation, but the theorem itself does not require that you remember a list of triples first.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Once a triangle is known to be **right**, the Pythagorean theorem finds a missing side.

To find the **hypotenuse** *c* from legs *a* and *b*: compute *a*² + *b*², then take the positive square root, because *c*² equals that sum and a length is positive.

To find a **leg** *a* from the other leg *b* and the hypotenuse *c*: compute *c*² − *b*², then take the positive square root. You subtract because *a*² = *c*² − *b*².

Watch the arrangement. Adding the two legs does not give the hypotenuse. The theorem adds **squares**.

A 6-8-10 triangle works because 36 + 64 = 100. It is also a scaled 3-4-5 triangle (scale factor 2, from week 13’s similarity work). You may notice that after the calculation. You do not have to memorize a list of triples in order to use the theorem.

If a triangle has not been shown to be right, you may not use this method to invent a third side as if it were a hypotenuse.


</div>

In a right triangle, c² = a² + b². A missing hypotenuse is the positive square root of a² + b². A missing leg is the positive square root of c² minus the other leg’s square. Do not add the legs themselves.

## Physics · Stability, and the base of support {#physics}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a vertical line from the center of mass, tested against the base</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-base-of-support">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="base of support" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear base of support">Hear</button> base of support</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The area on the ground (or other supporting surface) within which a vertical line from the center of mass must fall, if the object is not to tip.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A standing block whose center-of-mass line meets the ground inside its base stays put. If the block is tilted until that line falls outside the base, it tips.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the center of mass itself (which is a point in the object), and not a claim that a wider base always means a lower center of mass. A wider base makes it harder for the line to fall outside, which is a different statement.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Stability here is a geometric test with the center of mass, not a new force law. Classroom tilts and widths are invented and labeled, unless a sourced figure is named.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-stability-against-tipping">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="stability (against tipping)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear stability (against tipping)">Hear</button> stability (against tipping)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An object is stable against tipping while a vertical line from its center of mass falls inside its base of support. It begins to tip when that line reaches the edge of the base.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A taller, narrower object, with a higher center of mass and a smaller base, needs a smaller tilt before the line falls outside the base.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not “heavy things never tip,” and not a confusion with last week’s rotational equilibrium of a lever as a first look at a new law. The test is the line and the base.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Lowering the center of mass, or widening the base, both make a larger tilt necessary before the line leaves the base.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

An object resting on a surface has a **base of support**: the area on that surface inside which a **vertical line from the center of mass** must fall, if the object is not to tip.

If the line meets the ground **inside** the base, the object stays put. If the object is tilted until the line falls **outside** the base, it **tips**. The edge of the base is the last point at which the line can fall and still not tip.

A **wider base** makes it harder for the line to fall outside. A **lower center of mass** does the same, because a larger tilt is then needed to carry the line over the edge. A tall, narrow object is the opposite case.

The widths and tilts in today’s drawings are **invented classroom figures**, labeled as such. The test itself is the line and the base, not a new official measurement.
