## Mathematics · Geometry XVIII: regular polygons, and the interior angle {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: divide a convex polygon into triangles from one vertex, then specialize to the regular case</p>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A polygon with all sides equal and all interior angles equal. The name polygon comes from Greek πολύγωνον (polugōnon), from πολύς (polus), “many,” and γωνία (gōnia), “angle.”</dd></div>

**The sum, for any convex n-gon.** From one vertex, draw diagonals to all non-adjacent vertices. These cut the polygon into **n − 2** triangles. Each triangle has angle sum 180° (Day 6). So the interior angles of a convex n-gon sum to

**(n − 2) × 180°**.

**The regular case.** If the polygon is regular, the n interior angles are equal, so each is

**((n − 2) / n) × 180°**.

Checks you already know: an equilateral triangle (n = 3) gives ((1)/3)×180° = 60°. A square (n = 4) gives ((2)/4)×180° = 90°. A regular hexagon (n = 6) gives ((4)/6)×180° = 120°.

**A first look at the exterior angle.** If one side of a convex polygon is extended, the exterior angle and the interior angle on the same vertex add to 180°. For a regular n-gon, that exterior angle is **360° / n** (because the n exterior angles, one at each vertex, taken all the same way around, sum to 360°). For a regular hexagon, 360°/6 = 60°, and 120° + 60° = 180°, which agrees.

This is a first, Term-1 look at regular polygons (the deeper treatment of polygons and the circle’s area is still planned later). Today’s job is the sum, the one interior angle, and the matching exterior angle.

## Science · The lens changes shape: accommodation {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: a first map of near and far focus, without invented focal lengths or diopter numbers</p>

<h3>Learn</h3>

<article class="term" id="term-accommodation-eye">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="accommodation (eye)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear accommodation (eye)">Hear</button> accommodation (eye)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The change of shape of the eye’s lens that allows a near object, or a far object, to be focused on the retina. When the lens becomes more rounded, the eye is set for a nearer object; when it flattens, for a farther one (in this first look).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A printed page held close asks the lens to accommodate for near work; the horizon asks for a flatter lens. This lesson does not give a measured focal length, or a count in diopters.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a change of the cornea’s place, and not a new retina. The cornea still does much of the bending of light (Day 32); the lens makes a further, adjustable change.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The camera analogy is weaker here: a camera often focuses by moving a lens; the living lens changes shape. The analogy remains a map, not a mechanism we have measured in class.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Last week’s path still holds: cornea, pupil, lens, retina. Today the **lens** is not a fixed piece of glass.

**Accommodation** is the change of the lens’s shape so that a **near** or a **far** object can be focused on the retina. A more rounded lens is for near; a flatter lens is for far, in this first look.

This course will **not** invent a focal length for the eye, or a diopter number, or a measured time for the change. If those figures appear in other books, they are not today’s lesson unless a later, sourced lesson sets them.

The camera analogy, already limited, is weaker again: many cameras focus by moving a lens along a barrel; the eye’s lens changes shape.

## Music theory · Interval inversion, with the octave as the frame {#seminar}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: two intervals that add to an octave, named from intervals already taught</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-interval-inversion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="interval inversion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear interval inversion">Hear</button> interval inversion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The partner of an interval when one of the two notes is moved by an octave, so that the old top note is now below, or the old bottom note is now above. In this first look, the interval numbers add to nine (a third inverts to a sixth, a fifth to a fourth), and a perfect interval inverts to a perfect interval, while a major interval inverts to a minor interval.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>C up to G is a perfect fifth; G up to the next C is a perfect fourth. C up to E is a major third; E up to the next C is a minor sixth. Each pair fills an octave.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a claim that the two intervals sound the same, and not a new meaning of “inverse” from yesterday’s mathematics or from the logic inverse. And not a full table of augmented and diminished inversions today.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 12 already named the perfect fourth, perfect fifth, major third, and perfect octave (and Day 32 returned to the fifth and the major third). Today those intervals are paired by inversion, with the octave as the frame.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-octave-as-frame">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="octave as a frame" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear octave as a frame">Hear</button> octave as a frame</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The octave (from Latin octavus, “eighth”) is the interval of eight letter names, inclusive, for example C to the next C. In this lesson it is the frame that interval inversion fills: a fifth plus the fourth that inverts it make an octave.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin octavus, “eighth.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>C–C′ is a perfect octave. C–G and G–C′ together complete that octave.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a new scale, and not a claim that “eighth” is a count of steps beyond C. The count is inclusive, as already taught.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The word’s origin is a reminder of the eight letter names, not a new counting rule.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The frame.** An **octave** (C to the next C) has eight letter names, counted inclusively, as Day 12 taught. The word comes from Latin *octavus*, “eighth.”

**Inversion, in this first look.** Keep one note, and move the other by an octave so the pair is still the same two letter names, now in the other order:

- A **fifth** inverts to a **fourth** (5 + 4 = 9). Perfect stays perfect: C–G (P5) inverts to G–C (P4).
- A **third** inverts to a **sixth** (3 + 6 = 9). Major inverts to minor: C–E (M3) inverts to E–C (m6).

The numbers add to **nine** because the two counts share an endpoint and together fill the eight names of the octave. This lesson does not build a full table of every quality. It teaches these pairs, and the two quality rules above, on intervals you already can name.

**A word caution.** “Inversion” here is a music term. It is not f⁻¹, and not the logic inverse of a conditional.
