## Mathematics · Algebra I: identities and factoring {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Gelfand & Shen, <em>Algebra</em> (identities proved and used, not memorized as tricks)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-identity">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="identity" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear identity">Hear</button> identity</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An equality that is true for every allowed value of its letters, and that must be proved by expanding or by using a known identity.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Late Latin identitās, from idem (‘the same’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b² holds for all a and b.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>(a + b)² = a² + b² is not an identity: the middle term 2ab is missing, so it fails as soon as a and b are not zero.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Used forwards, an identity computes (51 × 49 = 50² − 1). Used backwards, it factors.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-factor">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="factor" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear factor">Hear</button> factor</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>To write an expression as a product; or one of those pieces. Identities used backwards are the usual method.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin factor (‘doer, maker’), from facere (‘to make’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>x³ − 8 = (x − 2)(x² + 2x + 4).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Factoring is not ‘guessing a number that divides the constant term and hoping’ without a reason that the product matches.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This is the same identity as in the definition, read from right to left.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A rectangular diagram (a geometric 'area model') can show why (*a*+*b*)² splits into *a*², *b*² and two rectangles *ab*. You must be able to prove each identity by multiplying out:

- (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
- a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
- a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) and a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
- (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³

**Used forwards, they compute; used backwards, they factor.** 51 × 49 = (50 + 1)(50 − 1) = 2500 − 1 = 2499. 103² = 10000 + 600 + 9 = 10609. Conversely, x² − 10x + 25 = (x − 5)².

**Worked example (Gelfand style).** Prove that the product of four consecutive integers, plus 1, is a perfect square.
Pair the outer and inner factors: n(n + 3) = n² + 3n and (n + 1)(n + 2) = n² + 3n + 2. Let m = n² + 3n + 1. The product is (m − 1)(m + 1) = m² − 1, so adding 1 gives m² = (n² + 3n + 1)². ∎ (Check: 1·2·3·4 + 1 = 25 = 5², and n = 1 gives m = 5.)

</div>

An **appositive** is a noun phrase set beside another noun to rename it: *Leonardo, the draughtsman, …*. A **subordinate clause** cannot stand alone as a sentence; it starts with a word such as *because*, *when*, *although*, *if*, or *who*. Both are tools for packing information into one controlled sentence rather than a string of short ones.

**Appositive.** A noun phrase placed beside a noun to rename it, set off by commas: *Gérôme, **a French painter and sculptor**, collaborated with Bargue on the drawing course.* Appositives pack identifying information into one sentence and are a mark of mature prose.

**Subordinating conjunctions** begin a dependent clause that cannot stand alone: *although, because, since, when, while, if, unless, after, before, until, whereas.* Starting with the dependent clause forces precision about the relationship:

> **Although** Latin has no native speakers today, it is still read daily by scholars.
> **Unless** the block-in is accurate, shading will only make the errors more visible.

**Sentence combining.** Joining short sentences into one well-built sentence trains you to choose the relationship deliberately.

## Physics · Acceleration and velocity–time graphs {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Peer Instruction protocol (answer → confidence → argue another option → re-answer → key)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-acceleration">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="acceleration" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear acceleration">Hear</button> acceleration <span class="symbol">a<sub>avg</sub> = Δv / Δt</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The rate of change of velocity: how quickly velocity increases, decreases, or changes direction.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin accelerātiō, from accelerāre (‘to hasten’); first attested in English in 1531.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A cart going from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 3 s has average acceleration 2 m/s². On a velocity–time graph, acceleration is the slope.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Acceleration is not ‘going fast’, and a negative acceleration is not a different kind of quantity from a; it is acceleration in the negative direction (or a slowing down, if velocity was positive).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Velocity (Day 1) is already a rate: change of position. Acceleration is the next rate: change of velocity.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-velocity-time-graph">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="velocity time graph" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear velocity time graph">Hear</button> velocity–time graph</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A graph with time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis. The slope is acceleration; the area between the graph and the time axis is displacement.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A horizontal line at v = 5 m/s for 4 s means zero acceleration and a displacement of 20 m.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not a position–time graph. A straight line sloping down on a v–t graph is constant (nonzero) acceleration, not ‘slowing only if the line is curved’.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 1 used the slope of a position–time graph as velocity. The same slope idea now reads acceleration.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Velocity** is the rate of change of position. **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: how quickly the velocity increases or decreases, including a change of direction. Average acceleration is Δ*v* / Δ*t*. On a velocity–time graph, the slope is acceleration, and the area under the graph (for the interval) is the displacement.

**Pre-reading.**
- **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / Δt (m/s²). It is a vector. An object slowing down has acceleration opposite to its velocity.
- On a **velocity–time graph**, the slope is the acceleration and the area between the graph and the time axis is the displacement (area below the axis counts as negative).
- With constant acceleration: v = v₀ + at; x = v₀t + ½at²; v² = v₀² + 2ax.
- Near Earth's surface, a freely falling object (ignoring air resistance) accelerates downward at g ≈ 9.8 m/s², whether it is going up, at the top or coming down.

**Context.** Hammurabi ruled Babylon in the 18th century BCE (the Louvre dates his reign 1792–1750 BCE). Near the end of his reign his laws were inscribed on a black stone stele about 2.25 m tall, crowned by a relief of the king standing before the sun god Shamash, god of justice. Some 600 years later the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte carried the stele off to Susa as war booty. French excavators found it there in 1901–02, and it is now in the Louvre. Several columns near the bottom had been erased in antiquity; some of the missing laws are known from other copies.

**The source (sections 196–199, Johns's translation):**

> 196. If a man has caused the loss of a gentleman's eye, his eye one shall cause to be lost.
> 197. If he has shattered a gentleman's limb, one shall shatter his limb.
> 198. If he has caused a poor man to lose his eye or shattered a poor man's limb, he shall pay one mina of silver.
> 199. If he has caused the loss of the eye of a gentleman's servant or has shattered the limb of a gentleman's servant, he shall pay half his price.

(Modern translators often render Johns's "gentleman" as *awīlum*, a free man of full status, and "poor man" as *muškēnum*, a lower class of free person. "Servant" here means a slave, whose "price" is his value.)

**Formal analysis** describes *what is there* before interpreting it: **line** (contour, direction, weight); **shape** and **mass**; **value** (light and dark); **color**; **texture**; **space** (overlap, scale, perspective); **composition** (how parts are arranged, where the eye goes). Describe precisely, then infer.

**The first images.** Painted caves in France and Spain preserve some of the oldest known figurative art. The paintings at Chauvet Cave (France) are more than 30,000 years old; those at Lascaux are about 17,000 years old. Animals dominate: horses, aurochs, deer, bison. Artists used the natural bulges of the rock to suggest the body's volume, drew in charcoal and mineral pigments (iron oxides for reds and yellows, manganese or charcoal for blacks), and sometimes blew pigment through a tube to make outlines of hands.
