A **trapezoid**, in the American sense used here, has **exactly one pair of parallel sides** (the bases). The **height** is the perpendicular distance between those bases.

Its area is the average of the two bases times the height:

**A = ½(a + b) × h.**

Classroom lengths: bases 6 and 10, height 4. A = ½(6 + 10) × 4 = ½ × 16 × 4 = **32**.

Why the average? Place a second copy of the same trapezoid so the two non-parallel sides join; the two copies make a parallelogram of base a + b and height h, area (a + b)h. One trapezoid is half of that, ½(a + b)h.

If the two bases were equal, the shape would be a parallelogram, not a trapezoid in this course’s usage, and the formula would reduce to b × h, which matches Monday.

The **analytical essay** (Day 9) is not a first look today. What is new is a **multi-paragraph outline**: a plan, written before the draft, that extends the **single-paragraph outline** (Day 6) across the whole short essay.

The outline has:

1. A **thesis** — one sentence that can be argued against (Day 9).
2. A plan for **each paragraph**: a claim (as a topic sentence would), and the grounds or short quotations it will use.
3. A note on the **other side**, or on a limit of the claim, so the essay does not pretend there is nothing to answer.

Use a public-domain line already in this course, not a new unsourced story. A fair model, using Longfellow’s Inferno I (already read in weeks 13–15):

- Thesis: Virgil’s offer to be a guide is a rescue from the beast, not a casual walk.
- Paragraph 1: the speaker has turned back from the beast (grounds: the speech just before the Greyhound passage, or the earlier turn).
- Paragraph 2: the offer itself, ‘I will be thy guide’ (lines 112–114, already taught).
- Other side: the same speech also promises ‘desperate lamentations,’ so the rescue is not a promise of ease.

Do not support the thesis with circular reasoning or with a post hoc jump. Do not draft the full essay yet; tomorrow writes from the outline.

A **simple pendulum** is a bob on a light string of length **L**. For a **small swing** — OpenStax, *College Physics 2e*, 16.4, says amplitudes less than about **15°** — the period is

**T = 2π√(L/g).**

The same section states that the period depends on the **length** and on **g**, and is **independent of mass**.

Use the already-taught Earth figure **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²**, not a new official measurement. For **L = 1.00 m**, T = 2π√(1/9.8). √(1/9.8) is √0.1020…, about 0.319, and 2π times that is **about 2.0 s**. That is a calculation from the formula and from g, not a certificate that a particular classroom bob was timed at exactly 2 s.

If you double the length, T becomes √2 times as long, because T goes with the square root of L. Changing the mass of the bob, in this model, does not change T.

Classroom lengths other than this 1.00 m example are invented and labeled invented unless you actually measure a string and say so. Never invent an official g to more digits than this course has already taught.
