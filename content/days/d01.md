## Mathematics · Modeling: from bar model to equation {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Singapore model method (part-whole, comparison and change models), then translation into algebra</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-bar-model">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="bar model" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear bar model">Hear</button> bar model</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A drawing of quantities as bars of equal units, so the relationship is visible before any equation is written.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French, ultimately a diminutive of Latin modulus (‘measure, standard’), itself from modus (‘measure’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Five equal units in a bar stand for fifths of a whole; three of them can be marked ‘sold’.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not a graph of a function, and it is not a decoration added after the algebra is finished.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This lesson uses the bar first, then the equation. Later lessons keep the same habit whenever a relationship has to be read.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-remainder">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="remainder" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear remainder">Hear</button> remainder</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>What is left of a quantity after a part of it has been taken. In a fraction-of-a-remainder problem, the next fraction is of this leftover amount, not of the original whole.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English, from Anglo-Norman remaindre, an infinitive used as a noun.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If 3/5 of the prints are sold in the morning, the remainder is 2/5 of the whole. ‘1/4 of the remainder’ means 1/4 of that 2/5.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not ‘whatever number is left when you subtract two parts in a simple part-whole bar’ if those parts were not taken in sequence from what was left.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The bar model makes the remainder a visible piece of the whole, which is then cut again.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-ratio">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ratio" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ratio">Hear</button> ratio</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A comparison of two quantities by units of the same size, written a : b. One quantity in a ratio can stay unchanged while the other changes.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Latin ratiō (a doublet of ration and reason).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Money in the ratio 5 : 3, after the first spends some, may become 2 : 3; the second amount is still 3 units.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A ratio is not a single fraction of a remainder, and it is not ‘the difference between two amounts’.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Like the bar model, a ratio is drawn in equal units so a change in one bar is easy to see.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Why start here.** Most errors in applied problems happen before any algebra: the relationship between quantities is misread. The Singapore model method draws each quantity as a bar so that the relationship is visible. Three structures cover almost every problem: **part-whole** (parts make a whole), **comparison** (one quantity is a multiple of, or differs from, another) and **change** (a quantity is increased, decreased or transferred). Draw first; write the equation second; check the answer in the original words third.

**Worked example 1 (part-whole with fractions of a remainder).** A gallery sold 3/5 of its prints in the morning and 1/4 of the remaining prints in the afternoon. 48 prints were left. How many were there at the start?

The remainder is 2/5 of the whole. A quarter of it was sold, so 3/4 of 2/5 = 3/10 of the whole is left. Then 3/10 of the total = 48, so the total is 160. *Algebra:* (3/10)x = 48, x = 160. *Check:* 160 → 96 sold, 64 left → 16 sold, 48 left. ✓

**Worked example 2 (comparison with one quantity unchanged).** Two artists had money in the ratio 5 : 3. After the first spent $84, the ratio was 2 : 3. How much did each have at first?

The second artist's amount does not change, so keep it as 3 units in both ratios. The first artist goes from 5 units to 2 units, so 3 units = $84, and 1 unit = $28. First artist: $140; second: $84. *Algebra:* (5u − 84)/3u = 2/3 gives 15u − 252 = 6u, so u = 28. The bar model finds the "unchanged quantity" trick that the algebra hides.

## Writing & Rhetoric · The sentence: kernels and expansion {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Hochman, <em>The Writing Revolution</em> (sentence-level instruction first, practiced on real content)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-kernel">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="kernel" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear kernel">Hear</button> kernel</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A short, complete sentence that can later be expanded: a subject and a verb, with nothing added yet.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Old English cyrnel, a diminutive of corn (‘grain’); equivalent to corn + -el. Related to the everyday word corn in the sense ‘grain’.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The painter looks. That is a kernel. ‘At dawn the painter looks out of the high window because the light has just reached the street’ is an expansion of it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A kernel is not a fragment, and it is not already a paragraph. ‘Because the light has just reached the street’ is not a kernel; it cannot stand alone.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Every later expansion in this course starts from a kernel, then answers when, where, why, and how.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-expansion">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="expansion" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear expansion">Hear</button> expansion</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The deliberate adding of information to a kernel by answering a short list of questions (when, where, why, how), without losing the original verb.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Kernel: The painter looks. When? at dawn. Where? out of the high window. Why? because the light has just reached the street.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Expansion is not ‘writing a longer sentence at random’, and it is not adding so much that the original kernel disappears.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The kernel is the stem. Expansion is how a controlled sentence, and later a paragraph, is built.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Principle.** Good paragraphs are built from controlled sentences, and controlled sentences are built deliberately. The Writing Revolution begins at the sentence: you take a bare **kernel** (subject + verb, sometimes an object) and expand it by answering the questions *who/what, when, where, why, how*. Each addition must be accurate and must earn its place.

**Worked example.** Kernel: *The mural faded.*
- when: *over four centuries*
- where: *on the damp north wall of the refectory*
- why: *because the painter had applied tempera to dry plaster*

Expanded: *Over four centuries, the mural on the damp north wall of the refectory faded, because the painter had applied tempera to dry plaster rather than pigment to wet plaster.* (Notice that the "why" is a causal claim, so it must be true. A real case: Leonardo painted the *Last Supper* in tempera and oil on a dry wall instead of in true fresco, and the paint began flaking within decades.)

**Because / but / so.** The same stem finished three ways forces three kinds of thinking: *because* gives a reason, *but* gives a contrast, *so* gives a consequence.

> Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, **because** canvas resisted the city's damp better than wall plaster.
> Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, **but** fresco remained common elsewhere in Italy.
> Venetian painters favoured oil on canvas, **so** large paintings could be rolled and shipped.

</div>

Position is where something is, measured from a chosen origin along a line or axis (it can be positive or negative). **Displacement** is the change in position: final minus initial. **Purpose of the course.** This year runs chronologically from the first written records to the modern world. You will read what people at the time actually wrote (primary sources) and judge later accounts (secondary sources) against them.

**Primary and secondary.** A *primary source* was produced at the time by someone involved: a law code, a letter, a coin, an inscription, a painting. A *secondary source* interprets the past afterwards: a textbook, a biography, a documentary. The same document can be either, depending on the question: a 1920s history of Rome is secondary for Rome but primary for how the 1920s saw Rome.

**Four questions for every source (sourcing).**
1. *Who* made it, and what position were they in?
2. *When and where*, and how close to the events?
3. *For whom*, and *why*: to inform, persuade, command, flatter, remember?
4. *What does it assume* that the author did not need to say?

Then **corroborate** (does another independent source agree?) and **contextualize** (what else was happening?).

**Dating.** BCE/CE and BC/AD count the same years; there is no year zero (1 BCE is followed by 1 CE). "c." (*circa*) marks an approximate date. Dates for the ancient Near East depend on which chronology scholars use, so they can differ by decades between books.

## Atelier · Setting up, sight-size and line control {#atelier}

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sight-size" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sight-size">Hear</button> sight-size</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A way of setting up so that, from a fixed viewing point, the drawing and the subject appear the same size, and lengths can be compared with a straight arm and a pencil.</dd></div>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="plumb line" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear plumb line">Hear</button> plumb line</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A weighted string, or a pencil held up as a vertical, used to check which points in the subject line up one above the other.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From plumb (‘lead weight’), from Latin plumbum (‘lead’).</dd></div>

Sight-size means the drawing is set so that, from a fixed viewing point, the drawing and the subject appear the same size. You can then compare lengths with a straight arm and a pencil, or with a plumb line. Line control starts with slow, light, straight or slightly curved strokes that you can adjust, not with a heavy outline you cannot erase cleanly.

**The atelier sequence.** Classical ateliers train the eye and hand in a fixed order: line and proportion (copying the Bargue plates), then value (light and shadow) on simple forms, then cast drawing from plaster, then painting in limited and full palettes, then the live model. Each stage removes one difficulty at a time. Speed comes later; accuracy comes first.

**Materials.** Graphite pencils 2H, HB, 2B; a kneaded eraser; smooth heavy drawing paper (Bristol or similar); a sandpaper block or blade for a long, sharp point; a plumb line (thread with a small weight); a thin straight stick or knitting needle for measuring; a hand mirror; masking tape; a board and an easel or a chair-back that holds the board upright.

**Sight-size.** Set the drawing beside the subject so that, from one fixed viewing spot a few paces back, the drawing and the subject *appear the same size*. Mark the spot on the floor. Make every judgment from that spot, then walk forward to draw. Because the two are seen side by side at the same size, errors of proportion and angle show up directly. **Vowels.** Spanish has five vowel sounds, and each is pure and short: *a* (casa), *e* (mesa), *i* (sí), *o* (todo), *u* (luna). They never glide as English vowels do.

## Logic · Propositions and truth tables {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Logic (Mondays)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-proposition">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="proposition" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear proposition">Hear</button> proposition</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A sentence that is either true or false (not a command, not a question).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Old French, from Latin prōpositiō, from prōpōnere (‘to set forth’), from pōnere (‘to place’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘Rome is in Italy’ is a proposition. ‘Shut the door’ is not.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A proposition is not a question, and it is not ‘a true sentence only’. False sentences can still be propositions.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Connectives will join propositions. If you do not start with a genuine proposition, a truth table cannot be written.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-conditional">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="conditional" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear conditional">Hear</button> conditional <span class="symbol">p → q</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The ‘if p then q’ connective. It is false only when p is true and q is false. It is true on a sunny day if the claim was ‘if it rains, the street is wet’.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin condiciōnālis, from condiciō (‘agreement, condition’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘If a shape is a square, then it has four sides’ is a true conditional. Its converse is not automatically true.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The conditional is not a claim that p has happened, and it is not false just because p is false.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>From a conditional come the converse, the inverse, and the contrapositive. Only the contrapositive always shares the truth value of the original.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-contrapositive">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="contrapositive" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear contrapositive">Hear</button> contrapositive <span class="symbol">¬q → ¬p</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The statement formed by swapping and negating both sides of a conditional. It always has the same truth value as the original.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The contrapositive of ‘If a shape is a square, then it has four sides’ is ‘If a shape does not have four sides, then it is not a square.’</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The contrapositive is not the converse (q → p) and not the inverse (¬p → ¬q). Those two may be false even when the original is true.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The truth table for p → q and for ¬q → ¬p is the same, which is why this move is safe in a proof.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


A **proposition** is a statement that is either true or false. "Rome is in Italy" is a proposition; "Shut the door" is not. Propositions combine with **connectives**:

| symbol | read | true when |
|---|---|---|
| ¬p | not p | p is false |
| p ∧ q | p and q | both are true |
| p ∨ q | p or q | at least one is true (inclusive "or") |
| p → q | if p then q | in every case *except* p true and q false |
| p ↔ q | p if and only if q | p and q have the same truth value |

The conditional surprises people: "If it rains, the street is wet" is not proven false on a dry, sunny day. It is false only when it rains *and* the street stays dry.

From p → q come three related statements: **converse** q → p, **inverse** ¬p → ¬q, **contrapositive** ¬q → ¬p. Only the contrapositive always has the same truth value as the original.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A proposition is true or false. Connectives: ¬, ∧, ∨ (inclusive), → (false only when p is true and q is false), ↔. From p → q: converse q → p, inverse ¬p → ¬q, contrapositive ¬q → ¬p. Only the contrapositive always matches the original.

<p>Key terms: proposition, conditional, contrapositive.</p>
