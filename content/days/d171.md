<p>Last week taught result clauses with ut and with ut nōn, and mixed them with already-taught purpose clauses. Today tightens that mix. No new construction. A purpose clause marks an intended purpose: ut, or nē when negative. A result clause marks what happens as a result, often with tam, ita, or sic: ut, or ut nōn when negative. The verb is the present subjunctive, not the future. Original classroom sentences, not copied from Ørberg. Identity is handled with Puer and Puella, and with he and she in the English gloss.</p>

<p>Last week taught when a function is continuous at a point. Today’s first look is a new increment: the difference quotient. For a function f, at a number a, the difference quotient is [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h, for an increment h not equal to 0. The quotient is not defined at h = 0, because the denominator would be 0. On the invented function f(x) = x², at a = 3, f(3) = 9, and f(3 + h) = (3 + h)² = 9 + 6h + h². So [f(3 + h) − f(3)] / h = (6h + h²) / h = 6 + h, for h ≠ 0. If h is 0.1, the quotient is 6.1; if h is −0.1, the quotient is 5.9. Those values sit near 6, but the limit as h approaches 0 is tomorrow’s job, not today’s. The function and the chosen numbers are invented classroom examples, not official data. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. A derivative remains reserved until tomorrow.</p>
