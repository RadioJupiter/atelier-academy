Two already-taught algebra methods, gathered. They are not new today.

**Composition.** (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f to that result. Iteration is composition of a function with itself: f(f(x)), also written (f ∘ f)(x). It is not the product f(x) · f(x). The inverse, already taught as a different job, undoes f.

**A line and a parabola, as a system.** Substitute the line into the parabola (or the other way when it is easier). Solve the resulting equation. Then **check** each candidate in both original equations. A point that fits only one equation is not a solution of the system.

**A worked system, in this course’s already-taught shape.** y = x² and y = x + 2. Then x² = x + 2, so x² − x − 2 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0, x = 2 or x = −1. The points are (2, 4) and (−1, 1). Check: 2² = 4 and 2 + 2 = 4; (−1)² = 1 and −1 + 2 = 1. Both hold.

Keep this picture separate from a quadratic *inequality* (which asks where a graph is above or below the axis) and from III.36.

Today is a close of that method. Every mass in the exercises is a **labeled invented** classroom number, not a measured object. The values of g are the ones already sourced: Earth **9.8**, the Moon **about 1.6**, Mars **about 3.7**, each in m/s².

We will not invent a g, and we will not invent an orbital speed.
