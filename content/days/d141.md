## Physics · The Doppler effect, a first look, from OpenStax 17.4 {#physics}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: OpenStax 17.4; f_obs for a moving source and for a moving observer; official Example 17.4 waits</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-the-doppler-effect">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="the Doppler effect" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear the Doppler effect">Hear</button> the Doppler effect</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The Doppler effect, in OpenStax 17.4, is an alteration in the observed frequency of a sound due to motion of either the source or the observer. The actual change in frequency due to that relative motion is called a Doppler shift. Relative motion of source and observer toward one another increases the received frequency; relative motion apart decreases it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>For a stationary observer and a moving source, f_obs = f_s × v_w / (v_w ± v_s), with the minus sign for motion toward the observer and the plus sign for motion away. For a stationary source and a moving observer, f_obs = f_s × (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w, with the plus sign for motion toward the source and the minus sign for motion away. Official Example 17.4 waits until Wednesday.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a change in the speed of sound in the medium, which remains v_w whether the source is moving or not. Not last week’s decibel, which is a level of intensity, not a shift of frequency. Not a sonic boom, which waits. Not an official measured frequency of a real train not in the book’s example.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s sound, speed of sound, and frequency. The effect is named for Christian Johann Doppler (1803–1853), as OpenStax 17.4 states.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>OpenStax 17.4, “Doppler Effect and Sonic Booms,” at https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms, defines the Doppler effect as an alteration in the observed frequency of a sound due to motion of either the source or the observer. The actual change in frequency due to that relative motion is called a Doppler shift. The sound still moves in the medium at the already-taught speed v_w, whether the source is moving or not; v_w = fλ remains. If the source moves, compressions are closer together ahead of the source and farther apart behind it, so a stationary observer ahead hears a higher frequency, and a stationary observer behind hears a lower one. If the observer moves toward a stationary source, the observer passes through more crests in the same time and hears a higher frequency; motion away lowers the frequency. In general, relative motion toward one another increases the received frequency, and relative motion apart decreases it.</p><p>For a stationary observer and a moving source, f_obs = f_s × v_w / (v_w ± v_s), where f_s is the source frequency, v_s is the speed of the source along the line joining source and observer, and v_w is the speed of sound. The minus sign is used for motion toward the observer, and the plus sign for motion away. For a stationary source and a moving observer, f_obs = f_s × (v_w ± v_obs) / v_w, where v_obs is the speed of the observer along that line; the plus sign is for motion toward the source, and the minus sign for motion away. Official Example 17.4, a 150 Hz train horn, waits until Wednesday. Sonic booms wait until Friday. Do not invent an official frequency of a real vehicle not in the book.</p>

## Seminar · Logic: the addition rule for overlapping events, a first look {#seminar}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: invented fair cube; overlap subtracted; distinguished from mutual exclusivity and from independence</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-the-addition-rule-for-overlapping-events">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="the addition rule for overlapping events" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear the addition rule for overlapping events">Hear</button> the addition rule for overlapping events</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>When two events can both happen, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The last term is subtracted so the overlap is not counted twice. This is not last week’s product rule for independent events, and it is not the earlier addition rule that was used only when events were mutually exclusive.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On an invented fair cube, P(even or greater than 3) = P(even) + P(greater than 3) − P(even and greater than 3) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 2/3. The even faces are 2, 4, and 6; the faces greater than 3 are 4, 5, and 6; the overlap is 4 and 6. The cube is an invented classroom figure, not an official experiment.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not P(A) + P(B) with the overlap left in, which would give 1 on this invented cube, a miss already noted in an earlier week. Not P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) used in place of today’s subtraction, unless independence has been separately established and the question is “and.” Not an official probability of a real event.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week’s independent events, and the earlier mutually exclusive addition rule. Official probabilities of real events remain reserved.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>
<p>When two events were mutually exclusive, they could not both happen, and P(A or B) was P(A) + P(B). Last week, independent events could both happen, and P(A and B) was P(A) × P(B). Today the “or” rule is completed for events that can overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The last term is subtracted so the outcomes that belong to both are not counted twice.</p><p>On an invented fair cube, let A be “even” and B be “greater than 3.” P(A) = 3/6, because 2, 4, and 6 are even. P(B) = 3/6, because 4, 5, and 6 are greater than 3. P(A and B) = 2/6, because 4 and 6 are both even and greater than 3. So P(A or B) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 = 2/3. The faces that satisfy the “or” are 2, 4, 5, and 6, four faces out of six, which agrees. Adding without the subtraction would give 1, which is a miss: 2 would be counted once, but 4 and 6 would be counted twice, and 1 would be left out of the “or” while the total was wrongly filled. The cube and the events are invented classroom figures, not official probabilities of a real event.</p><p>If two events are mutually exclusive, P(A and B) is 0, and today’s rule reduces to the earlier addition rule. Today’s subtraction is not last week’s product rule, and the product must not be used in place of P(A and B) unless independence has been separately established.</p>
