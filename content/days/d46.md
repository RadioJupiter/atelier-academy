## Mathematics · Algebra XV: iterating a function {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: composition already taught, now used as f(f(x)); invented rules stay labeled.</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-iteration-of-a-function">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="iteration of a function" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear iteration of a function">Hear</button> iteration of a function</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Applying the same function more than once: f(f(x)) is f applied to the result of f(x). It is composition of a function with itself, not multiplication of f by itself.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin iterātiō, from iterō, “to do something for a second time, repeat,” from iterum, “again” (English Wiktionary, entries “iteration” and “iterate”).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(f(x)) = 2(2x + 1) + 1 = 4x + 3. So f(f(3)) = 15.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not f times f, and not the inverse f⁻¹ unless the lesson asks for an inverse. f(f(x)) is also written (f ∘ f)(x).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 41’s composition: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Today the two functions are the same function.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Last week, composition meant (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), with g applied first. Today the two functions may be the same function: **iteration** is applying f more than once.

**f(f(x))** means: first find f(x), then put that result into f again. The same idea is (f ∘ f)(x). It is not f(x) · f(x), and it is not the inverse unless a problem asks for the inverse.

**A worked function.** Let f(x) = 2x + 1, an invented classroom rule, labeled as invented.

- f(3) = 2 · 3 + 1 = 7.
- f(f(3)) = f(7) = 2 · 7 + 1 = 15.
- As a formula: f(f(x)) = 2(2x + 1) + 1 = 4x + 3, and 4 · 3 + 3 = 15, the same value.

You already know how to compose a function with its inverse: (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x. That is a different job from iterating f.

## Physics · g from G M / R², with sourced Earth figures {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: F = G M m / R² and F = m g, then a sourced computation. No invented orbital speed.</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-g-from-gm-over-r-squared">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="g from G M / R²" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear g from G M / R²">Hear</button> g from G M / R²</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Near a sphere, the gravitational field g is G M / R², because F = G M m / R² and F = m g, so the m cancels. g is not the same symbol as G.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Using this course's G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², and the NASA Earth Fact Sheet mass 5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg with equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, the computation gives about 9.79 m/s², limited by the three significant figures in G.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a new measurement of G, and not a claim that 9.8 N/kg is exact at every place on Earth. The fact sheet itself lists more than one surface figure.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 36's F = G M m / r², and the already-used working value g = 9.8 N/kg near the Earth.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

Newton's law, already taught, says the force between two masses is **F = G M m / r²**. Near the Earth, we have also written the weight as **F = m g**, with this course's working value **g = 9.8 N/kg** (or 9.8 m/s²). If both describe the same force on a mass m at the surface, then

**g = G M / R²**.

G is still the constant 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², a standard value, not a classroom measurement. M and R must come from a named source.

**Sourced figures.** NASA's Earth Fact Sheet lists mass **5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg** and equatorial radius **6,378.137 km**. The same sheet also lists a polar radius 6,356.752 km and a volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km, so Earth is **not a perfect sphere**. Today's computation uses the equatorial radius, treated as a sphere of that radius, and labels that choice.

**The arithmetic**, with G kept at three significant figures:

- R = 6,378.137 km = 6.378137 × 10⁶ m.
- G M = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹)(5.9722 × 10²⁴) = 3.9834574 × 10¹⁴.
- R² = 4.0680638 × 10¹³.
- g = 3.9834574 × 10¹⁴ / 4.0680638 × 10¹³ ≈ **9.79 m/s²**.

The extra digits in M and R cannot make the result more precise than G's three significant figures, so we write **about 9.79 m/s²**, not a longer string of digits.

**The fact sheet's own surface figures**, which we do not pretend to have measured: surface gravity (mean) **9.820 m/s²**; surface acceleration (equator) **9.780 m/s²**. This course's working value **9.8 N/kg** is a rounded near-Earth figure, not "the" exact number at every place. The difference between the sheet's two listed figures is noted, not explained by an invented classroom mechanism.

No invented orbital speed.

## Logic · Modus ponens and modus tollens {#seminar}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: two valid forms, set beside last week's two invalid forms, with the square-and-rectangle contrast.</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-modus-ponens">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="modus ponens" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear modus ponens">Hear</button> modus ponens</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A valid form: If P, then Q; P; therefore Q. From a conditional and its “if” side, you may infer the “then” side. It is not affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P), which was taught as invalid last week.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin modus ponendo ponens, roughly “mode where affirming affirms” (English Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle. This figure is a square. Therefore it is a rectangle.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not affirming the consequent, which uses the “then” side as a reason for the “if” side, and is invalid. A non-square rectangle is the already-taught counterexample to that invalid form, not to modus ponens.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week's affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent, now set beside two valid forms.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-modus-tollens">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="modus tollens" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear modus tollens">Hear</button> modus tollens</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A valid form: If P, then Q; not Q; therefore not P. From a conditional and the denial of its “then” side, you may infer the denial of its “if” side. It is not denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q), which was taught as invalid last week.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin modus tollendo tollens, “the mode where the denying denies,” from modus (“mode”) and forms of tollere, “to take away” (English Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle. This figure is not a rectangle. Therefore it is not a square.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not denying the antecedent, which uses “not P” as a reason for “not Q,” and is invalid. A non-square rectangle is a rectangle and not a square, so “not a square” does not give “not a rectangle.”</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week's denying the antecedent, and today's modus ponens.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


Last week taught two **invalid** forms: affirming the consequent (If P then Q; Q; therefore P) and denying the antecedent (If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q). Today names two **valid** forms that are easily confused with those two.

**Modus ponens.** If P, then Q. P. Therefore Q. You affirm the “if” side, and you may take the “then” side.

**Modus tollens.** If P, then Q. Not Q. Therefore not P. You deny the “then” side, and you may take the denial of the “if” side.

The already-taught square-and-rectangle pair still helps:

- Valid (ponens): If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is a square. Therefore it is a rectangle.
- Valid (tollens): If it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It is not a rectangle. Therefore it is not a square.
- Invalid (affirming the consequent): It is a rectangle; therefore it is a square. A non-square rectangle blocks that.
- Invalid (denying the antecedent): It is not a square; therefore it is not a rectangle. The same non-square rectangle blocks that.
