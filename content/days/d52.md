From a point **P** outside a circle, a **tangent** touches the circle at exactly one point, **T**. A **secant** from the same point cuts the circle at two points: **A**, the nearer, and **B**, the farther.

**Euclid III.36 (the tangent-secant theorem).** PT² = PA × PB.

**A numerical check.** If PA = 4 and PB = 9, then PA × PB = 36, so PT² = 36, and the length **PT = 6**. We take the positive square root because PT is a length.

**Beside intersecting chords.** Geometry XIII (Day 24) used Euclid III.35: if two chords meet inside a circle, each meeting-point splits its chord into two segments whose products are equal. Today’s product is the same *kind* of relation — a product of two lengths along a line through P — but P is *outside*, one line is a tangent, and the tangent’s two “segments” are the same length PT, so the product is a square.

**What we will not do.** We will not invent a tangent length, and we will not treat III.36 as a replacement for III.35. The two theorems have different pictures: inside the circle, and outside it.

Week 10 used the National Eye Institute page “Refractive Errors” for myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and presbyopia. That page also names **eyeglasses** and **contact lenses** as ways of correcting refractive errors. (Surgery is listed by NEI as well; this course still does not teach a surgical method.)

**What a lens can do, in this course’s words.** A refractive error is a problem of focus: the image is not formed cleanly on the retina. A corrective lens changes the path of the light *before* it enters the eye’s own optics, so the image can fall where it should. That is a tool. It does not rewrite week 10’s definitions, and it does not “cure” the eye in the sense of making the eye’s own optics different unless some later sourced lesson says so — and today no such claim is made.

**What this sitting will not do.** We will not invent a diopter (a unit of lens strength this course has not taught). We will not diagnose a classroom partner, or treat a Tuesday blur as myopia. We will not prescribe a lens. If a real question about someone’s sight arises, it belongs to an eye-care professional, not to this lesson.

Day 27 taught the primary triads I, IV, and V in a major key, and a first **authentic cadence**: V moves to I, a close. That lesson reserved other cadence types, including the half cadence. Today is that reserved type, only.

**A half cadence** ends on **V**. The phrase pauses on the dominant and does not close to the tonic. In C major, a short phrase that goes I–V, or IV–V, and stops there, is a half cadence. It sounds like a comma or a question, not like a finished sentence.

**The contrast.** V–I can end (an authentic cadence, already taught). A stop on V is a pause, not an end. We will not begin a deceptive cadence (a later reserved type) today, and we will not treat every popular or folk stop as a school half cadence.
