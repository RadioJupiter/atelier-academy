## Latin · The pluperfect of a deponent, a first look {#latin}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: a first look at locūtus eram, contrasted with the already-taught perfect and imperfect</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-pluperfect-of-a-deponent">

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The pluperfect of a deponent uses the already-taught perfect participle with the imperfect of sum, and it still looks passive and is translated as active: locūtus eram, locūtus erās, locūtus erat, locūtī erāmus, locūtī erātis, locūtī erant. </dl></article>

<p>Last term the deponent had three taught tenses: loquor, I speak; loquēbar, I was speaking or I used to speak; and locūtus sum, I spoke or I have spoken. Today a first look at a fourth: the pluperfect. The same participle locūtus is used, but the helping verb is the already-taught imperfect of sum: eram, erās, erat, erāmus, erātis, erant. Locūtus eram means I had spoken: the speaking is already completed before some other past moment. The forms still look passive and are translated as active, as with the earlier deponent tenses.</p>

<p>Term 3 writing begins with the classical progymnasmata, a sequence of graded exercises. The first is the fable. A fable is a short invented narrative, often with animals that act, that ends in a stated moral. Today the set text is George Fyler Townsend’s 1887 wording of “The Crow and the Pitcher,” from <em>Three Hundred Æsop’s Fables</em>, as posted on Wikisource. The wording is:</p>
<blockquote><p>A Crow perishing with thirst saw a pitcher, and, hoping to find water, flew to it with great delight. When he reached it, he discovered to his grief that it contained so little water that he could not possibly get at it. He tried everything he could think of to reach the water, but all his efforts were in vain. At last he collected as many stones as he could carry, and dropped them one by one with his beak into the pitcher, until he brought the water within his reach, and thus saved his life. Necessity is the mother of invention.</p></blockquote>
<p>The action is short: thirst, a pitcher with too little water, failed efforts, stones dropped one by one, the water brought within reach, the life saved. The moral is stated after the action, not inside it. The Wikisource page appears to lack a stop after “delight”; the stop is restored here as ordinary punctuation, not as a change of wording. Do not add an official natural-history claim about crows, and do not treat the moral as a law of physics.</p>

## Physics · Superposition, and constructive and destructive interference, a first look {#physics}
<p class="meta">40 min · Method: OpenStax 16.10, amplitudes add; two pure cases, in phase and out of phase</p>
<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>
<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-superposition">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="superposition" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear superposition">Hear</button> superposition</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>When two or more waves arrive at the same point, their disturbances are superimposed: the resulting disturbance is the sum of the individual disturbances. If the disturbances are along the same line, the amplitudes add, with sign.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From French superposition, from Late Latin superpositio, from superponere, to place over (Etymonline; Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Two identical waves that arrive in phase, crest with crest, produce a wave of twice the amplitude and the same wavelength, which OpenStax 16.10 calls pure constructive interference.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a replacement of one wave by the other, and not a claim that energy is created. Not the reserved first look at beats, and not an official speed of sound or of light invented today.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last term’s wave, wavelength, and wave velocity, from OpenStax 16.9. Constructive and destructive interference are the two pure cases of this sum.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-constructive-interference">
