## Mathematics · Two-week problem set: constructions to tangents, and a first look at progressions {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: mixed practice at the level of this week's worked examples; a short introduction to progressions</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-arithmetic-progression">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="arithmetic progression" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear arithmetic progression">Hear</button> arithmetic progression</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by adding the same fixed number, the common difference d. The nth term is a + (n − 1)d, where a is the first term.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Arithmetic is from Middle English arsmetike, from Old French, from Latin arithmētica, from Ancient Greek ἀριθμητική (‘(art of) counting’), from ἀριθμός (‘number’). Progression is from Middle English progressioun, from Old French and Latin prōgressiō; in English, progress + -ion.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>5, 9, 13, 17, … has d = 4. The 8th term is 5 + 7 × 4 = 33.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>An arithmetic progression is not a list that merely ‘goes up’. If the difference changes, it is not arithmetic. It is also not a geometric progression, where you multiply by a constant each time.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The same idea as a linear function (Day 9): the terms sit on a straight line when you plot them against the term number.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-geometric-progression">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="geometric progression" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear geometric progression">Hear</button> geometric progression</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by multiplying by the same fixed number, the common ratio r. The nth term is a rⁿ⁻¹, where a is the first term.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Late Latin geometricus, from Ancient Greek γεωμετρικός, from γεωμετρία (‘geometry’). Here the name is historical: such a sequence was studied alongside geometric figures.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>3, 6, 12, 24, … has r = 2. The 5th term is 3 × 2⁴ = 48.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A geometric progression is not a list of geometry measurements, and it is not arithmetic: 3, 6, 12 does not have a constant difference (the differences are 3, then 6).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The common ratio is the same habit as yesterday's exponents: multiplying by r, n − 1 times, is multiplication by rⁿ⁻¹.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

<article class="term" id="term-two-paragraph-essay">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="two-paragraph essay" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear two-paragraph essay">Hear</button> two-paragraph essay</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A complete short argument with one thesis, two paragraphs (each with a topic sentence and support), at least one introduced and explained quotation or a precise précis of a source, a real concession or a limit, and a concluding sentence that returns to the thesis in new words.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not two unconnected paragraphs, not a summary of a lesson, and not a page of quotations. Length is not the test; the jobs on the list are the test.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The piece uses the week’s skills: combining and transitions (Day 11), a legal semicolon if needed (Day 13), quotation or précis (Days 15 and 18), and the outline with a concession (Days 16 and 19).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-revision-pass">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="revision pass" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear revision pass">Hear</button> revision pass</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A second writing, after the draft is complete, in which you check a short list — thesis, topic sentences, evidence, attribution, concession or limit, concluding sentence, and punctuation of joins — and change the sentences that fail, rather than recopying the whole for neatness.</dd></div>

Write the two-paragraph essay from your revised outline. Then run the list.

**The list.** (1) An arguable thesis in the first or second sentence. (2) Two topic sentences, with a reason for the order. (3) Support in each paragraph (examples, a short attributed quotation, or a précis of a source). (4) Every quotation introduced and explained. (5) A real concession or a precise limit. (6) A concluding sentence in new words. (7) Legal joins: no comma splices; semicolons used only between complete sentences.

**Subject.** Use the history, literature, science, or Latin question you outlined on Day 16, or this prompt: *A primary source is not self-interpreting. Show this from two documents you have actually read in the last four weeks.*

## Physics · Two-week check: friction to momentum {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Mixed check at the level of this week’s worked examples</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-impulse">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="impulse" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear impulse">Hear</button> impulse <span class="symbol">J = Δp</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The product of a net force and the time it acts, equal to the change in momentum: J = F_net Δt = Δp, when the force is constant (or using an average force). The SI unit is N·s, which is the same as kg·m/s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Borrowed from Middle French impulser, from Latin impulsus.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 1 kg cart going at 6 m/s is stopped in 0.3 s: Δp = −6 kg·m/s, so the average force is −20 N, and the impulse is −6 N·s.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Impulse is not the same as a force (it includes time), and it is not the same as energy. A small force over a long time can deliver the same impulse as a large force over a short time.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s F = Δp/Δt is this definition, rearranged. Today it is named so that a mixed check can use the word exactly.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-independence-of-components">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="independence of components" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear independence of components">Hear</button> independence of components</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The rule, used for projectiles, that the horizontal motion (constant v_x, if air is neglected) and the vertical motion (acceleration g) can be solved separately, and that a change in one component does not by itself change the other.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>From a given height, a faster horizontal throw has a larger range but the same time of flight.</dd></div>

## Elective · Poetry 2: caesura, enjambment, and the volta {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: terms taught, then close reading of a public-domain sonnet · After Day 10 (meter and iambic pentameter)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-caesura-enjambment">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="caesura and enjambment" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear caesura and enjambment">Hear</button> caesura and enjambment</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A caesura is a pause inside a line of verse, often (but not only) in the middle, and often marked by punctuation. Enjambment is the running-on of a sentence or clause across the end of a line, without a pause at the line break.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Caesura is from Latin caesūra (‘a cutting’), from caesus, the perfect participle of caedere (‘to cut’). Enjambment is borrowed from French enjambement.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In Shakespeare’s Sonnet 73, the thought of the hanging leaves runs from ‘When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang / Upon those boughs…’ across the line end: that is enjambment. ‘Bare ruined choirs, where late the sweet birds sang’ can show a mid-line pause after ‘choirs’ if the text is punctuated that way — a caesura.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A caesura is not a stanza break, and enjambment is not ‘the poet forgot to punctuate’. Both are tools for controlling breath and sense against the metrical line you already scanned on Day 10.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Iambic pentameter is still the meter of the sonnet you will read. These terms describe how the sentence sits on that meter.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-volta">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="volta" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear volta">Hear</button> volta</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The turn in a sonnet: the place where the argument or the figure shifts. In a Petrarchan sonnet this often comes after the first eight lines. In a Shakespearean sonnet (abab cdcd efef gg) it is often felt at the closing couplet, and sometimes earlier, around line 9. Look for a ‘but’, a ‘yet’, or a change of thought; do not treat a line number as a superstition.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Italian volta (‘turn’). The English poetic sense is a turning point in a poem, most often a sonnet.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In Sonnet 18, many readers feel a turn at ‘But thy eternal summer shall not fade’ (line 9), when the poem stops listing summer’s faults and starts claiming that the beloved’s summer will not fade, in verse.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A volta is not a change of typography, and it is not automatically ‘line 9 in every sonnet’. If you cannot hear a turn, say so, and point to what the poem does instead.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 10’s meter tells you how the line is built. The volta tells you how the sonnet’s thought is built.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Day 10 taught **iambic pentameter** (five iambs, a weak-strong pattern, with license). Today adds how a **sentence** and an **argument** sit on those lines.

**Caesura.** A pause inside the line. Read the line aloud and mark where you have to stop, even briefly. Punctuation often helps; it is not the only clue.

**Enjambment.** The sense runs over the line end. The line is still a line (the meter still turns there); the sentence does not.

**The sonnet and the volta.** A **Shakespearean sonnet** has fourteen lines, usually iambic pentameter, rhyming **abab cdcd efef gg**. The **volta** is the turn of thought. In **Sonnet 18** (public domain), the first lines compare the beloved to a summer’s day and then list summer’s faults. Line 9 begins: “But thy eternal summer shall not fade.” That *but* is a candidate for the turn: the poem moves from comparison and decay to a claim that verse will keep the summer from fading.

Quote only a short phrase, and attribute it: Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, line 9, or the line you actually use. You may use a printed or a Project Gutenberg text; lineation should follow a standard text.
