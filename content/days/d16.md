## Mathematics · Algebra V: the laws of exponents {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Gelfand, <em>Algebra</em>, exponents as repeated multiplication, then the laws</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-exponent">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="exponent" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear exponent">Hear</button> exponent <span class="symbol">a<sup>n</sup></span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The small number that says how many times a base is used as a factor: in aⁿ, a is the base and n is the exponent. For a positive integer n, aⁿ = a · a · … · a (n factors).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin expōnēns, the present participle of expōnō (‘to set out, to explain’), from ex- (‘out’) + pōnō (‘to place’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. The exponent is 4; the base is 2.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The exponent is not the same as the base, and a² is not ‘a times 2’. Also, (ab)ⁿ is not a·bⁿ unless a = 1 or n = 1.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Tomorrow's radicals undo even exponents: if x ≥ 0, (√x)² = x and √(x²) = x.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-power">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="power" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear power">Hear</button> power</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The result of raising a base to an exponent, or the expression aⁿ itself. The laws of powers are: aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0), (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ, (ab)ⁿ = aⁿ bⁿ, and a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0), a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>2³ · 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128, and 2⁷ / 2³ = 2⁴ = 16. Also 5⁰ = 1 and 2⁻³ = 1/8.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A power is not addition: 2³ + 2⁴ is not 2⁷. You can add exponents only when you multiply powers of the *same* base.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The laws are identities (Day 3): they hold for every allowed base, and they must be proved from the meaning, not memorized as tricks.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

For a positive integer *n*, the power *aⁿ* means *n* factors of *a*. The **laws** then follow by counting factors.

- *aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ* (m factors, then n more, is m + n factors).
- *(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ* (n groups of m factors).
- *aᵐ / aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ* when *a* ≠ 0 and *m* ≥ *n* (n factors cancel). The same pattern suggests the definitions *a⁰ = 1* (*a* ≠ 0) and *a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ* (*a* ≠ 0), so the law still holds when the exponent is zero or negative.
- *(ab)ⁿ = aⁿ bⁿ*.

**Do not** add the exponents when you add the powers: 2³ + 2⁴ = 8 + 16 = 24, not 2⁷. The laws apply when you *multiply* or *divide* powers of the same base, or raise a power to a power.

</div>

## Writing & Rhetoric · From one paragraph to two: the two-paragraph outline {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, expanding the single-paragraph outline</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-two-paragraph-outline">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="two-paragraph outline" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear two-paragraph outline">Hear</button> two-paragraph outline</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A short plan for a piece that has two paragraphs, each with its own topic sentence and supporting points, under one overall thesis. It is the single-paragraph outline (Day 6) used twice, plus a sentence that says how the two paragraphs work together.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Thesis: the Egyptian–Hittite treaty should be read as a pair of royal self-presentations, not as a neutral record. ¶1: the Hattusa tablet. ¶2: the Karnak inscription. Each paragraph lists two or three points, not new theses.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not two separate short essays, and it is not one long paragraph cut in half. If the second paragraph could be first without any loss, the outline has not yet said why the order exists.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 6’s SPO is still the unit inside each paragraph. The new skill is the job of the second paragraph relative to the first.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-concluding-sentence">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="concluding sentence" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear concluding sentence">Hear</button> concluding sentence</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The last sentence of a paragraph or of a short essay that does not merely repeat the topic sentence, but closes the thought — often by stating a consequence, a limit, or a return to the thesis in new words.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>After evidence about both copies of the treaty: ‘Read together, the two versions show what each court needed the other to accept.’</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A concluding sentence is not ‘In conclusion, as I said…’ and it is not a new claim that would need its own paragraph.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>In a two-paragraph piece, the last sentence of paragraph two should close the thesis, not only the second topic sentence.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A two-paragraph piece has **one thesis**, two topic sentences, and a final concluding sentence.

**The outline.** Write: (1) the thesis, in one sentence that can be argued with; (2) the topic sentence of paragraph 1, plus two or three supporting points as phrases; (3) the topic sentence of paragraph 2, plus its points; (4) a concluding sentence that touches the thesis again in new words.

**The test of order.** Paragraph 2 should do work that paragraph 1 cannot do alone: a second source, a contrast, a consequence, or a limit. If you can swap the two paragraphs and nothing changes, rewrite the topic sentences until the order has a reason.

Today, build an outline only. The essay itself can wait until Day 20; a first draft may be written later in the week as a Franklin or quotation exercise needs it.

## Physics · Uniform circular motion and centripetal force {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Peer Instruction (Mazur) adapted for one student</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-centripetal-acceleration">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="centripetal acceleration" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear centripetal acceleration">Hear</button> centripetal acceleration <span class="symbol">a = v²/r</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The inward, center-seeking acceleration of a body that moves at constant speed on a circle, of size a = v²/r (or a = 4π²r/T², where T is the period). The velocity’s direction is changing even when the speed is not, so there is an acceleration.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From New Latin centripetālis, coined by Isaac Newton, from Latin centrum (‘center’) + petere (‘to seek, to aim at’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A body moving at 10 m/s on a circle of radius 5 m has a = 10²/5 = 20 m/s², toward the center.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Centripetal acceleration is not ‘a force that appears in circular motion’, and it is not outward. The word names the acceleration; the force that produces it has a separate name and a real source (tension, friction, gravity).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 1: velocity can change by changing direction. Day 3: acceleration is the change of velocity. Today that change points toward the center.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-centripetal-force">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="centripetal force" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear centripetal force">Hear</button> centripetal force <span class="symbol">F = mv²/r</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The net force toward the center that produces the centripetal acceleration: F_net, inward = m v²/r. It is not a new kind of force; it is the net inward force, supplied by whatever is actually pushing or pulling inward.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If m = 2 kg, v = 10 m/s, r = 5 m, then F_net inward = 2 × 20 = 40 N. That 40 N might be the tension in a string, or friction, or a component of a normal force.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>There is no outward ‘centrifugal force’ acting on the moving body in an inertial frame. The feeling of being ‘thrown out’ is the body’s inertia (Day 6) trying to go in a straight line.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Newton’s second law still reads F_net = ma, with a = v²/r toward the center. Do not add an extra force to ‘balance’ the motion.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A body can move at **constant speed** on a circle. Its direction is always changing, so it is accelerating. The acceleration points **toward the center** and has size

*a = v²/r*.

If *T* is the time of one lap (the period), then *v* = 2π*r*/*T* and *a* = 4π²*r*/*T*².

**The force is not a new type.** *F<sub>net</sub>* toward the center is *m v²/r*, and it is supplied by a real force or a sum of real forces: a string’s tension, friction at the tires, gravity in an orbit. In an inertial frame there is no outward force on the body; inertia would have carried it in a straight line (Day 6).

**A clean example.** *v* = 10 m/s, *r* = 5 m, *m* = 2 kg: *a* = 20 m/s², *F<sub>net</sub>* = 40 N inward.

## Atelier · Combining solids: a small still life {#atelier}

**Order of work.**
1. The large group: one enclosing shape, the height of the tallest solid, the width of the group.
2. Each solid constructed (cube planes, cylinder axis and ellipses, sphere contour), including light construction of hidden parts where overlap needs it.
3. A check of alignments and of shared table-line or box they all sit on.
4. Value: first the largest families (the group in light, the group in shadow, the table), then **relative value** between objects, then a controlled hatch or plane tone, and the inner shadows (sphere on cube, and so on).

A **reflexive** verb has an object that is the same person as the subject. The infinitive carries **-se**: *lavarse*, *levantarse*, *vestirse*, *llamarse*, *irse*, *acostarse*, *despertarse*.

**The pronouns.** me, te, se, nos, os, se — standing before the conjugated verb: *Me lavo. Te lavas. Se lava. Nos lavamos. Os laváis. Se lavan.*

**Stem changes still apply.** *Despertarse* (e → ie): *me despierto, nos despertamos.* *Acostarse* (o → ue): *me acuesto, nos acostamos.* Do not invent a new stem change for a reflexive; use only the patterns already taught.

**Body parts.** *Me lavo las manos. Se lava la cara.* The article, not *mis/su*.

**Placement with an infinitive.** *Voy a levantarme* or *Me voy a levantar.*

## Logic · Necessary and sufficient conditions {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: explicit definitions, then translation of ‘only if’ and ‘if and only if’ · After implication (Day 1) and De Morgan (Day 11)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sufficient-condition">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sufficient condition" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sufficient condition">Hear</button> sufficient condition</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A condition that is enough to guarantee the result: if P is sufficient for Q, then P → Q. Whenever P holds, Q holds.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Being equilateral is sufficient for a triangle to be isosceles: if it is equilateral, then it is isosceles.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Sufficient is not the same as necessary. A condition can be enough without being the only way. A triangle can be isosceles without being equilateral.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>In symbols, ‘P is sufficient for Q’ is P → Q, the same arrow you have used since Day 1.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-necessary-condition">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="necessary condition" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear necessary condition">Hear</button> necessary condition</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A condition that must hold if the result holds: if P is necessary for Q, then Q → P (equivalently, ¬P → ¬Q). Without P, you cannot have Q.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Being a quadrilateral is necessary for being a square: if a figure is a square, then it is a quadrilateral. If it is not a quadrilateral, it is not a square.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Necessary is not the same as sufficient. Being a quadrilateral is not enough to make a square.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>‘Q only if P’ is another English form of Q → P: Q only if P means P is necessary for Q.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Implication can be read as a pair of everyday words: **sufficient** and **necessary**.

**Sufficient.** P is sufficient for Q when P is enough to give Q: P → Q. ‘If P, then Q.’

**Necessary.** P is necessary for Q when Q cannot hold without P: Q → P. Equivalently, ¬P → ¬Q. English also says ‘Q only if P’.

**Both: if and only if.** P is necessary *and* sufficient for Q when (P → Q) and (Q → P). We write P ⇔ Q, the same equivalence idea as Day 11, now in the language of conditions.

**A test.** Ask two questions. (1) If I have P, am I guaranteed Q? If yes, P is sufficient for Q. (2) If I lack P, am I guaranteed to lack Q? If yes, P is necessary for Q.

**A trap.** ‘Only if’ is not ‘if’. ‘A figure is a square only if it is a rectangle’ means: if it is a square, then it is a rectangle. It does not mean that every rectangle is a square.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

P sufficient for Q means P → Q. P necessary for Q means Q → P (‘Q only if P’). Both together: P ⇔ Q. ‘Only if’ is not ‘if’.


<p>Key terms: sufficient condition, necessary condition.</p>
