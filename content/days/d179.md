</div>
<p>This is the last mathematics review before Friday’s examination. No new rule. A function is continuous at a when it is defined at a and the limit equals the value. Invented f(x) = 2x is continuous at 3, because f(3) = 6 equals the limit. Invented (x² − 4)/(x − 2) is not continuous at 2, even though the limit is 4, because it is not defined there. The derivative of invented f(x) = x² is found from the difference quotient [ (x + h)² − x² ] / h = 2x + h, for h ≠ 0; the limit as h approaches 0 is f'(x) = 2x. So f'(3) = 6. A formal epsilon argument remains reserved. Derivatives of other functions remain reserved. The functions are invented.</p>

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Continuous at a means defined at a, with the limit equal to the value. For invented f(x) = x², f'(x) = 2x, and f'(3) = 6. Continuity and a derivative remain different jobs.
