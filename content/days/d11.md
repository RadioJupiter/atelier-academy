**The new case.** The **ablative** singular of the second declension ends in *−ō* (*hortō*, *ātriō*, *cubiculō*); of the first, in *−ā* (*vīllā*). The plural, for all three genders, ends in *−īs* (*hortīs*, *vīllīs*, *cubiculīs*).

**Three prepositions today.** *in* + ablative = in/on (place where); *ex* + ablative = out of; *ab* + ablative = away from. The book uses the forms *ex* and *ab* (not yet *ē* and *ā*).

Original sentences, not from the book: *Mārcus est in hortō.* *Iūlia est in vīllā.* *Puer ex hortō venit.*

A **construction** uses only an unmarked straightedge and a compass. You do not read a length in centimeters off a ruler and you do not trust a freehand right angle. Each step is justified by a definition, an axiom, or a theorem already proved.

**To copy a segment.** Given AB, draw a ray from a new point A′. Open the compass from A to B and, with the point at A′, mark A′B′ equal to AB.

**To copy an angle.** Given ∠BAC, draw a ray A′D. Swing an arc centered at A that cuts both sides of the angle, at P and Q. With the same opening, swing an arc centered at A′ that cuts A′D at P′. Open the compass from P to Q and, with the point at P′, mark Q′ on the new arc. The ray A′Q′ makes ∠Q′A′P′ equal to ∠BAC (SSS on the two small triangles).

**The perpendicular bisector of AB.** With opening more than half of AB, swing an arc centered at A and an arc centered at B, so that they meet at two points, C and D. The line CD is the perpendicular bisector of AB.

**Why it works (locus).** C is drawn so that CA = CB, and likewise CD's other point D. So C and D both lie on the locus of points equidistant from A and B, which Day 8 already named: that locus is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Then CD is that line, and it meets AB at its midpoint, at right angles.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Sentence combining and transitions {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, sentence combining, then planned transitions</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sentence-combining">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sentence combining" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sentence combining">Hear</button> sentence combining</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The skill of joining two or more short, complete sentences into one sentence that keeps all the meaning and is easier to follow, using a conjunction, a relative clause, an appositive, or a joining punctuation mark.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The fresco is damaged. It is still readable. → The fresco is damaged, but it is still readable. Or: The fresco, which is damaged, is still readable.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Combining is not gluing sentences with a comma alone (a comma splice), and it is not leaving out a fact in order to make the sentence short.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Days 1–3 already gave because/but/so, appositives, and subordinating conjunctions. Today those tools are used on purpose, not one at a time as drills.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-transition">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="transition" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear transition">Hear</button> transition</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A word or short phrase that shows the relation between two sentences or two parts of a sentence: addition, contrast, cause, result, or sequence (for example, however, therefore, next, for instance).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French transition, from Latin trānsitiō; in English, transit + -ion.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The treaty is early. It is not the only evidence. → The treaty is early; however, it is not the only evidence.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A transition is not a decoration added at the start of every sentence, and ‘however’ is not a synonym of ‘and’. The relation has to be real.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Tomorrow’s copywork will show how a strong model chooses a transition rather than repeating the same conjunction.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Two short sentences are often clearer after they become one, if the joining word tells the real relation.

**Combine with a purpose.** *Because / but / so* remain the first tools (Day 1). A relative clause or an appositive (Day 3) can tuck a fact beside a noun. A semicolon plus a conjunctive adverb (*however, therefore, meanwhile*) is for two closely related complete sentences; that mark will be named carefully on Day 13.

**Transitions between sentences.** After a sentence has ended, the next sentence may need a clear relation: *for example, however, therefore, next, in the same way.* Choose the word that matches the thought, or use none if the relation is already obvious.

**A test.** If you can delete the transition and the two sentences still seem to have the same relation, the transition was only decoration. If the relation disappears or reverses, the transition was doing work.

</div>

<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle French friction and from Latin frictiō (‘a rubbing’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If N = 49 N and μ<sub>k</sub> = 0.2, then f<sub>k</sub> = 9.8 N, opposite the slide. If μ<sub>s</sub> = 0.4, a horizontal pull of 15 N, which is less than 19.6 N, need not start the block moving.</dd></div>

A surface pushes back **perpendicular** to itself: that push is the **normal force** *N*. On a level table, with no vertical acceleration, *N* equals the weight *mg*.

**Friction** is the force that resists sliding, along the surface. While the object slides, kinetic friction is often well modeled as *f<sub>k</sub> = μ<sub>k</sub> N*, opposite the velocity along the surface. Before it starts to slide, static friction matches the sideways push, up to *μ<sub>s</sub> N*, and then gives way.

The coefficients *μ<sub>s</sub>* and *μ<sub>k</sub>* are measured ratios, not explanations. Typically *μ<sub>s</sub>* is a little larger than *μ<sub>k</sub>*. Always draw the forces before writing *F<sub>net</sub> = ma*.

**A clean example, with *g* = 9.8 m/s².** A 5 kg block is on a level surface, so *N* = 49 N. If *μ<sub>k</sub>* = 0.2, then *f<sub>k</sub>* = 9.8 N. A horizontal pull of 19.8 N gives *F<sub>net</sub>* = 19.8 − 9.8 = 10 N, so *a* = 10/5 = 2.0 m/s².

## Atelier · The cube: planes and foreshortening {#atelier}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: sight-size or a fixed viewing point; one main light · After the egg (Day 6) and the first cast</p>

<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A flat face of a form, or a flattened patch used to understand a curve. A cube has six square planes; a drawing of the cube starts by finding those planes and the angles between them, not by rounding the corners early.</dd></div>

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="foreshortening" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear foreshortening">Hear</button> foreshortening</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The apparent shortening of a length or a shape that recedes from you. A square plane of the cube that turns away no longer looks square on the page; a side that comes toward you looks shorter than you know it is.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The top of a cube below eye level appears as a flattened shape, not a true square. You measure the shape you see, from a fixed viewing point, rather than the square you know is there.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Foreshortening is not a trick of style, and it is not an excuse to guess. It is the same proportion skill as Day 2, applied to a length that points toward or away from you.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Sight-size (Day 1) and a still viewing point make the foreshortened shape comparable with a pencil at arm’s length.</dd></div>

A **cube** is the first faceted solid. It teaches **planes** and **foreshortening** before the cylinder and the sphere.

**Set-up.** One cube (wood or paper, large enough to see), one main light, a fixed viewing point, the same sight-size habit as Week 1. Do not move the light mid-drawing.

**Construction, not decoration.** First, the overall block: the height, width, and the angles of the front edges. Then the receding edges. A receding square is **foreshortened**: you measure the shape on the picture plane, not the shape you know. Check parallels: edges that are parallel on the cube should converge only as perspective requires, and they should not wander independently.

**Light on planes.** Each visible plane gets one family of value, nearly even, with a clear change at the edge. A cube is not an egg: do not melt the edges into a curve. Reserve a highlight only if a plane actually catches a glare; most matte cubes simply have a light plane, a mid plane, and a shadow plane, plus a cast shadow on the table.

**A common error.** Drawing the top as a full square ‘because I know it is square’, or rounding the corners to make it ‘look nice’. Both flatten or destroy the form.

Timed block-in first (10 minutes), then a measured correction, then the three plane values.

<article class="term" id="term-stem-changing-e-ie">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="stem-changing verb (e → ie)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear stem-changing verb (e → ie)">Hear</button> stem-changing verb (e → ie)</h5>

Some present-tense verbs change the **stem** in the forms that stress that stem. Today: **e → ie**.

**The pattern.** The change happens in *yo, tú, él/ella/usted, ellos/ellas/ustedes*. It does **not** happen in *nosotros* or *vosotros*, where the stress has moved onto the ending.

**pensar** (to think): pienso, piensas, piensa, **pensamos**, **pensáis**, piensan.

**querer** (to want): quiero, quieres, quiere, **queremos**, **queréis**, quieren.

**preferir** (to prefer): prefiero, prefieres, prefiere, **preferimos**, **preferís**, prefieren.

**empezar** (to begin) is the same: empiezo, empiezas, empieza, empezamos, empezáis, empiezan.

## Logic · De Morgan’s laws {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: explicit rule, then truth tables · After Day 1’s propositions and Day 6’s validity</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-de-morgan">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="De Morgan’s laws" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear De Morgan’s laws">Hear</button> De Morgan’s laws <span class="symbol">¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q; ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two equivalences that move a negation through ‘and’ and ‘or’, and switch the connective: not (P and Q) is equivalent to (not P) or (not Q); not (P or Q) is equivalent to (not P) and (not Q).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>‘It is not the case that the figure is both a square and a rhombus that fails to be a square’ is messy. Clearer: ‘either it is not a square, or it is not a (non-square) rhombus’ — but the clean work is on symbols: ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>De Morgan does not say that ‘not P and Q’ is the same as ‘not (P and Q)’. Parentheses matter: ¬P ∧ Q is a different claim from ¬(P ∧ Q).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 1’s truth tables are the test. An equivalence holds when the two sides have the same truth value in every row.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-logical-equivalence">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="logical equivalence" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear logical equivalence">Hear</button> logical equivalence</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two statements that have the same truth value in every possible case. We write ⇔ or ‘is equivalent to’ only after the tables (or a proof) show that match.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>P and ¬¬P are equivalent: double negation does not change the value. ¬(P ∧ Q) and ¬P ∨ ¬Q are equivalent by De Morgan’s first law.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Equivalence is not ‘sounds similar in English’, and it is not the same as ‘one implies the other’ in a single direction (Day 6’s validity can hold one way without equivalence).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>If even one row differs, the two sides are not equivalent.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A negation in front of a whole conjunction or disjunction can be rewritten by **pushing the negation in** and **switching** ∧ and ∨.

**The two laws** (named for the nineteenth-century logician Augustus De Morgan; the pattern is older than the name):

1. ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q. To deny both at once is to say that at least one is false.
2. ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q. To deny an ‘or’ is to deny each part.

**Why parentheses matter.** ¬P ∧ Q means: P is false, and Q is true. That is not the same as ¬(P ∧ Q), which is true whenever at least one of P or Q is false.

**How we check.** Build a truth table with columns for P, Q, the inner compound, the negated inner compound, and the proposed right-hand side. The two sides are equivalent only if those columns match in every row.

English is slippery (‘not both’, ‘neither…nor’). Translate into symbols first, then apply the law.

</div>
