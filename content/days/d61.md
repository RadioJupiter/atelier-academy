<p>Capitulum XI turns to the human body, and with it a useful pattern in the third family. <strong>Neuter</strong> nouns such as <em>corpus</em> and <em>caput</em> still form the genitive singular in <em>-is</em>, so the stem is still read there: <em>corpor-</em>, <em>capit-</em>.</p>
<p>What is new is the neuter rule. In the singular, the nominative and the accusative are the same form: <em>corpus</em> can be the subject or the object. In the plural, those two cases both end in <em>-a</em>: <em>corpora</em>, <em>capita</em>. Do not reach for a made-up accusative in <em>-em</em>.</p>
<p>Original sentences, not lines copied from the book: <em>Corpus magnum est.</em> <em>Puella caput videt.</em> <em>Puer corpus sānum habet.</em> <em>Capita puerōrum parva sunt.</em></p>

## Mathematics · Geometry XXV: similar triangles {#math}

<p class="meta">40 min · Method: a picture of two same-shape triangles, then equal angles and proportional sides</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-similar-triangles">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="similar triangles" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear similar triangles">Hear</button> similar triangles</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Two triangles are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are proportional — they are the same shape, not necessarily the same size. We write △ABC ∼ △DEF when angle A = angle D, angle B = angle E, angle C = angle F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From French similaire, from Medieval Latin similaris, from Latin similis, “like,” akin to simul, “together” (English Wiktionary, entry “similar”).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A triangle with sides 3, 5, and 6 is similar to a triangle with sides 6, 10, and 12: each side of the second is twice the corresponding side of the first, and the angles match.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not congruence. Congruent triangles (△ABC ≅ △DEF) can be placed exactly on one another; similar triangles may differ in size. Equal angles alone, without a check of which angles correspond, is not yet a similarity statement.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Congruence, corresponding parts, and the angle sum of a triangle were taught in Term 1. Today those ideas are used for same-shape triangles whose sides stay in one ratio.</dd></div>

<p>Two triangles can be the same shape even when they are not the same size. That is <strong>similarity</strong>. Corresponding angles are equal, and corresponding sides are <strong>proportional</strong>: they stand in one constant ratio, the scale factor.</p>
<p>If △ABC ∼ △DEF, then ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, ∠C = ∠F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD. The letters tell you which vertex matches which. A scale factor of 2, as in the figure, means every side of the larger triangle is twice the matching side of the smaller one: 3 maps to 6, 5 to 10, and 6 to 12.</p>
<p>Congruence is the stricter matching you already know: same shape and same size. Similar triangles need only the same shape. Do not write ≅ when you only have ∼.</p>

## Writing & Rhetoric · The Toulmin model: claim, grounds, and warrant {#writing}

## Physics · Rotational equilibrium of a rigid body {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: the already-taught torque, then Στ = 0 and ΣF = 0 for a body at rest</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-rotational-equilibrium">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="rotational equilibrium" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear rotational equilibrium">Hear</button> rotational equilibrium</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A rigid body is in rotational equilibrium about a point or an axis when the sum of the torques about that point is zero (Στ = 0): the clockwise torques and the counterclockwise torques balance, so there is no net tendency to start turning. A body that remains at rest also has no net force (ΣF = 0).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin aequilibrium, from aequus, “equal,” and lībra, “balance” (English Wiktionary, entry “equilibrium”).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A light rod, 2.00 m long, is supported at its center. A 30 N force downward at the left end is balanced by a 30 N force downward at the right end: each torque about the center is 30 N × 1.00 m = 30 N·m, in opposite senses, so Στ = 0. The support then supplies 60 N upward, so ΣF = 0 as well.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the already-taught market equilibrium of supply and demand. Not a claim that one force of zero is enough, or that torques may be added without naming the point they are taken about. The unit of each torque is still the newton-meter (N·m), not the joule.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 33 taught torque as τ = F × d⊥, in N·m, and already hinted that equal-and-opposite torques can balance. Today that hint becomes a condition: Στ = 0, and, for a body at rest, ΣF = 0 as well.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

<p>Day 33 treated torque as a first look: τ = F × d⊥, measured in newton-meters, not joules. A force can turn a body about a point; a force through the point gives no torque.</p>
<p>If a rigid body does not start to turn, the torques about that point add to zero. That is <strong>rotational equilibrium</strong>: Στ = 0. Choose a sense (clockwise or counterclockwise) and treat the other sense as the opposite sign, so that equal torques in opposite senses cancel.</p>
<p>If the body also remains at rest, the forces themselves must balance: ΣF = 0. A support can supply the upward force that makes the vertical forces add to zero, even while the torques are balanced about the same support.</p>
<p>A labeled invented example. A light rod, 2.00 m long, is supported at its center. 30 N acts downward at the left end, 1.00 m from the support, and 30 N downward at the right end, also 1.00 m from the support. Each of those forces gives a torque of 30 N × 1.00 m = 30 N·m, in opposite senses, so Στ = 0. The support supplies 60 N upward, so 30 N + 30 N downward and 60 N upward give ΣF = 0. These numbers are invented classroom figures, not a measurement from a named machine.</p>

## Logic · Celarent, and the major and minor terms {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: a second valid first-figure mood, beside the already-taught Barbara</p>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In a syllogism, the major term (P) is the predicate of the conclusion, and the minor term (S) is the subject of the conclusion. The middle term (M), already taught, links them in the premises and does not appear in the conclusion.</dd></div>

<p>Day 31 taught the syllogism and one valid first-figure mood, <strong>Barbara</strong>: All M are P; All S are M; therefore All S are P. The middle term M linked the other two and did not appear in the conclusion. Today adds a second valid mood in the same figure: <strong>Celarent</strong>.</p>
<p>The vowels in the name record the pattern: E, A, E. That means: No M are P; All S are M; therefore No S are P. The figure is still the first figure: M is the subject of the first premise and the predicate of the second.</p>
<p>The <strong>major term</strong> (P) is the predicate of the conclusion; the <strong>minor term</strong> (S) is the subject of the conclusion; the middle term (M) is already known. In this example, gases is P, irons is S, and metals is M:</p>
<p>No metals are gases.<br>All irons are metals.<br>Therefore no irons are gases.</p>
<p>The conclusion is a universal negative, not Barbara’s universal affirmative. An undistributed middle, already taught as invalid, is still invalid here: the middle term must do real linking work in the premises.</p>
