<p>The exact sines and cosines already taught on the unit circle in degrees do not change when the same angles are named in radians. 30° is π/6, and sin(π/6) = 1/2, cos(π/6) = √3/2. 45° is π/4, and sin(π/4) = cos(π/4) = √2/2. 60° is π/3, and sin(π/3) = √3/2, cos(π/3) = 1/2. 90° is π/2, and sin(π/2) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0. 180° is π, and sin π = 0, cos π = −1. 0, or 0°, is 0 radians, and sin 0 = 0, cos 0 = 1. </div>
<p>Example 17.4 in OpenStax 17.4, “Calculate Doppler Shift: A Train Horn,” uses official figures: a train horn of 150 Hz, moving at 35.0 m/s, in still air on a day when the speed of sound is 340 m/s. That 340 m/s is the example’s official speed for that day, not last week’s 331 m/s at 0°C or 343 m/s at 20.0°C, and not to be replaced by those figures in this calculation.</p><p>For a stationary person at the side of the tracks, the moving-source formula is used. As the train approaches, f_obs = 150 Hz × 340 m/s / (340 m/s − 35.0 m/s) = 150 Hz × 1.11 = 167 Hz. As the train recedes, f_obs = 150 Hz × 340 m/s / (340 m/s + 35.0 m/s) = 150 Hz × 0.907 = 136 Hz. The book notes that the shift is 17.0 Hz toward and 14.0 Hz away, and that the shifts are not symmetric. The engineer on the train hears 150 Hz, the source frequency, because the relative velocity between engineer and horn is zero; OpenStax shows that the moving-observer and moving-source factors then cancel, leaving f_obs = f_s. These are official Example 17.4 figures, not invented official measurements of a real train. Sonic booms remain reserved until Friday. Do not invent an official horn frequency for a train not in the example.</p>

<p>Estar is irregular in the present subjunctive: esté, estés, esté, estemos, estéis, estén. The accent on esté, estés, and estén marks the stress, and distinguishes esté from este as a demonstrative in writing; today’s job is the verb. Esté is not está, not estará, and not estaría. It is also not sea; ser and estar remain different verbs, even in the subjunctive. Invented frames may still use “quiere que”: “Quiere que estemos listos.” Haya, the present subjunctive of haber, remains reserved.</p>

<p>Last week, in Dewing’s 1940 translation of Procopius, <em>Buildings</em> I.3.16–18, the shrine of Michael was rebuilt. Today the same translation continues at I.4.1–8, still as posted on LacusCurtius. “His faith in the Apostles of Christ he displayed in the following manner. First he built a church of Peter and Paul, which had not previously existed in Byzantium, alongside the imperial residence which in former times was called by the name of Hormisdas.” “When he became Emperor of the Romans he joined it to the Palace proper.” “There too he built another shrine to the famous Saints Sergius and Bacchus, and then also another shrine which stood at an angle to this one.”</p><p>“These two churches do not face each other, but stand at an angle to one another, being at the same time joined to each other and rivalling each other; and they share the same entrances (eisodoi) and are like each other in all respects, even to the open spaces (kraspeda) by which they are surrounded; and each of them is found to be neither superior nor inferior to the other either in beauty or in size or in any other respect.” “Rivalling” is Dewing’s wording and stays inside the quotation. Teaching prose uses US spelling.</p><p>“In just one respect, however, they do differ. For the long axis (mêkos) of one of them is built straight, while in the other church the columns stand for the most part in a semi-circle (hêmikyklos).” They have propylaea “entirely in common,” “share a single court (aulê),” and “are alike in that they belong to the Palace.” “These two churches are so admirable that they manifestly form an adornment of the whole city, and not merely of the Palace.” Dewing does not give official lengths or official column counts, and the classroom invents none. Buildings I.4.9 onward, including the later church of the Apostles, remain reserved.</p>
