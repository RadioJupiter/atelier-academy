<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The area of a parallelogram is the base times the corresponding perpendicular height: A = b × h. The height is perpendicular to the chosen base, not a slanted side.</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 14 named the parallelogram; Day 15 used the rectangle as a special parallelogram. Today’s area is the same product b × h that a rectangle uses, once the height is perpendicular to the base.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A parallelogram is already known from Day 14: both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. Today’s job is its **area**.

You can slide the slanted end across and rearrange a parallelogram into a rectangle with the same base and the same perpendicular height. The area does not change under that slide. A rectangle’s area is base times height, so a parallelogram’s area is the same product.

**A = b × h**, where h is perpendicular to the chosen base.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Circular reasoning {#writing}

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="circular reasoning" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear circular reasoning">Hear</button> circular reasoning</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Circular reasoning is a flaw in which a claim is supported by a restatement of the same claim, or by a premise that already assumes what was supposed to be shown. It is a kind of informal fallacy, not merely a false statement.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Invented (labeled invented): ‘This rule is fair because it is a fair rule.’ The because-clause restates the claim instead of giving a new ground.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a merely false claim (a claim can be false and still be argued in a straight line). Not last week’s straw man, false dilemma, or ad hominem, which are different flaws.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Last week a fallacy was distinguished from a merely false claim, and three named flaws were taught. Today adds a fourth: the argument goes in a circle instead of offering independent grounds (Toulmin’s grounds, already taught).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Last week a **fallacy** was a flaw in reasoning, not merely a false claim. Straw man, false dilemma, and ad hominem remain those three jobs; they are not retaught as first looks.

Today’s flaw is **circular reasoning**: the support for a claim is only the claim again, or a premise that already assumes the conclusion.

Invented classroom examples, labeled invented:

- ‘This rule is fair because it is a fair rule.’ The because-clause is the same claim in other words. There is no new ground.
- ‘She is reliable, because you can always rely on her.’ Same circle.

A straight argument would offer a **ground** that can be inspected apart from the wording of the claim (the Toulmin job from week 13): a fact, an example, a sourced line.

A circle is still a fallacy even if the claim happens to be true. The flaw is in the support, not only in the truth-value of the sentence.

## Physics · Oscillation, period, and frequency: a first look {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Physics · oscillations begin; the simple-pendulum formula is tomorrow’s job</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-oscillation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="oscillation" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear oscillation">Hear</button> oscillation</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An oscillation is a repeating back-and-forth motion about a central position, such as a hanging bob swinging to and fro, or a mass on a spring moving in and out, once the motion repeats.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A hanging bob that swings left, through the middle, to the right, and back, and then does the same again, is oscillating.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a single shove that does not repeat, and not a trip that goes forward without returning. Not tomorrow’s formula for a simple pendulum, which is not yet today’s job.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Term 2’s physics plan includes oscillations after rotation. Last week’s equilibrium is a related but different job: a balance or a tip, not a repeating swing.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-period-oscillation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="period (oscillation)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear period (oscillation)">Hear</button> period (oscillation)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The period, T, is the time for one complete cycle of an oscillation — for example, the time from a leftmost position out and back to that same leftmost position.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a hanging bob takes 2 seconds to go out and return to the same point in the same direction, its period is 2 s. A particular classroom timing, if measured, is invented and labeled invented unless a source is named.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not frequency, which is how many cycles happen in one second. Not a calendar period, such as a month, from another subject.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Frequency f and period T are reciprocals: f = 1/T, when T is in seconds and f is in cycles per second. That relation is taught today; the pendulum formula T = 2π√(L/g) waits until Wednesday, with its OpenStax source.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

An **oscillation** is a motion that goes back and forth and **repeats**. A hanging bob that swings, or a quiet mass on a spring, is a first picture. A single shove that does not repeat is not an oscillation.

The **period**, T, is the time for **one complete cycle**. The **frequency**, f, is how many cycles happen in one second. They are reciprocals: **f = 1/T**. If T is 2 s, f is ½ cycle per second.

Classroom timings, labeled invented: a bob that takes 2 s for one cycle has period 2 s. That 2 s is not an official laboratory certificate, and it is not yet taken from a formula.

Tomorrow will use OpenStax, *College Physics 2e*, 16.4, for a simple pendulum: the period depends on length and on g, not on mass, for small swings. Today, do not use that formula yet. Today the job is the words **oscillation**, **period**, and **frequency**, and the relation f = 1/T.
