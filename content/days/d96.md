## Mathematics · Geometry LI: the surface area of a right pyramid {#math}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: the surface area of a right square pyramid is the base plus four triangular faces; the slant height is the altitude of a face, and may be found by Pythagoras</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-surface-area-of-a-right-pyramid">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="surface area of a right pyramid" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear surface area of a right pyramid">Hear</button> surface area of a right pyramid</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The surface area of a right pyramid is the area of the base plus the areas of the triangular faces. For a right square pyramid, there are four congruent triangular faces. If l is the slant height of a face — the altitude of that triangle from the apex to the base edge — the four faces have total area 2bl, and the whole surface is B + 2bl, where b is a base edge and B = b².</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A classroom right square pyramid has base 6 by 6 and face slant height 5, invented lengths. Each face has area (1/2) × 6 × 5 = 15, four faces 60, plus the base 36, so the surface area is 96 square units.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a first look at last week’s volume V = (1/3)Bh, which used the perpendicular height, not the slant height. Not the surface area of a cone, which is tomorrow. Classroom lengths are invented and labeled invented.</dd></div>

## Elective · Logic: the third figure, and Darapti {#seminar}

<article class="term" id="term-darapti">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="Darapti" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear Darapti">Hear</button> Darapti</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Darapti is a third-figure syllogism of the form AAI: a universal affirmative major, a universal affirmative minor, and a particular affirmative conclusion. <p>Last week’s Baroco was second figure: the middle term was the predicate of both premises. Today the middle term is the subject of both premises. That placement is the third figure.</p>
<p>The first mood taught in this figure is Darapti, AAI-3: All M are P; all M are S; therefore some S are P.</p>
<p>The conclusion is particular. The premises say that the whole of M lies in P, and that the whole of M also lies in S, so the two classes P and S overlap at least in M. They do not say that the whole of S lies in P. “All S are P” is the near-miss.</p>
<p>Classroom invented terms only, not a claim about a living person or a named official set.</p>
