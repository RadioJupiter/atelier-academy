## Mathematics · Algebra VI: radicals {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Gelfand, <em>Algebra</em>, square roots as the inverse of squaring</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-square-root">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="square root" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear square root">Hear</button> square root <span class="symbol">√a</span></h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>For a ≥ 0, the square root √a is the unique number ≥ 0 whose square is a. It undoes squaring on the non-negative numbers.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>√25 = 5, because 5² = 25 and 5 ≥ 0. √0 = 0. There is no real square root of a negative number.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>√a is not allowed to be negative: √25 is 5, not −5, even though (−5)² = 25 too. The equation x² = 25 has two real solutions, ±5; the symbol √25 names only the non-negative one.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday, a² meant two factors of a. The square root is the inverse operation when a ≥ 0: (√a)² = a and √(a²) = a for a ≥ 0.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-radical">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="radical" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear radical">Hear</button> radical</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>An expression that uses a root symbol, such as √a or ³√a. A square-root radical with a whole-number radicand is often rewritten by taking out a square factor: √(k² · n) = k√n when k ≥ 0.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English radical, from Latin rādīcālis (‘of the root, having roots’), from rādīx (‘root’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>√72 = √(36 · 2) = 6√2, and √12 + √27 = 2√3 + 3√3 = 5√3.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>You may not add radicands: √12 + √27 is not √39. You may multiply them: √a · √b = √(ab) for a, b ≥ 0.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Simplified form (for this course): no perfect-square factor other than 1 remains under a square-root sign, and no radical sits in a denominator that you can clear.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

The **square root** √*a*, for *a* ≥ 0, is the unique number ≥ 0 that squares to *a*. The two real solutions of *x*² = 25 are *x* = 5 and *x* = −5; the symbol √25 means only 5.

**Product law.** For *a*, *b* ≥ 0, √*a* · √*b* = √(*ab*). In the other direction, a square factor comes out: √(*k*² · *n*) = *k*√*n* when *k* ≥ 0.

**Sum.** Radicals add only when they are like terms: √12 + √27 = 2√3 + 3√3 = 5√3. You cannot write √12 + √27 as a single square root of a sum.

**Check by squaring.** If you claim √72 = 6√2, then (6√2)² = 36 · 2 = 72.

## Science · Pigment and binder {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: the paint as a mixture, named from what each part does</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-pigment">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="pigment" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear pigment">Hear</button> pigment</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A finely divided solid that gives a paint its color (and often its opacity) and that remains as particles in the film. It is not the liquid that hardens around it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English pigment, from Latin pigmentum, from pingere (‘to paint’) + -mentum.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Yellow ochre is an earth pigment colored by iron oxides; a carbon black is a different solid, and neither one is the linseed oil.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A pigment is not a dye dissolved as a true solution (though some colorants are dyes), and it is not the binder. Crushing a rock does not, by itself, make a paint until a binder is added.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Term 3 will return to ‘from rock to pigment’. Today you only need the distinction: the solid particles that color the film, versus the oil that forms the film.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-binder">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="binder" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear binder">Hear</button> binder</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The substance that holds the pigment particles in a film and attaches the film to the support. In oil paint, the binder is a drying oil; in watercolor, it is usually gum, and the water is mainly a vehicle that evaporates.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English, bind + the agent ending -er.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The same ochre, mixed in linseed oil or in gum and water, makes two different paints, because the binder is different.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The binder is not the pigment, and it is not a solvent added only to thin the paint (that solvent, if used, is meant to leave).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The last two lessons explained how a drying-oil binder forms a film. Today that film is what holds the pigment.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A paint is, at its simplest, **pigment** in a **binder**.

The **pigment** is a solid in small particles: earth ochres (colored by iron oxides), carbon blacks, and, in later history, many others, including lead white (toxic, and not a material to handle in this course). The pigment gives color and, often, hiding power. It does not have to polymerize.

The **binder** is what becomes the film and holds those particles. For oil paint, that is the drying oil you have been studying. For watercolor, a gum binder remains after the water evaporates.

**A small, safe comparison.** If you have a yellow ochre or another dry earth pigment, mix a little with water alone, and a little with a drop of linseed oil, on a labeled plate. The oil mixture should, over the days of this module, begin to hold as a film; the water mixture will either dust off or, if a gum is present, hold by a different binder. Do not use lead or other toxic historical pigments.

The solvent (turpentine or a modern substitute), if used, is there to change the handling and then to leave. It is not the binder.

</div>

## Atelier · A plaster feature: from plane to a more particular form {#atelier}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: a small plaster cast of a feature (nose, ear, mouth, or eye), one light, fixed viewing point · After the geometric solids and the Bargue feature</p>

A **plaster feature** is the meeting of the **solids** and the **Bargue feature**: a real, pale form in the round, particular, and still constructed from **large planes**.

**Set-up.** One feature, one main light (a clear light plane and a clear shadow), a fixed viewing point, sight-size if you can. A simple background or a cloth that does not compete.

**Order of work.**
1. Enclosing shape and main angles (as if it were a small cube or wedge).
2. Large planes and the transition between them (sharp, soft, or stepped).
3. Alignments: a plumb through the bridge of a nose, a horizontal across the wings, and so on.
4. Families of light, then hatch or plane tone, with control. The terminator may change character: harder on a sharp plane change, softer on a turn.

**White plaster is not a reason to leave the drawing pale.** The shadow family on plaster can be quite dark relative to the light family; what plaster gives you is a chance to see that relation without local color. Compare, do not invent a timid mid-gray everywhere.

## Modern language · Spanish: the personal a {#language}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: explicit rule, then contrast with things and with tener · Instruction in English</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-personal-a">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="personal a" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear personal a">Hear</button> personal a</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The preposition a placed before a direct object that is a specific person (or a pet, treated as a person). It does not translate as a separate English word.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Veo a María. Busco a mi amigo. ¿Conoces a los estudiantes? But: Veo la casa. Busco el libro.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The personal a is not the a of ir a + infinitive, and it is not needed before a thing. It is also not used with tener for ordinary possession: Tengo un hermano, not *Tengo a un hermano* in that sense.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>You have used a in voy a estudiar and voy al museo. Those are different jobs. Today’s a marks a person as the direct object.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-specific-person-object">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="a specific person as object" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear a specific person as object">Hear</button> a specific person as object</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The personal a appears when the object is a particular person you have in mind, including names, family words used of real people, and pronouns for people. It is not used when the object is an indefinite, unspecific ‘anyone’ in a few set patterns, and it is not used for things.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Visito a mi profesora. No veo a nadie is a related pattern you may meet later; today, stay with clear, specific people versus things.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Do not put a before la casa, el libro, or la lección. Do not drop a before María.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Direct-object pronouns still replace the whole object: ¿Ves a María? Sí, la veo. The a is not kept in front of la.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

When the **direct object is a specific person**, Spanish puts **a** before that object. English has no separate word for this *a*.

**People, not things.** *Veo a Ana. Visito a mi familia. Busco a mi amigo.* But *Veo el museo. Visito la ciudad. Busco el libro.*

**Names and family words.** A name is specific: *¿Llamas a Pedro?* A family noun used of a real person is too: *Ayudo a mi hermano.*

**Not with tener (possession).** *Tengo un hermano. Tengo una profesora.* Do not write *Tengo a un hermano* for ordinary ‘I have’.

**Not the same a as motion or the near future.** *Voy a la escuela* (motion). *Voy a estudiar* (near future). *Veo a la profesora* (personal *a*).

**With pronouns.** The personal *a* is not stacked in front of *lo/la*: *¿Ves a Ana? Sí, la veo.*

## Music theory · The triad {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: build from intervals already taught · After Day 12</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-triad">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="triad" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear triad">Hear</button> triad</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A three-note chord built from a root, a third above the root, and a fifth above the root. In this lesson the fifth is perfect, and the third is either major or minor.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin triad-, the stem of trias (‘three, triad’), from Ancient Greek τριάς.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A C-major triad is C–E–G: C to E a major third, C to G a perfect fifth. An A-minor triad is A–C–E: A to C a minor third, A to E a perfect fifth.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A triad is not ‘any three notes’, and it is not a scale. Three neighboring scale steps (C–D–E) are not a triad in this sense.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 12’s intervals are the measuring tools. The quality of the third decides whether the triad is major or minor, when the fifth is perfect.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-major-minor-triad">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="major and minor triad" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear major and minor triad">Hear</button> major and minor triad</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A major triad has a major third and a perfect fifth above the root (the upper interval, from the third to the fifth, is then a minor third). A minor triad has a minor third and a perfect fifth (the upper interval is then a major third).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>G–B–D is major (G–B is a major third). E–G–B is minor (E–G is a minor third). Both have a perfect fifth from the root (G–D, E–B).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>‘Major’ and ‘minor’ here name the triad, not the key of a whole piece, and not a mood. A minor triad can appear in a major key, and a major triad in a minor key.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>You do not need diminished or augmented triads for the check, except to know that they are what you get if the fifth is no longer perfect.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A **triad** stacks a **root**, a **third**, and a **fifth**.

**Major.** Root, major third, perfect fifth. In C major, the triad on C is C–E–G. The triad on F is F–A–C; on G, G–B–D. Those three are the major triads in C major (built on scale degrees 1, 4, and 5).

**Minor.** Root, minor third, perfect fifth. In C major, the triad on D is D–F–A (D–F is a minor third); on E, E–G–B; on A, A–C–E.

**A building check.** From the root, count the third’s quality first, then check that the fifth is perfect (seven half steps, or the span you already named a perfect fifth on Day 12).

**The same three notes in another order** are still that triad (C–E–G, E–G–C, G–C–E are all a C-major triad). Today’s check will mostly use root position: the root at the bottom.

Do not invent extra chromatic notes unless a question asks for a triad from a named quality (for example, a major triad on D needs F♯, because D–F is a minor third).

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A triad is root + third + fifth. Major: major third and perfect fifth. Minor: minor third and perfect fifth. In C major, triads on C, F, G are major; on D, E, A are minor. The same three notes remain that triad if the order changes.


<p>Key terms: triad, major and minor triad.</p>
