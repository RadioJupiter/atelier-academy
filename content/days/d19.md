<p class="meta">30 min · Text: Ørberg, <em>Familia Romana</em>, Cap. VI · Method: the natural method</p>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-passive">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="passive" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear passive">Hear</button> passive</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A verb form in which the subject is the person or thing that the action happens to, not the one who does it. In this chapter you meet third-person forms such as portātur (‘is carried’) and portantur (‘are carried’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Saccus ā servō portātur. The sack is carried by the slave. The subject is saccus; the doer is named by ā/ab + ablative.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>The passive is not a past tense. portātur is still a present-tense form. And ā servō (by the slave) is not the same as cum servō (with the slave).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>You already know the active portat / portant (he carries / they carry) from earlier chapters. The new endings −tur and −ntur flip who the subject is.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-agent-ablative">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ablative of agent" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ablative of agent">Hear</button> ablative of agent</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The construction ā or ab + ablative that names the person who does the action of a passive verb.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Saccus ā servō portātur (the sack is carried by the slave). Saccī ā servīs portantur (the sacks are carried by the slaves).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>This is not motion from, even though it uses the same preposition ab. In a passive sentence ā servō names the doer, not a place left behind.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>cum + ablative remains accompaniment (with). Agent is only for the doer of a passive verb.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**The pattern.** Active: *Servus saccum portat.* Passive: *Saccus ā servō portātur.* The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive; the old subject is kept, if you need it, as *ā/ab* + ablative.

Original sentences: *Saccus ā servō portātur.* *Saccī ā servīs portantur.* *Puer ā dominō nōn portātur — puer ambulat.*

Passive present, 3rd person: −tur (singular), −ntur (plural). The subject receives the action. The doer, if named, is ā/ab + ablative (agent), not cum. portātur is not a past tense.

<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="two tangents from a point" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear two tangents from a point">Hear</button> two tangents from a point</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>From a point outside a circle, the two tangent segments to the points of contact are equal. The two radii to those points, the two tangents, and the line from the center to the outside point make two congruent right triangles.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If both tangents from P have length 8, the two points of contact are the same distance from P, and OP bisects angle APB and also bisects the angle AOB at the center.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>This is not ‘any two tangents of the same circle are equal’ — only the two drawn from the same outside point. Parallel tangents on opposite sides are a different figure.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The right angle at each point of contact is today's first theorem; congruence of the two right triangles gives the equal lengths.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

A line that meets a circle at exactly one point is a **tangent**, and that point is the **point of contact**.

**Theorem.** The radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent.

**Theorem.** The two tangent segments from an outside point to the points of contact are equal.

If *O* is the center, *A* the point of contact, and *P* a point on the tangent, then △*OAP* is right-angled at *A*, so

*OA*² + *AP*² = *OP*².

A 5-12-13 triangle, or a 6-8-10, will often appear, the same triples you already know.

</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A tangent meets the circle once, and is perpendicular to the radius at that point. The two tangent segments from an outside point are equal. OA² + AP² = OP².

## Writing & Rhetoric · Concession in a two-paragraph argument {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: The Writing Revolution, the although-sentence used at the level of a paragraph</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-concession">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="concession" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear concession">Hear</button> concession</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A real point on the other side that you state fairly, before you show why your thesis still stands. In this course it often takes the form of an although-sentence (Day 9), and in a two-paragraph essay it often opens the second paragraph or appears in the concluding sentence.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From late Middle English, from Middle French concession, from Latin concessiō (‘a grant, a conceding’), from concēdere.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Although the Karnak text is a royal inscription and not a neutral minutes-of-the-meeting, it can still be compared, clause by clause, with the Hattusa tablet.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A concession is not a fake weakness (‘although some people may disagree’), and it is not a surrender of the thesis. If you ignore the point after stating it, it was only decoration.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 9 taught the although-sentence at sentence level. Today that sentence has a job in the two-paragraph outline from Day 16.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-limit-of-claim">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="limit of a claim" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear limit of a claim">Hear</button> limit of a claim</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A precise statement of what the thesis does not try to prove, so that the argument stays as strong as the evidence. A limit is not a concession of a contrary fact; it is a boundary around your own claim.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>The thesis that the two copies of the treaty should be read together does not, by itself, prove who ‘won’ the earlier battle of Kadesh; that question is beyond the present evidence.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A limit is not timidity, and it is not ‘we can never know anything’. It is a way of not claiming more than the sources will bear (Day 1’s sourcing habit).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A strong concluding sentence often carries a limit, a concession, or both, and then returns to what the evidence *does* support.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

A two-paragraph argument is stronger when it **concedes** a real point and when it **limits** its own claim.

**Where to put them.** A concession often opens paragraph 2 (‘Although…, …’). A limit often belongs in the concluding sentence, so the close is exact rather than inflated.

**A test for a fake concession.** If the conceded point is ‘some people may disagree’, or if you never use it, it is decoration. Name a specific fact or a specific reading that a fair opponent would actually use.

## Science · The oil-paint module: a quantitative check and a short synthesis {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: check the model against a measurement, then write a short synthesis</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-mass-check">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="mass check (of a film)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear mass check (of a film)">Hear</button> mass check (of a film)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A measurement that can distinguish evaporation from autoxidation: a film that is mainly losing a liquid to the air should lose mass; a drying-oil film can gain mass as it takes up oxygen (before any later, slower losses). The check is the direction of the change, not a promised percentage.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A labeled smear of linseed oil, weighed on a day it is still liquid and again when it is tacky, may be a little heavier if oxygen has been taken up. A watercolor smear should be lighter once the water has gone.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A mass check is not a license to invent a number you did not measure, and a single weighing on a coarse kitchen scale may not be sensitive enough. If the scale cannot show the change, say so; do not fake a gain.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The model of Days 12–14 predicts a possible gain for the oil and a loss for the watercolor. The observation has to be real.</dd></div>

<div class="keyterms">

<article class="term" id="term-comparative">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="comparative" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear comparative">Hear</button> comparative</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A pattern that compares two people or things. In Spanish: más + adjective (or adverb or noun) + que; menos + … + que; and tan + adjective or adverb + como for ‘as … as’.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Más que is not tan como, and más de is a different pattern used before a number (más de diez), not before the second person or thing being compared.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Adjectives still agree: más larga, menos difíciles, tan altos como…</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-irregular-comparative">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="irregular comparative" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear irregular comparative">Hear</button> irregular comparative</h5>

To **compare**, Spanish uses three regular patterns and a short irregular set.

**More / less.** *más + adjective + que*: *El museo es más grande que la casa.* *menos + adjective + que*: *La carta es menos larga que el libro.* The adjective agrees with the first noun.

**As … as.** *tan + adjective + como*: *La lección es tan importante como el dibujo.*

**Nouns.** *más / menos + noun + que*: *Tengo más libros que cuadernos.* *tan* is not used this way with a noun; for ‘as many as’ you will later meet *tantos/tantas … como*. Do not invent *tan libros como* today.

**Before a number.** *más de / menos de* + a number: *más de diez páginas.* That *de* is not the *que* of a two-thing comparison.

**Irregular.** mejor que, peor que, mayor que, menor que. Never *más mejor*.

## Economics & personal finance · Supply and demand {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: schedules, then shortage, surplus, and equilibrium · After Day 14 · Classroom numbers are invented examples</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-demand-supply">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="demand and supply" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear demand and supply">Hear</button> demand and supply</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Demand is the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy, other things equal. Supply is the relationship between the price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer, other things equal. Each is a whole schedule or curve, not a single number.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Demand is from Middle English, from Old French demander, from Latin dēmandāre. Supply is from Middle English, from Old French, from Latin supplēre (‘to fill up, to complete’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If, in an invented classroom table, students will buy 40 sandwiches at $3 and 20 at $5, those two points belong to a demand schedule. If the canteen will offer 15 at $3 and 35 at $5, those points belong to a supply schedule.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Demand is not ‘what people wish they could have’, and supply is not ‘how many exist in a warehouse’. Willingness and ability, at a price, are part of the definition.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 14’s price signal is what these two schedules together explain: why a price might rise or fall when the two sides do not match.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-equilibrium-price">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="equilibrium, shortage, and surplus" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear equilibrium, shortage, and surplus">Hear</button> equilibrium, shortage, and surplus</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At a price below that, there is a shortage (quantity demanded greater than quantity supplied). At a price above it, there is a surplus (quantity supplied greater than quantity demanded).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Equilibrium is from Latin aequilībrium, from aequus (‘equal’) + lībra (‘balance’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In the invented table: at $3, buyers want 40 and the canteen offers 15 — a shortage of 25. At $5, buyers want 20 and the canteen offers 35 — a surplus of 15. The equilibrium, if the table were completed, would be a price where the two quantities match.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Equilibrium is not ‘the fair price’ and not ‘a price that never changes’. It is the matching price in this model, other things equal. A change in tastes, costs, or income can shift a whole schedule and move the equilibrium.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Shortage and surplus are about quantities at a price, not about whether anyone is morally at fault.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

Day 14 treated a price as a signal. Today we draw (or tabulate) the two sides of that signal.

**Demand.** At each price, how many units buyers are willing and able to buy, other things equal. Usually, a higher price means a lower quantity demanded. That is a claim about the slope of a demand schedule, not a law about every luxury or every emergency.

**Supply.** At each price, how many units sellers are willing and able to offer, other things equal. Usually, a higher price means a higher quantity supplied.

**The invented canteen table** (not real data):

| Price | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $2 | 50 | 10 |
| $3 | 40 | 15 |
| $4 | 30 | 30 |
| $5 | 20 | 35 |
| $6 | 10 | 40 |

At **$4**, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (30). That is the **equilibrium** of this table. At **$3**, there is a **shortage** of 25. At **$5**, there is a **surplus** of 15.

**A story that stays in the model.** If the price were $3, some buyers would not find a sandwich; pressure on the price would be upward. If it were $5, some sandwiches would go unsold; pressure would be downward. The model does not say the canteen is kind or unkind; it says what the unmatched quantities do to the price, if the price is free to move.

**Shifts** (a first look). If more students suddenly want sandwiches at every price, the demand schedule shifts, and the equilibrium price in a new table would be higher. If costs of bread rise and the canteen will offer fewer sandwiches at each price, supply shifts, and the new equilibrium price would be higher. Do not confuse a movement along a schedule (a price change, other things equal) with a shift of the whole schedule.
