**Three jobs, kept apart.** *hic* points near: this. *ille* points far, or contrasts: that. *is* refers back without pointing strongly.

Original sentences: *Hic puer in hortō est; ille puer in vīllā est.* *Iūlia illam rosam, nōn hanc, Mārcō dat.* *Mārcus librum habet; eum Iūliae ostendit.*

Neuter *illud* and *hoc* end in *-d* in the nominative and accusative singular, not in *-um* (compare *pulchrum*). That is a pronominal signature you can learn to see.

## Mathematics · Algebra VIII: the sum of a finite geometric progression {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: the rS − S subtraction as the reason, then the formula; r = 1 as a separate case</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-sum-of-a-finite-gp">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="sum of a finite geometric progression" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear sum of a finite geometric progression">Hear</button> sum of a finite geometric progression</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The sum of the first n terms of a geometric progression with first term a and common ratio r is S<sub>n</sub> = a(r<sup>n</sup> − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1. When r = 1, every term is a, and S<sub>n</sub> = n a. Day 20 defined the GP and the nth term; today adds the finite sum.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>2 + 6 + 18 + 54: a = 2, r = 3, n = 4, S = 2(3<sup>4</sup> − 1)/(3 − 1) = 2(81 − 1)/2 = 80.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the AP pairing formula n/2 · (a + ℓ), which belongs to a constant *difference*. And not an infinite-sum formula, which this course does not use today (and which would need |r| < 1 and a later argument).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>A reason: S = a + ar + … + ar^{n−1}, rS = ar + … + ar^n, subtract: rS − S = ar^n − a, so S(r − 1) = a(r^n − 1).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The subtraction trick, which is the reason.** Write S, then rS, and subtract. Most terms cancel, and you are left with S = a(r^n − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1.

If r = 1, that formula divides by zero and is the wrong tool: the sum is just n a.

**Worked numbers.** 2 + 6 + 18 + 54 = 80. 5 + 10 + 20 + 40 + 80 = 5(2^5 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 5(32 − 1) = 155. Five terms of 3, 3, 3, … : r = 1, S = 15.

**A signed ratio** is allowed: 3 + (−6) + 12 + (−24): a = 3, r = −2, n = 4, S = 3((-2)^4 − 1)/(-2 − 1) = 3(16 − 1)/(−3) = 3(15)/(−3) = −15. Check by adding: 3 − 6 + 12 − 24 = −15.


</div>

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

S = a(r<sup>n</sup> − 1)/(r − 1) when r ≠ 1; S = n a when r = 1. Do not use the AP pairing formula on a GP. No infinite sums today.

## Writing & Rhetoric · The three-paragraph outline {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: an extension of the two-paragraph outline (Day 16) with a closing concession and return</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-three-paragraph-outline">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="three-paragraph outline" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear three-paragraph outline">Hear</button> three-paragraph outline</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A plan for a short essay of three paragraphs: an opening paragraph that states a thesis, a middle paragraph that develops one line of support (with evidence or a précis of a source), and a closing paragraph that includes a concession or a limit and then returns to the thesis in new words.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Thesis: inscribed angles are half the central angle only on the same arc. Middle: Euclid III.20 and a worked 80°/40° figure. Close: a concession that a different arc gives a different measure, then a restatement of the same-arc rule.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not three unconnected paragraphs, and not one long paragraph chopped at two indentations. The jobs are different.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 16 taught the two-paragraph outline. Today adds a third paragraph for a concession and a return, which Day 19 already practiced in two-paragraph form. The new skill is the three-job plan, not a longer page for its own sake.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


**The three jobs.** (1) Open: thesis, in one or two sentences, plus a brief map of the support. (2) Support: one focused paragraph with a reason and a piece of evidence (a theorem, a short attributed quotation, or a précis of a source). (3) Close: a real concession or limit, then a concluding sentence that returns to the thesis in new words.

**What does not change.** Topic sentences still head the paragraphs. A quotation still needs an introduction and an explanation (Day 18). A concession is still a real limit, not a fake “some people say” you immediately dismiss without looking.

**A school subject.** Geometry, a Latin contrast, a science caution, or a literary design from these two weeks — not a new political essay on the Declaration. The Declaration remains a model of *form* when you need one.

## Physics · Gravitational potential energy near the Earth {#physics}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: PE = m g h as a first model with a chosen zero; g = 9.8 N/kg; honest limits</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-gravitational-pe">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="gravitational potential energy (near Earth)" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear gravitational potential energy (near Earth)">Hear</button> gravitational potential energy (near Earth)</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>In this course’s first model, the gravitational potential energy of an object near the Earth, treated as a particle, is PE = m g h, where h is the height above a chosen reference level, g is 9.8 N/kg (or 9.8 m/s²), and PE is in joules. Only differences in PE are physically usable; the zero of h is a choice.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Latin <em>potentiālis</em>, from <em>potentia</em>, ‘power’ (Wiktionary).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A 2 kg object at 2.5 m above a chosen zero has PE = 2 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 49 J relative to that zero.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a claim about gravity far from the Earth (no 1/r formula today), and not a third kind of mass. The m is the same mass as in F = m a and in KE = ½ m v².</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Work against gravity, when you lift an object slowly without changing KE, equals the gain in PE: W = m g Δh (force m g along Δh, upward).</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-reference-level">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="reference level" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear reference level">Hear</button> reference level</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The height you choose as h = 0 when you write PE = m g h. Changing the reference changes the number you write for PE, but it does not change a difference PE₂ − PE₁ between two heights.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>If a shelf is 2.5 m above the floor, PE is 49 J for a 2 kg mass with the floor as zero, and 0 J if the shelf itself is the zero. The drop from shelf to floor still changes PE by −49 J.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not a “true” zero required by nature in this model, and not a reason to mix two zeros in one calculation without converting.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Pick one zero and keep it for the problem. The floor, the ground, or the lowest point of a path are common choices.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**Four forms, in geometry you already know.**

- A: All squares are rectangles. (true, in this course)
- E: No squares are rectangles. (false)
- I: Some squares are rectangles. (true — in fact all are, and “some” still holds)
- O: Some squares are not rectangles. (false)

**Contradictories.** A and O cannot share a truth value: if A is true, O is false, and conversely. E and I likewise.

**Contraries.** A and E cannot both be true. They can both be false: “All rectangles are squares” and “No rectangles are squares” are both false.

**A named assumption.** The traditional square, as used today, treats “All S are P” as implying “Some S are P,” which requires that there is at least one S. We will not build arguments about empty classes in this lesson; we only name the assumption so it is not smuggled.
