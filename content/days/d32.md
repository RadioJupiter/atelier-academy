## Mathematics · Algebra IX: graphs of linear functions {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: a table of values, then a straight graph; slope as rise over run</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-linear-function">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="linear function" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear linear function">Hear</button> linear function</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A function whose graph is a straight line. In the form f(x) = mx + b, b is the y-intercept (the output when x = 0), and m is the slope (the rise over the run between any two points on the line).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>f(x) = 2x + 1 has intercept 1 and slope 2: from (0, 1) to (2, 5) the run is 2 and the rise is 4, and 4/2 = 2.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not every graph is a line (tomorrow’s parabola is not). And m is not automatically the y-intercept; that job belongs to b in this form.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Yesterday’s f(x) is what we plot. A table of inputs and outputs becomes points; a line through those points is the graph of a linear function.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-slope">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="slope" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear slope">Hear</button> slope</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The constant rate of change of a linear function: rise over run, (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), between any two points on the line.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>On y = 2x + 1, from (0, 1) to (1, 3) the slope is (3−1)/(1−0) = 2, the same m.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the intercept, and not a turn in degrees (that was an angle). A vertical line would have an undefined slope; we do not need that case today.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The same m appears in the formula f(x) = mx + b and in the rise-over-run calculation. That is a check, not a coincidence.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**To graph a linear function**, make a short table, plot the points, and draw the line through them. For f(x) = 2x + 1:

| x | f(x) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |

The point (0, 1) sits on the y-axis: that is the **intercept** b = 1. From (0, 1) to (2, 5) the run is 2 and the rise is 4, so the **slope** m is 4/2 = 2, which matches the 2 in 2x + 1.

**A first warning.** Two points already determine a straight line; a third point is a check. If a plotted point misses the line, recompute the table before “bending” the graph.

## Science · How we see: the eye as a kind of camera {#science}

<p class="meta">50 min · Method: a labeled picture (cornea, pupil, lens, retina) and a careful analogy, not a measured optical bench</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-cornea">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="cornea" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear cornea">Hear</button> cornea</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The clear front surface of the eye, which begins the bending of incoming light. The name comes from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn,” for a tough, horn-like tissue — not because the eye is made of horn.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Light from a lamp first meets the cornea, then the pupil and the lens. The cornea does much of the eye’s bending of light; this lesson does not assign a measured focal length to it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the colored iris, and not the retina at the back. The cornea is at the front, clear, and in contact with the outside air (under the tear film).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The camera analogy: the cornea and lens together play a role like a camera’s lens; the analogy is a first map, not a claim that the eye is a manufactured camera.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-retina">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="retina" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear retina">Hear</button> retina</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, where an image is formed and a signal begins. The name comes from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.”</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Light that has been bent by the cornea and lens reaches the retina. We do not invent a count of cells, or a measured focal length, in this lesson.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Not the pupil (an opening), and not the optic nerve itself (the path of the signal leaving the eye — a first name today, treated more carefully on Thursday).</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The camera analogy: the retina is like a film or a sensor, a place where the pattern of light is received. It is living tissue, not a manufactured film.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>


A first map of the eye, by analogy with a camera, and then the limits of the analogy:

- Light enters through the **cornea** (clear front), then the **pupil** (an opening in the iris), then the **lens**.
- An image is formed on the **retina** at the back.
- A signal then leaves toward the brain (Thursday’s lesson names the path more carefully).

The analogy helps you remember the order. It does **not** give you a measured focal length, a shutter speed, or a count of receptor cells. This course will not invent those numbers.


</div>

Today we name two intervals in a major scale as this course has used it. **perfect fifth and major third (first look).** Own words should match this idea: In a major scale, the interval from the first degree to the fifth is a perfect fifth (for example, C up to G). The interval from the first degree to the third is a major third (C up to E). These are first names; other qualities (minor, diminished, augmented) are not taught as a full system today. Near-miss: Not “any two notes five letter-names apart are always a perfect fifth in every context,” if an accidental has changed the size — a caution named, not a full accidentals lesson. In the unaltered major scale taught here, 1-to-5 is a perfect fifth and 1-to-3 is a major third. The fresh case should use the term correctly and not be copied from the card.
