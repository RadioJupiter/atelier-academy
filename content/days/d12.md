## Mathematics · Geometry VI: the angle bisector and a parallel through a point {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · Method: Kiselev, <em>Planimetry</em>, constructions</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-angle-bisector">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="angle bisector" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear angle bisector">Hear</button> angle bisector</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The ray that divides an angle into two equal angles. Every point on the angle bisector is equidistant from the two sides of the angle (the distances being perpendicular distances to the sides).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>In an angle of 70°, the bisector makes two angles of 35°. A point 2 cm from one side of the angle and 2 cm from the other lies on the bisector.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not a line that merely ‘looks in the middle’, and the distances to the sides are perpendicular distances, not distances along the sides.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>The construction uses two equal arcs and a copied length, the same habit as yesterday's copied angle. The reason is again a locus: points equidistant from two lines.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-parallel-construction">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="parallel through a point" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear parallel through a point">Hear</button> parallel through a point</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The unique line through a given point P, not on a line ℓ, that is parallel to ℓ. It can be constructed by copying a corresponding (or alternate interior) angle made by a transversal through P.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>Draw a transversal through P that cuts ℓ. Copy, at P, the corresponding angle so that the new line is parallel to ℓ by the converse proved in Day 6.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>It is not ‘any line through P that does not meet ℓ on the page’. Parallelism is a property in the plane, justified by equal corresponding angles, not by the drawing happening to stop short.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>Day 6: corresponding angles equal if and only if the lines are parallel (given a transversal). The parallel axiom says there is at most one such line through P.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
</div>

**To bisect an angle.** Given ∠BAC, swing an arc centered at A that cuts both rays, at D and E. With the same opening (or any equal openings), swing an arc centered at D and an arc centered at E, meeting at F inside the angle. The ray AF is the angle bisector.

**Why it works.** AD = AE (same opening from A), DF = EF (same opening from D and E), and AF is common, so △ADF ≅ △AEF by SSS, and ∠DAF = ∠EAF.

**Locus form.** The points inside the angle that are equally far from the two sides lie on the bisector. That is the same two-direction locus habit as the perpendicular bisector, applied to distance from two lines rather than from two points.

**A parallel through a point.** Given line ℓ and a point P not on it, draw any transversal through P that cuts ℓ, forming (for example) a corresponding angle. Copy that angle at P, on the corresponding side of the transversal. The new line through P is parallel to ℓ, because corresponding angles are equal (Day 6). By the parallel axiom, it is the only such line.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

## Science · Phenomenon: why oil paint dries {#science}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Finnish phenomenon-based learning, starting from a real process in the studio</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-drying-oil">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="drying oil" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear drying oil">Hear</button> drying oil</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>A plant oil, such as linseed oil (from flax seed), that can form a solid film by reacting with oxygen in the air, rather than by the evaporation of water. Painters use it as the binder of oil paint.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>Linseed is from Old English līnsǣd, ‘seed of flax’, from līn (‘flax’) + sǣd (‘seed’).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A thin film of linseed oil left on a glass plate becomes tacky and then firm over days, even though little water is leaving it.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>A drying oil is not an oil that is already dry, and it is not the same as a volatile solvent (such as spirits of turpentine) that does leave by evaporation. Watercolor ‘dries’ mainly by water leaving; oil paint does not.</dd></div>
<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>This module starts from a process you can see in the atelier. Tomorrow names the chemical reaction; later lessons add the pigment and the film.</dd></div>
</dl></article>
<article class="term" id="term-evaporation">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="evaporation" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear evaporation">Hear</button> evaporation</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The change in which a liquid becomes a gas at its surface. A watercolor wash becomes a dry layer mainly because water evaporates; that is not the main way a drying oil forms a film.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Example</dt><dd>A drop of water on a plate shrinks and disappears; a drop of linseed oil does not disappear in the same way, even over a longer time.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>Evaporation is not boiling (which happens throughout the liquid at a characteristic temperature), and it is not the same as a chemical reaction with oxygen.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-ellipse">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="ellipse" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear ellipse">Hear</button> ellipse</h5>

<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The shape a circle appears to take when it is foreshortened — the end of a cylinder, or a wheel seen at an angle. A **cylinder** is a circle carried along an **axis**. In a drawing, each circular end becomes an **ellipse**.

**Construction.** Draw the axis first (a light line). Then the two end ellipses, sharing that axis: the short axis of each ellipse lies on the cylinder’s axis. The sides of the cylinder are tangents to the ellipses, not independent straight lines that miss the ends.

**The ellipse, honestly.** No points. The curve is steepest at the ends of the long axis and flatter at the ends of the short axis, but it remains a curve. A standing cylinder below eye level: the top ellipse is more open or more closed than the bottom one, according to how far each end is from eye level. Nearer eye level, the ellipse is thinner (more foreshortened). Well below eye level, it opens. Do not invent a rule you have not checked from your viewing point: measure the height of each ellipse.

**Light.** One main light. The cylinder has a light family, a terminator (often a soft band running with the axis), a form shadow, possible reflected light, and a cast shadow. The value bands run with the axis, around the curve, not as a flat rectangle of gray.

**A common error.** A pointed oval; or two ellipses of different tilt, as if the cylinder were bent; or sides that taper for no reason (unless you are actually drawing a slightly tapered can and have measured it).

## Music theory · Intervals {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Method: explicit naming, then ear and staff practice · After Day 2 (staff and clefs) and Day 7 (the major scale)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="keyterms">
<h4>Key terms</h4>
<article class="term" id="term-interval">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="interval" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear interval">Hear</button> interval</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>The distance between two pitches, named by counting letter-names inclusively, then qualified as perfect, major, or minor (and, later, augmented or diminished).</dd></div>
<div><dt>Origin</dt><dd>From Middle English interval, from Old French intervalle, from Latin intervallum (‘space between’, originally the space between palisades or walls), from inter (‘between’) + vallum (‘wall’).</dd></div>

<div><dt>Not this</dt><dd>An interval is not ‘how hard the piece is’, and the number is not a count of steps alone (C to E is a third, whether you count two whole steps or not). Name the letter-span first, then the quality.</dd></div>

<article class="term" id="term-perfect-and-major">
<h5><button type="button" class="listen listen-inline no-speak" data-say="perfect, major, and minor" data-lang="en-US" aria-label="Hear perfect, major, and minor">Hear</button> perfect, major, and minor</h5>
<dl class="frayer">
<div><dt>Definition</dt><dd>Qualities of intervals. Unison, fourth, fifth, and octave are called perfect in the major scale from the tonic (C–C, C–F, C–G, C–C′ in C major). <div><dt>Example</dt><dd>C–D is a major second (a whole step); C–E is a major third; C–A a major sixth; C–B a major seventh. E–F is a minor second (a half step). C–E♭ is a minor third.</dd></div>

<div class="wide"><dt>Connects to</dt><dd>You do not need augmented and diminished intervals for today’s check, except to know that they are further alterations and not guessed at.</dd></div>
</dl></article>

An **interval** has a **number** and a **quality**.

**The number.** Count letter-names inclusively. C to C is a unison (or, an octave up, an octave). C to D is a second; to E a third; to F a fourth; to G a fifth; to A a sixth; to B a seventh; to the next C an octave.

**The quality, from a major scale.** From the tonic of a major scale, the intervals to the other scale degrees are:

- perfect unison, perfect fourth, perfect fifth, perfect octave;
- major second, third, sixth, and seventh.

A **minor** interval is a major interval made one half step smaller, without changing the letter-name (C–E is a major third; C–E♭ is a minor third, not a stretched second).

**Half step and whole step, as a check.** A minor second is a half step (E–F, B–C in C major, or any two neighboring keys with no pitch between them). A major second is a whole step. A major third is two whole steps; a minor third is a whole step plus a half step.

**Harmonic and melodic.** Two pitches at once, or one after the other, are still the same interval. Name them the same way.

Today stays in written C major for the first drill, then asks you to name intervals on the staff from other notes by letter-count plus half-step check, not by guessing.
