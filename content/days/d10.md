## Mathematics · Two-week problem set {#math}

<p class="meta">60 min · In the tradition of the Russian problem books: many short problems in graded difficulty, mixing every topic so far. Work without notes for 50 minutes.</p>

1. A sculptor used 1/4 of a block of clay for a bust and 2/3 of the rest for small figures. 150 g were left. How much clay was there at first? (Draw the model.)
2. Factor x⁴ + 4. (Hint: add and subtract 4x².)
3. Solve x² − 2x − 15 = 0 and check with Vieta.
4. In triangle ABC, ∠A = 80°. The bisectors of angles B and C meet at I. Find ∠BIC.
5. Solve |2x − 3| < 5.
6. Find the minimum of 2x + 8/x for x > 0, and where it occurs.
7. Can a triangle have sides 3, 5 and 9? Prove your answer.
8. Prove that n³ − n is divisible by 6 for every integer n.
9. Sketch y = |x − 1| − 2 and state where it crosses the x-axis.
10. *(Stretch.)* Prove that in any triangle, a median is shorter than half the sum of the two sides that meet at its vertex. (Hint: extend the median to twice its length.)

### Answer key

1. After the bust, 3/4 is left; figures take 2/3 × 3/4 = 1/2; left: 3/4 − 1/2 = 1/4 of the block = 150 g ⇒ **600 g**.
2. x⁴ + 4x² + 4 − 4x² = (x² + 2)² − (2x)² = **(x² + 2x + 2)(x² − 2x + 2)**.
3. (x − 5)(x + 3) = 0 ⇒ **x = 5 or −3**; sum 2 ✓, product −15 ✓.
4. ∠B + ∠C = 100°, so half of each sums to 50°; ∠BIC = 180° − 50° = **130°** (in general 90° + ∠A/2).
5. −5 < 2x − 3 < 5 ⇒ **−1 < x < 4**.
6. By AM–GM, 2x + 8/x ≥ 2√16 = **8**, with equality when 2x = 8/x, i.e. **x = 2**.
7. **No**: 3 + 5 = 8 < 9 violates the triangle inequality.
8. n³ − n = (n − 1)n(n + 1), the product of three consecutive integers: one of them is divisible by 3 and at least one is even. ∎
9. A V with vertex (1, −2); crosses the x-axis at **x = −1 and x = 3**.
10. Let AM be the median to BC. Extend AM past M to D with MD = AM. Triangles AMB and DMC are congruent (SAS: BM = MC, AM = MD, vertical angles), so CD = AB. In triangle ACD: AD < AC + CD = AC + AB, i.e. 2AM < AB + AC. ∎

**Scoring:** 1 point each for 1–9, 2 for 10. Record your score out of 11.

## Writing & Rhetoric · Trivium III, rhetoric: the three appeals {#writing}

<p class="meta">45 min · Method: Aristotle, <em>Rhetoric</em> (the three "proofs"), taught as the final stage of the Trivium</p>

**The appeals.** Aristotle names three means of persuasion that the speaker provides: **ethos**, the speaker's character and credibility; **pathos**, putting the audience in the right emotional state; **logos**, the argument itself, the proof or apparent proof. Good rhetoric uses all three honestly; manipulation uses pathos or ethos to replace logos.

**In the sources you have read.** Pericles uses pathos (the dead, the city's glory) and ethos (the leader speaking for the city). Socrates' gadfly passage uses logos and a vivid figure, and refuses the pathos usual in Athenian courts. (In the *Apology* he notes that he will not bring his weeping children before the jury.)

### Exercises

1. Write a persuasive paragraph (150–200 words) on: *Every serious art student should study Latin.* Plan it as an SPO first. Use all three appeals and label each in the margin.
2. Revise it with this checklist: topic sentence arguable? Each claim supported? One sentence with *although*? One appositive? No sentence that could be cut without loss?
3. Identify the main appeal in each: (a) *As a surgeon for twenty years, I can tell you that this treatment is safe.* (b) *Imagine the faces of the children who will have no school.* (c) *The data from all eight trials show the same effect.*

### Answer key

1–2. Use the Day 9 rubric adapted for a paragraph. A logos sentence might cite the Latin roots of art vocabulary (*pigment* from *pigmentum*, *sculpture* from *sculptura*, *medium*) and the Latin inscriptions and titles on Renaissance works; ethos might cite the training of past artists; pathos the pleasure of reading an inscription unaided. Every factual claim must be true.
3. (a) ethos (b) pathos (c) logos.

## Physics · Newton's third law and the two-week check {#physics}

<p class="meta">45 min · Peer Instruction protocol</p>

**Pre-reading.** When object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on *different* objects, so they never cancel each other. A rocket pushes exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward.

### ConcepTests

1. A heavy truck and a small car collide head-on. During the collision: (A) the truck exerts a larger force on the car than the car exerts on the truck (B) the forces are equal in size (C) the car exerts the larger force.
2. In the same collision, which experiences the larger acceleration? (A) the truck (B) the car (C) the same.
3. A horse pulls a cart. By the third law the cart pulls back on the horse equally. How can they move forward? (A) They can't (B) The horse pushes backward on the ground, and the ground pushes the horse forward with a force larger than the cart's pull (C) The horse's force is slightly larger.

### Two-week check (problems)

4. A ball is thrown up at 20 m/s. How high does it rise? How long until it returns?
5. A 1,500 kg car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 8.0 s. Find the net force.
6. A 70 kg skater pushes off a wall with an average force of 140 N. What is the skater's acceleration, and what force does the skater exert on the wall?

### Answer key

1. **B.** Third-law pair.
2. **B.** Equal forces, smaller mass ⇒ larger acceleration.
3. **B.** Whether the horse accelerates depends on the forces *on the horse*: the ground's forward push against the cart's backward pull. The horse–cart pair do not cancel because they act on different objects.
4. h = 400/19.6 ≈ **20.4 m**; t = 2 × 20/9.8 ≈ **4.1 s**.
5. a = 2.5 m/s², F = **3,750 N**.
6. a = 140/70 = **2.0 m/s²** away from the wall; the skater pushes the wall with **140 N**.

## History · Synthesis: law, citizenship and judgement {#history}

<p class="meta">45 min · Document-based essay using Days 3, 5, 6 and 8</p>

**Question.** *What did the ancient sources studied so far believe about who should be judged, by whom, and by what standard?*

Use all four documents: Hammurabi's laws (sections 196–199), Herodotus's opening, Pericles' Funeral Oration (2.37) and Plato's *Apology*. Write 500 words: a thesis, three body paragraphs organised by *idea* rather than by document, and a conclusion. For every document you use, include one sentence of sourcing (who, when, why, how reliable).

### Answer key

**Rubric (1–4 each):** Thesis answers the question and can be argued · Uses all four documents accurately · Sourcing sentence for each · Organised by idea, with comparison inside paragraphs · Acknowledges limits of the evidence (e.g., Thucydides' reconstruction of speeches; Plato's loyalty to Socrates; the stele as royal self-presentation). A strong thesis might be: *These sources move from law as a king's decree applied by rank, to law as the shared possession of citizens, to the claim that even the citizens' judgement must answer to reason.*

## Elective · Poetry: metre and the iambic pentameter {#seminar}

<p class="meta">30 min · Seminar rotation: Elective</p>

**Metre** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line. The unit is the **foot**: iamb (da-DUM), trochee (DUM-da), anapest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), spondee (DUM-DUM). **Iambic pentameter** is five iambs per line, the most common metre of English verse from Chaucer onward, and the line of Shakespeare's sonnets and blank verse.

**Model** (Shakespeare, Sonnet 18, lines 1–4, public domain):

> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date:

### Exercises

1. Scan all four lines: mark each syllable as stressed (/) or unstressed (×), and divide the feet.
2. Where does the rhythm vary from strict iambs? What effect does the variation have?
3. Write four lines of your own in iambic pentameter, rhyming ABAB, describing a drawing in progress.

### Answer key

1. Line 1: shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer's DAY (the first foot is often read with the stress on *I* or on *Shall*). Line 2: thou ART | more LOVE | ly AND | more TEM | per-ATE (the last foot uses a light final stress on *-ate*). Line 3: ROUGH WINDS (a spondee) | do SHAKE | the DAR | ling BUDS | of MAY. Line 4: and SUM | mer's LEASE | hath ALL | too SHORT | a DATE.
2. The opening question and the heavy "Rough winds" break the regular beat; the spondee makes the winds feel forceful. Natural speech stresses are allowed to pull against the metre.
3. Check that each line has ten syllables in a mostly da-DUM pattern, and the ABAB rhyme.
