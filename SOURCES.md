- Surface gravity and lunar periods, when used in cross-site work or in later checks: NASA Moon fact sheet (surface gravity 1.62 m/s² against Earth 9.80 m/s², ratio about 0.165, about 1/6; synodic month 29.53 days; sidereal month 27.3217 days); NASA Mars fact sheet (mean surface gravity 3.73 m/s² against Earth 9.82 m/s², ratio about 0.380).

## Weeks 5–6 (additional sources)

- Euclid, *Elements*, Book III, especially propositions 20 (central and inscribed angles on the same arc), 21 (angles in the same segment), 22 (opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral), 31 (angle in a semicircle), and 35 (intersecting chords). Public-domain translations (for example Heath) may be used; the course uses the theorems, not a copyrighted commentary.
- Work and the joule as an SI derived unit (1 J = 1 N·m): BIPM, *The International System of Units* (SI Brochure, 9th ed.). Kinetic energy \( \tfrac12 mv^2 \) and \( W_{\mathrm{net}} = \Delta \mathrm{KE} \) in the constant-force case already prepared by Newton’s second law and the kinematics relation \( v^2 = u^2 + 2as \). Gravitational potential energy near the Earth is taught as the first model \( PE = mgh \) with \( g = 9.8\,\mathrm{N/kg} \), not as a general \( 1/r \) formula.
- Photosynthesis: the widely taught net equation \( 6\,\mathrm{CO}_2 + 6\,\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{light energy} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6 + 6\,\mathrm{O}_2 \) is used as a school-level summary, with the limits stated (not a one-step mechanism; \( \mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6 \) is a representative carbohydrate). The modern account that released \( \mathrm{O}_2 \) comes from water, not from \( \mathrm{CO}_2 \), is stated and not treated as a classroom proof. No invented yields or rates.
- Paper chromatography of leaf pigments is taught as a method (solvent and paper, bands from different travel) without invented \( R_f \) values. Autumn color: unmasking of carotenoids as chlorophyll breaks down, with a caution that reds and purples in many species involve anthocyanins made in the season, not only unmasked pigments. No invented percentages.
- United States Declaration of Independence (1776), public domain, used as a model of form (parallelism, imitatio, Franklin hints, précis), not as a political assignment.
- Homer, *Odyssey*, Books 1–12: Butler (1898), Project Gutenberg [#2199](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2199), as a public-domain option for short, attributed quotation; verse translations remain recommended for reading.
- Early Rome and the Republic: Livy is treated as late (late first century BCE–early first century CE). Traditional dates such as 753 BCE and 509 BCE are labeled traditional. The Twelve Tables are treated as a mid-fifth-century tradition that survives in later fragments, not as a complete original text. Polybius (second century BCE) is used as an analyst of mid-Republican government and as a source for the Punic Wars. Standard chronology for those wars: 264–241, 218–201 (Cannae 216, Zama 202), 149–146 BCE, with the destruction of Carthage in 146. Caesar’s *Commentaries* are tagged as participant sources and as advocacy; Cicero’s letters as primary and from a position.
- Parthenon: conventional construction dates about 447–432 BCE, used as a conventional scholarly range. Doryphoros of Polykleitos: a fifth-century BCE bronze type known from later marble copies; no invented canon measurements. Nike of Samothrace: usually dated to the second century BCE (often around the early second century); fragmentary; Louvre display is not the original setting. The Vatican Laocoön: date and original-or-copy status are debated and are not settled in the lesson.
- William Blake, “The Tyger,” *Songs of Experience* (1794), public domain, for imagery and metaphor.
- Disjunctive syllogism is taught as a valid form (P or Q; not P; therefore Q), distinct from last week’s modus ponens and modus tollens. This course’s default “or” is inclusive unless a problem says exactly one; from P one may not infer not-Q under that default. Necessary and sufficient conditions remain Day 16’s lesson and are not reteached as new in week 11.
- Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins were omitted when the etymology section was missing, incomplete, or ambiguous (including *inscribed*, *photosynthesis*, *contrapositive*, *anthocyanin* beyond a brief name, and the physics word *work*).

## Weeks 7 (additional sources)

- Functions and function notation; graphs of y = mx + b; quadratic graphs, with the vertex from completing the square or from x = −b/(2a). π is not used as a new claim here; loci are the circle (points at a fixed distance from a center) and the perpendicular bisector (PA = PB), with Euclid III.31 named only as a contrast (the circle on diameter AB is a different locus).
- Francis Bacon, “Of Studies” (1625), public domain. The course uses one short attributed sentence as a model of form: “Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability.” No longer extract is quoted on the lesson pages.
- Sophocles, *Antigone*: a public-domain English translation (for example Jebb 1891, or another named public-domain version) may be used in class for short, attributed quotation. The week 7 pages do not lift a long passage and do not tell the end of the play.
- Power and the watt as an SI derived unit (1 W = 1 J/s): BIPM, *The International System of Units* (SI Brochure). The unit is named after James Watt. Torque, in a first look, is force times perpendicular distance, named in newton-meters and not called a joule.
- Seeing: a first anatomical map (cornea, pupil, lens, retina, optic nerve, blind spot) without invented focal lengths, travel times, or receptor counts. Etymologies used: cornea from Latin cornea (tela), from cornu, “horn”; retina from Latin rētīna, from rēte, “net.”
- Actium, 31 BCE; the settlement of 27 BCE as a conventional start of Augustus’s principate, not as a single “fall of the Republic” day. The Res Gestae Divi Augusti is treated as a first-person record (known from later copies, notably at Ankara) and as advocacy. Tacitus and Suetonius are dated to the early second century CE, about a century after Augustus’s death (14 CE).
- Augustus of Prima Porta: a marble statue in the Vatican Museums, generally treated as a copy of a lost bronze type; no single year is assigned to the copy in the lesson. Ara Pacis Augustae: conventional dates 13 BCE (voted) and 9 BCE (dedicated), labeled conventional.
- Latin grammar for Capitulum IX follows a public syllabus (third declension; declining ūnus, duo, trēs; invariable higher numerals as taught; quot; a large number with the genitive; cēterī). Practice sentences are original. Ørberg’s story and companion-book examples are not copied.
- Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins were omitted when the etymology section was missing, incomplete, ambiguous, or the first sense was the wrong word (*function*, *parabola*, *budget*, and *torque* were omitted for those reasons).

## Week 8 (additional sources)

- Euclid, *Elements*, Book III, Proposition 32 (the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment). The course’s proof uses III.20 (inscribed angle half the central angle on the same arc) and the Day 19 fact that a radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent, together with the triangle angle sum. Public-domain translations (for example Heath) may be used; the course uses the theorems, not a copyrighted commentary.
- Vieta’s formulas for a quadratic are taught from the expansion of (x − r)(x − s), and named after François Viète (Vieta). The inverse of a linear function f(x) = mx + b (m ≠ 0) is f⁻¹(x) = (x − b)/m, checked by composition; the graph as a reflection in y = x is a first look. This “inverse” is distinguished from the logic inverse of a conditional (already taught) and from the reciprocal 1/f(x).
- Newton’s law of gravitation, F = G Mm / r², as a first look, with the inverse-square pattern. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is used as a standard value of the constant, not as a classroom measurement (BIPM / standard CODATA-style school rounding). g = 9.8 N/kg near the Earth remains the first-model gravitational field. Lunar g is given as about one sixth of Earth’s, labeled as a commonly taught approximate first figure, not as a classroom measurement. Newton’s sideways-throw / mountain thought-picture is used qualitatively (a picture associated with his *System of the World*); no invented orbital speed; orbits are not all circles.
- Accommodation of the lens, and two slightly different views as a first cue for judging distance, without invented focal lengths, diopter numbers, or interocular measurements.
- Augustus’s death and the succession of Tiberius, 14 CE; a conventional Julio-Claudian list (Augustus, Tiberius 14–37, Gaius/Caligula 37–41, Claudius 41–54, Nero 54–68). Later writers (Tacitus, Suetonius, early second century CE) remain later; anecdotes are not treated as simple facts. 14 CE is a succession, not a second founding; 68 CE is the end of this line and a year of civil war in the conventional narrative, not a “fall of the Republic” day.
- The Pantheon in Rome: the fabric we have (rotunda, concrete dome, oculus) generally treated as a Hadrianic rebuild of the early second century CE, with a conventional range around 118–125 CE used here and labeled conventional. The Agrippa inscription is treated as belonging to an earlier Agrippan temple, conventionally associated with 27 BCE, and is not used to date the present dome. No invented measurements of the dome.
- Sophocles, *Antigone*, short attributed quotation from R. C. Jebb’s translation in *The Tragedies of Sophocles* (Cambridge, 1917), public domain, via Wikisource. Opening: “Ismene, sister, mine own dear sister, knowest thou what ill there is, of all bequeathed by Oedipus, that Zeus fulfils not for us twain while we live?” Close: “Wisdom is the supreme part of happiness; and reverence towards the gods must be inviolate. Great words of prideful men are ever punished with great blows, and, in old age, teach the chastened to be wise.”
- Percy Bysshe Shelley, “Ozymandias,” first published 1818, public domain, short attributed extracts only (“I met a traveller from an antique land…”; “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”).
- Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins used include *tangent* (Latin *tangēns*, “touching”), *inverse* (Latin *inversus* / *invertere*), *infinitive* (Late Latin *īnfīnītīvus*, from *īnfīnītus*), *octave* (Latin *octavus*, “eighth”), *polygon* (Greek *polus* + *gōnia*), *Pantheon* (Greek *Pantheion*, “temple of all gods”), and *weight* (Old English *wiht* / *ġewiht*, related to *weigh*). Origins were omitted when the etymology section was tree-only, incomplete, or the wrong sense (*gravity*, *gravitation*, *mass*, *accommodation*, *refraction*).

## Week 9 (additional sources)

- Composition of functions is taught as (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), with order mattering, and then as composition with an inverse: (f ∘ f⁻¹)(x) = x = (f⁻¹ ∘ f)(x), the identity. The inverse of a linear function remains as already taught, and is still distinguished from the logic inverse. The equation of a circle, (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r², is taught as the coordinate form of the already-taught locus. A line and a circle are solved as a system by substitution; a line may meet a circle twice, once (a tangent), or not at all.
- Etymology of *composition* (the English word): Middle English *composicioun*, from Old French *composicion*, from Latin *compositiō*, as on English Wiktionary (entry “composition”). Other new week-9 terms were left without an origin rather than a guessed root.
- Tacitus, *Histories* Book I, opening, in the Loeb Classical Library translation (1925), public domain as presented on LacusCurtius (Bill Thayer). The opening begins with Galba’s second consulship and names Galba, Otho, and Vitellius, and then Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian, including the career line that Tacitus’s political career owed its beginning to Vespasian. “Year of the Four Emperors” and “Flavian” are taught as later names, not as Tacitus’s titles. 69 CE is a civil-war year after 68 CE, not a fall day of Rome.
- John Dryden, trans., opening of *Aeneid* Book I, in *The Works of Virgil containing his Pastorals, Georgics and Æneis*, first published 1697 (Wikisource, *The Works of Virgil (Dryden)/Aeneid/Book I*; the work page notes the 1697 first publication). Set extract only: “Arms, and the Man I sing, who forc'd by Fate, And haughty Juno's unrelenting Hate; Expell'd and exil'd, left the Trojan Shoar.” The spelling *Shoar* is Dryden’s. Later books are reserved.
- National Eye Institute, “How the eyes work”: photoreceptors in the retina turn light into electrical signals, which travel by the optic nerve; the brain turns the signals into images. National Eye Institute, “Color blindness”: color vision deficiency as seeing colors differently than most people; most often, trouble telling certain colors apart; complete color vision deficiency rare; common types often genetic; men have a much higher risk than women. No invented rod-or-cone count. Afterimages are taught as a careful, optional observation without an invented mechanism, time, or wavelength.
- Circular motion and a centripetal (center-seeking) force are taught qualitatively: velocity along the tangent; a change of direction is a change of velocity; if the inward force stops, the motion continues along the tangent in this first look. No invented orbital speed, string length, or force number.

## Week 10 (additional sources)

- Iteration of a function is taught as f(f(x)), or (f ∘ f)(x), using composition already taught. The incircle is taught from the already-taught equal-tangents theorem (Day 19). A line and a parabola are solved as a system by substitution, as a line and a circle were in week 9; points are checked in both equations. The incenter is the meeting of angle bisectors (construction already taught), equally far from the sides, and A = r s, with s the semiperimeter.
- Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins used include *iteration* / *iterate* (Latin *iterātiō* / *iterō*, from *iterum*, “again”); *incircle* (in- + circle); *incenter* (in- + center); *patronage* (Middle English and Old French *patronage*); *commemorate* (Latin *commemorātus*, from *commemorō*); *spoils* (from *spoil*, Middle English *spoilen*, Old French *espoillier*, Latin *spoliāre*); *myopia* (Ancient Greek *muōpía*, from *múō* + *ṓps*); *hyperopia* (hyper- + -opia); *astigmatism* (a- + stem of Greek *stígma* + -ism; coined by William Whewell in 1846); *presbyopia* (New Latin *presbyōpia*, from Greek *présbus* and *ṓps*); *modus ponens* and *modus tollens* (Latin phrases as on Wiktionary); *sonnet* (Middle French *sonnet*, Italian *sonetto*, Old Occitan *sonet*, Latin *sonus*); *profit* (Middle English and Old French *profit*, Latin *prōfectus*, from *proficiō*); and *plane* (Latin *plānum*). Origins were omitted when a modern phrase, a tree-only entry, or a previously unconfirmed family would have required a guess (*refractive error* / *refraction*, already omitted in week 8; *gravity* and *g* likewise).
- NASA Earth Fact Sheet: mass 5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg; equatorial radius 6,378.137 km; polar radius 6,356.752 km; volumetric mean radius 6,371.000 km; surface gravity (mean) 9.820 m/s²; surface acceleration (equator) 9.780 m/s². https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html
- NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (comparative table): Earth mass 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 12,756 km, gravity 9.8 m/s²; Moon mass 0.073 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 3,475 km, gravity 1.6 m/s². https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/
- NASA Science, “Moon Facts”: “the gravity on the surface of the Moon is one-sixth of Earth’s.” https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/
- This course’s G remains 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², a labeled standard constant, not a classroom measurement. A computation of Earth’s g from that G and the Earth Fact Sheet mass and equatorial radius is about 9.79 m/s², limited by G’s three significant figures. The Moon computation is about 1.6 m/s², limited by the two significant figures in 0.073. No invented orbital speed.
- Flavius Josephus, *The War of the Jews* Book VI, chapter 4, William Whiston’s translation, public domain, via Wikisource (*The War of the Jews/Book VI*), including “And thus was the holy house burnt down, without Caesar’s approbation,” and the dating of the destruction to “the second year of the reign of Vespasian” (70 CE in the conventional count used here). Flavius Josephus, *The Life of Flavius Josephus*, Whiston, public domain, via Wikisource, for the later Roman apartment, citizenship, and annual pension under Vespasian, and for Titus’s continued respect. Patronage is taught as a reading caution, not as a trial.
- Samuel Ball Platner, as completed and revised by Thomas Ashby, *A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome* (London: Oxford University Press, 1929), entry “Arcus Titi,” as presented on LacusCurtius (Bill Thayer), public domain: erected in honour of Titus and in commemoration of the siege of Jerusalem, but not finished and dedicated until after his death (CIL VI.945); south inner relief of spoils from the temple at Jerusalem (table of shewbread, seven-branched candlestick, silver trumpets); north inner relief of Titus in a quadriga; taken down in 1822 and rebuilt by Valadier.

- National Eye Institute, “Refractive Errors”: a vision problem in which the shape of the eye keeps light from focusing correctly on the retina; four common types (myopia, far-away objects blurry; hyperopia, nearby objects blurry; astigmatism, far-away and nearby objects blurry or distorted; presbyopia, hard for middle-aged and older adults to see things up close; common in adults 45 and older); causes include eyeball length, corneal shape, and aging of the lens; treatment named here as eyeglasses or contact lenses (surgery is listed by the NEI and is not taught). - John Keats, “On First Looking into Chapman’s Homer,” published in *Poems* (1817), public domain. The Cortez comparison is taught as a figure in the poem, not as a history lesson.

## Week 11 (additional sources)

- Quadratic inequalities are read from the already-taught parabola graph: for an upward-opening product such as (x − 2)(x − 6) < 0, the solution is between the roots. When the leading coefficient is negative, the regions swap. A square (x − a)² is never negative; (x − a)² < 0 has no real solution, and complex numbers are not begun. Euclid III.36 (the tangent-secant theorem): if from an exterior point P a tangent touches at T and a secant cuts at A (nearer) and B (farther), then PT² = PA × PB. Intersecting chords (III.35) remain a separate picture.
- Etymologies of new key terms were checked on English Wiktionary. Origins used include *inequality* (Middle English *inequalite*, from Old French *inequalité*, Medieval Latin *inaequālitās*, from Latin *inaequālis*, *in-* + *aequālis*); *secant* (Latin *secāns*, from *secō*, “to cut”); and *eruption* (Middle French *éruption*, from Latin *eruptio*). *Cadence* was already sourced in an earlier week (Middle French *cadence*, from Old Italian *cadenza*, related to Latin *cadō*, “to fall”); a reconstructed form was not presented as a recorded noun. Origins were omitted when a modern phrase or an unconfirmed family would have required a guess (*counterexample* was not given a new card; *unit price*, *half cadence* as a phrase, *necessary condition*, and *sufficient condition* were left without an invented root).
- NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (comparative table): Mars mass 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg, diameter 6,792 km, gravity 3.7 m/s². g = G M / R² with this course’s G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² and R = 3,396 km (half of 6,792 km) gives about 3.71 m/s², reported as about 3.7 m/s² to match the sheet and the significant figures. 3.7 / 9.8 ≈ 0.38, about 19/50, a little less than 2/5. Weight uses W = m g with labeled invented masses. No invented orbital speed. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/
- National Eye Institute, “Refractive Errors,” already used in week 10, for eyeglasses or contact lenses as a named treatment (surgery is listed by the NEI and is not taught). No invented diopter numbers, and no classroom diagnosis. https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/refractive-errors
- Pliny the Younger, letter to Tacitus, in J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation, as posted at Attalus (which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified): https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html . Set wording includes “On the 24th of August, about the seventh hour,” the uncle at Misenum, and the pine-tree cloud extract beginning “Those who were looking at the cloud from some distance…” 79 CE is taught as the conventional event-year in Titus’s reign, not as a year the set sentence states. The set cloud extract does not name Pompeii and does not give a death toll. Later of the letter (the voyage, Rectina, Stabiae, the uncle’s death) remains reserved.
- John Dryden, trans., *Aeneid* Book II, first published 1697 (Wikisource, *The Works of Virgil (Dryden)/Aeneid/Book II*). Set extract only, the four lines beginning “Thus they pretend, but in the hollow Side.” Tenedos, the gates, Sinon, Priam, and later books remain reserved.
- Samuel Ball Platner, as completed and revised by Thomas Ashby, *A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome* (London: Oxford University Press, 1929), entry “Amphitheatrum Flavium,” as presented on LacusCurtius (Bill Thayer), public domain: built by Vespasian on the site of the stagnum of Nero’s *domus Aurea*; carried to the top of the second arcade of the outer wall and dedicated before his death in 79; Titus added the third and fourth stories and celebrated the dedication of the enlarged building in 80 with games that lasted one hundred days (Platner cites Suetonius, *Titus* 7, among other authorities; this course cites Platner and does not pretend to have read Suetonius independently).
- William Wordsworth, “I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud,” first published 1807, public domain. The text used is the familiar four-stanza text as printed by the Poetry Foundation (with “golden daffodils” and the “inward eye”). This course does not date that exact wording to a later revision year without a source opened for that year. “Ten thousand” is taught as a figure, not a census. https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45521/i-wandered-lonely-as-a-cloud

## Week 12 (additional sources)

- Week 12 is a Term 1 consolidation. It re-teaches methods already sourced in weeks 1–11 and does not add a new theorem, a new chapter, a new Dryden extract, or a new Pliny extract. Capitula I–X still bound the Latin; Capitulum XI is not begun. Term examinations follow the methods page: under 80% means re-teaching before Term 2. They are not scores, badges, or prizes.
- Already-used NASA Planetary Fact Sheet figures are gathered, not re-sourced as new measurements: Earth working g 9.8 m/s² beside about 9.79 from the Earth Fact Sheet computation; Moon about 1.6 m/s², about one-sixth; Mars about 3.7 m/s² from g = G M / R² with M = 0.642 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 3,396 km, matching the sheet. G remains 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². Weight uses W = m g with labeled invented masses. No invented orbital speed. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/
- National Eye Institute, “Refractive Errors,” already used in weeks 10–11, is gathered for the Term 1 science examination: myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, presbyopia, in the NEI wording; eyeglasses or contact lenses as a named tool; no invented diopters; no classroom diagnosis. https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/refractive-errors
- Pliny the Younger, letter to Tacitus, in J. B. Firth’s 1900 translation, as posted at Attalus (which notes that a few words and phrases have been modified): https://www.attalus.org/old/pliny6.html . The set date wording and the set pine-tree cloud extract are reused, not grown. 79 CE remains the conventional event-year. Later of the letter remains reserved.
